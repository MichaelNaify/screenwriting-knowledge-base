A.1 Purpose & Scope of Stage 2 Integration (#purpose #stage2)

Stage 2 transforms the Stage 1 TRUEFORM decomposition into a unified theory of dramatic dialogue that is at once scholarly and operational. The goal is to weld McKee’s core proposition—dialogue as verbal action—to a five-dimensional model that writers can apply line-by-line, beat-by-beat, and scene-by-scene. We ground claims in the primary text where McKee (i) expands dialogue to any words a character says across three tracks—talk to others, to self, to audience—while insisting those words are actions rather than ornament, and (ii) locates those actions in two delivery modes: dramatized (in-scene) and narratized (out-of-scene) verbal performance.      

Scope. Part A establishes: (1) McKee’s thesis and corollaries; (2) a five-dimensional architecture (Structural, Psychological, Linguistic/Pragmatic, Performative, Ethical); (3) a unified model tying dialogue functions (exposition, characterization, action) to scene mechanics; (4) Stage-2 methodology and verification standards; and (5) quality targets and compliance metrics inherited from Stage 1.

A.2 The Central Thesis: Dialogue as Verbal Action (#thesis #verbal-action)

McKee’s foundation principle is explicit: to say something is to do something; every line is a performative act in pursuit of desire, moving the scene from beat to beat and shifting the character toward or away from need-satisfaction.   He further specifies delivery modes—dialogue “carries out its actions” as either dramatized (acted in conflict, including inner duologues) or narratized (address to self, reader, or audience that still enacts intention).    

Corollary A — Three Tracks. McKee surveys: (1) talk to others, (2) talk within oneself, (3) talk to audience/reader. These tracks define where intention aims and how power circulates.  

Corollary B — Three Functions. Dialogue performs exposition, characterization, action. This trims bloat: if a line does not deliver one of these—and preferably two or all three—it should likely be cut or reengineered.  

Corollary C — Exposition as Ammunition. McKee operationalizes “show, don’t tell” at the verbal layer: embed facts as weapons wielded at turning points under pressure (e.g., “I am your father” as a tactic that reorients power, empathy, and plot).  

[SYNTHESIS:] The thesis Dialogue = Action maps cleanly to speech-act theory (Austin/Searle) where utterances are performances (promising, accusing, threatening). In McKee’s frame, each line is an illocution with a tactical aim, and the scene records whether its perlocutionary effect succeeds against resistance. (Academic grounding noted; attribution to McKee limited to concepts explicitly present above.)

A.3 The Five-Dimensional Architecture (#five-dimensions #model)

Stage 2 articulates a model that aligns McKee’s practice with a cross-disciplinary scaffold:
	1.	Structural (#structural-dialogue) — Dialogue as micro-causality. Lines trigger beat-level value shifts that accumulate into scene progression; dramatized/narratized choices modulate when and how change lands.  
	2.	Psychological (#psychological-dialogue) — Dialogue as goal-oriented behavior. Intention, obstacle, tactic drive each utterance; inner talk is still conflict between “selves.”  
	3.	Linguistic/Pragmatic (#linguistic-dialogue) — Dialogue as performative language and implicature management; the line’s power lies in what’s done and what’s implied, not what’s explained. McKee’s insistence on subtext as source aligns with pragmatic inference; his “action/reaction” rhythm foregrounds illocution/perlocution.  
	4.	Performative/Theatrical (#performative-dialogue) — Dialogue as embodied cue for actors and as a two-audience communication (character↔character; text↔audience). Dramatized vs. narratized talk is partly a staging choice; McKee details mode shifts in theatre and screen (soliloquy, VO, direct address).    
	5.	Ethical/Moral (#ethical-dialogue) — Dialogue as truth/deceit vector. Exposition-as-ammunition and revelation sequences stage confessional pressure; secrets surface at dilemmas, turning value charges and audience judgment.

PART B — STRUCTURAL-FUNCTIONAL THEORY OF VERBAL ACTION

B.1 Foundations: Dialogue as Structural Element (#dialogue #verbal-action #structural-dialogue)

McKee defines dialogue as “any words said by any character to anyone” and insists that all talk is an outward execution of an inner action rooted in desire and purpose【turn3file3†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L21-L26】. He subdivides character talk into three tracks—to others, to self, and to audience/reader—each still governed by intentional action【turn3file3†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L24-L34】. Within this framing, to say something is to do something (drawing explicitly on Austin’s concept of performatives), and each through-speech action moves the scene from one beat to the next toward or away from a character’s desire【turn3file4†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L9-L12】【turn3file4†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L11-L13】.

Critically, McKee distinguishes dramatized dialogue (spoken within scenes in conflict, each line an action that causes reaction) from narratized dialogue (spoken outside the scene to audience/reader/self)【turn3file4†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L17-L22】【turn3file4†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L26-L29】. B.2 Value Physics: Dialogue as Micro-Causality (#value-shift #beat-architecture #intention-obstacle)

McKee’s beat is a unit that contains an action and a reaction, ideally topping the previous beat and shaping the scene’s progression to its turning point【turn3file7†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L5-L13】. Because every verbal action causes a reaction, dialogue is the kinetic energy inside the scene’s “value field.” A character’s behavior flows through five steps—desire, sense of antagonism, choice, action, expression—which give writers a precise lens for diagnosing whether words on the page are genuine verbal tactics or mere talk【turn3file7†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L16-L25】.

Structural takeaway: if a line does not contribute to action → reaction → topping → turn, it is likely exposition, rhetoric, or mood that belongs in narration, staging, or silence—not in dramatized dialogue.

B.3 Scene Dynamics: Beat-by-Beat Architecture (#scene-construction #dialogue-diagnostics #beat-architecture)

McKee insists that each exchange of dialogue creates an action/reaction that progresses the scene, and the wellspring of these actions is subtext—the invisible motive force beneath talk【turn3file11†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L18-L18】【turn3file11†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L16-L18】. Practically, he recommends labeling beats with gerunds (e.g., insulting/ridiculing) to keep the focus on doings, not sayings【turn3file7†Dialogue - Robert McKee.md†L12-L13】.

B.3.1 Diagnostic cues
	•	If a scene’s dialogue does not escalate beat-over-beat, progression stalls (no turn).
	•	If text states what subtext should imply, subtext collapses and the scene dies (McKee’s consistent stance across examples).
	•	If words recap backstory without conflictual use, exposition becomes visible and un-dramatic (see §B.5).

PART C — PSYCHOLOGICAL-BEHAVIORAL THEORY OF SPEECH

(Dimension 2 of the Five-Dimensional Architecture)

C.1 Goal-Oriented Communication Theory (#psychological-dialogue #intention-obstacle)

McKee anchors all character language in goal pursuit: “Every line a character speaks is an action designed to get what they want.”  He defines intention as the inner aim of the line, obstacle as the opposing force that blocks satisfaction, and tactic as the chosen verbal behavior that tries to overcome that block【turn3file7†Dialogue – Robert McKee.md†L16-L25】.

This maps directly to his five-step behavioral flow:
	1.	Desire – the want.
	2.	Sense of Antagonism – awareness of resistance.
	3.	Choice – selection of strategy.
	4.	Action – execution of that strategy through speech or gesture.
	5.	Expression – the audible or visible result the audience can read.

Thus, dialogue functions as audible willpower – thought pressurized into sound.

[SYNTHESIS] Psychologically, this aligns with Freud’s topography: the conscious intention arises from the ego’s planning, the obstacle triggers unconscious defenses, and the chosen tactic – sarcasm, deflection, seduction – reveals displacement of deeper drives.  In McKee’s dramaturgy this becomes subtext: repression speaking sideways.

⸻

C.2 Hierarchy of Objectives (#scene-objective #super-objective #tactic)

McKee distinguishes between a line’s local objective and the character’s super-objective (what they pursue throughout the story). Each scene’s dialogue serves both:
	•	the immediate beat goal (“get the truth,” “deflect the question”) and
	•	the long-arc need (“win love,” “secure freedom”).

[SYNTHESIS] Truby’s desire vs. need model fits here: McKee’s scene goal = Truby’s “desire”; the super-objective = Truby’s “need.” When a character changes, the shift appears first in speech habits – lexical field, tempo, or tone.

In McKee’s beat design, the tactic must change when it fails; otherwise, the beat stagnates【turn3file7†Dialogue – Robert McKee.md†L1-L11】. A character who persuades, fails, then intimidates demonstrates escalation and reveals depth.

[SYNTHESIS] From a behavioral view, this mirrors operant conditioning – characters try new strategies to reduce frustration or restore equilibrium. The writer’s task is to design each beat as a test that modifies behavior.

McKee equates speech style with character truth: “If characters sound the same, they are the same.”【turn3file10†Dialogue – Robert McKee.md†L55-L59】 Idiolect thus becomes a psychological fingerprint. Lexical choices signal education and worldview; rhythm betrays emotion; pauses betray fear.

[SYNTHESIS] Goffman’s dramaturgical theory clarifies this as front-stage presentation: each speaker curates a persona through verbal masks. Dialogue’s drama emerges when the speaker’s words serve the wrong goal – protecting ego instead of fulfilling need. McKee demonstrates this in his analysis of characters who speak to avoid pain rather than to pursue truth (see his contrast between sincere and phony talk in the opening chapters of Part I of Dialogue). The writer should design misaligned speech, where intention = surface want, but subtext = repressed need.

[SYNTHESIS] Freud’s “return of the repressed” becomes a structural tool: subtext is the unconscious leaking through syntax and rhythm. When rewriting, note where dialogue rings too conscious – that often marks a missed chance to let the unconscious speak.

C.7 Diagnostic Protocol – Psychological Authenticity Tests (#dialogue-diagnostics #rewrite-protocols)

Test 2 – Obstacle Visibility

Test 3 – Tactic Shift

Dialogue is the visible end of a chain that begins in desire and passes through resistance and choice. Every spoken line is an attempt to re-balance psychic pressure and social power. When intention, obstacle, and tactic interlock, the audience reads behavior as truth. When they disconnect, the audience reads acting. That difference – between truth and performance – is the drama inside dialogue.
