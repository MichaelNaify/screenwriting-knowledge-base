## ACCEPTANCE

**Universal Definition**: The foundational improvisational principle of receiving and building upon offers from scene partners rather than blocking or denying them.

**Source Coverage**: Johnstone (Impro), Close (Truth in Comedy), Besser (Manual), Leonard/Yorton (Yes And)

**Text-by-Text Analysis**:

**Johnstone - Impro**:
- Acceptance as removing blocks to creativity
- "Yes And" philosophy: accept first, then add
- Blocking kills scenes; acceptance creates possibilities
- Status acceptance: agreeing to status relationships
- Emotional acceptance: receiving emotional offers

**Close - Truth in Comedy**:
- Acceptance as foundation of long-form improv
- Building game through acceptance
- Pattern recognition requires accepting patterns
- Agreement creates collaborative discovery
- "Follow the fear" requires accepting uncomfortable offers

**Besser - Comedy Improv Manual**:
- Acceptance technique in UCB methodology
- Game finding requires accepting initial patterns
- Heightening depends on accepting premise
- Specific acceptance exercises and drills
- Acceptance mistakes and corrections

**Leonard/Yorton - Yes, And**:
- "Yes And" in business and collaboration contexts
- Acceptance as leadership and team principle
- Practical applications beyond performance
- Building ideas through acceptance
- Failure acceptance and learning

**Related Concepts**: #yes_and #building #offer #blocking #listening #spontaneity #collaboration

**Contradictions/Tensions**:
- Acceptance philosophy vs. dramatic choice (McKee: characters must make difficult choices, not accept everything)
- Resolution: Different contexts (improvisation discovery vs. scripted drama logic)

**Practical Applications**:
1. Use in improvisation exercises to generate material
2. Apply in writers room for collaborative idea building
3. Practice in character discovery work
4. Implement in creative partnerships
5. Use for removing creative blocks

**Examples**:
- Improv scenes that build successfully through acceptance
- Writers room dynamics where "Yes And" produces creative breakthroughs
- Character development through accepting unexpected discoveries

**Tags**: #comedy_improv #improvisation #collaboration #yes_and #johnstone #close #besser #leonard_yorton

## ACT STRUCTURE

**Universal Definition**: The division of a screenplay into major units (typically three acts) that organize the dramatic progression and provide structural clarity.

**Source Coverage**: Field (Screenplay), McKee (Story), Truby (Anatomy), Seger (Making Great), Snyder (Save the Cat), Owen (Adaptation), Blake (Comedy Movie), Finn (Sitcom)

**Text-by-Text Analysis**:

**Field - Screenplay**:
- Three-act structure as "paradigm"
- Act One (Setup): Pages 1-30
- Act Two (Confrontation): Pages 30-90
- Act Three (Resolution): Pages 90-120
- Plot points define act breaks
- Midpoint in center of Act Two
- Structure is universal framework

**McKee - Story**:
- Acts as major movements in story
- Act defined by significant shift in story values
- Flexible act structure (not rigid page counts)
- Classical design typically three acts
- Some stories require more or fewer acts
- Acts emerge from story's needs, not formula

**Truby - Anatomy of a Story**:
- Rejects three-act structure as limiting
- Proposes seven key story steps instead
- Acts are artificial divisions
- Story should flow organically
- Focus on character desire line, not acts
- Structure emerges from character journey

**Snyder - Save the Cat**:
- Three-act structure with specific beats
- Act One: Pages 1-25
- Act Two: Pages 25-85
- Act Three: Pages 85-110
- 15 specific beats define structure
- Page number targets for each beat
- Formulaic but proven effective

**Seger - Making a Good Script Great**:
- Three-act structure standard
- Act problems: setup weak, second act sags, resolution unsatisfying
- Flexible application of structure
- Scene sequencing more important than rigid acts
- Structure serves story

**Owen - Art of Screen Adaptation**:
- Act structure varies by source material
- Some sources suggest different structures
- Three acts common but not universal
- Stage plays often five acts
- Adapt structure to source needs

**Blake - Writing the Comedy Movie**:
- Three-act comedy structure
- Comedy-specific act considerations
- Setup establishes comic world
- Second act escalates comedy
- Third act delivers comic payoff

**Finn - Sitcom Writers Talk Shop**:
- Television act structure different from film
- Half-hour: Often two acts with cold open and tag
- Act breaks placed for commercial timing
- Four-act structure for one-hour
- Network vs. cable vs. streaming differences

**Related Concepts**: #structure #plot_point #paradigm #three_act_structure #setup #confrontation #resolution #act_break

**Contradictions/Tensions**:
- Field's rigid page counts vs. McKee's flexibility vs. Truby's rejection
- Resolution: Field good for learning/teaching; McKee for mastery; Truby for character-driven work
- Three acts vs. alternative structures (five-act, seven-act, organic)
- Resolution: Choose based on story needs and experience level

**Practical Applications**:
1. Begin with Field's three-act paradigm for foundation
2. Apply Snyder's beats for specific moments
3. Flex structure using McKee principles for story needs
4. Consider Truby's alternatives for character-driven narratives
5. Adapt for television using Finn's guidance

**Examples**:
- Most commercial films follow three-act structure
- Some art films use alternative structures
- Television episodes have format-specific act divisions

**Tags**: #structure #structure_foundation #three_act #paradigm #field #mckee #truby #snyder #seger #finn

## ADAPTATION

**Universal Definition**: The process of transforming a work from one medium to another (typically novel, play, or true story to film/television), requiring creative interpretation, medium translation, and selective compression or expansion.

**Source Coverage**: Hutcheon (Theory of Adaptation), Owen (Art of Screen Adaptation), Seger (Making Great)

**Text-by-Text Analysis**:

**Hutcheon - A Theory of Adaptation**:
- Adaptation as interpretation, not copying
- Fidelity discourse problematic
- Three modes of engagement: telling, showing, participating
- Adaptation as palimpsest (layered text)
- Cultural translation essential
- Time, place, and audience affect adaptation
- Adapters are artists, not servants
- Transmedia storytelling considerations

**Owen - The Art of Screen Adaptation**:
- Comprehensive practical methodology
- Source material analysis essential
- Research process detailed
- Different source types require different approaches
- Novel to screen most common
- Play to screen: "opening out"
- True story: fact vs. drama balance
- Contemporary relevance important
- Character and structure core challenges

**Seger - Making a Good Script Great**:
- Adaptation as problem-solving
- Identify essential elements of source
- Compress without losing meaning
- Create cinematic equivalents
- Preserve theme and character essence
- Make necessary changes for medium
- Handle exposition cinematically

**Related Concepts**: #adaptation_fidelity #source_material #medium_translation #compression #expansion #cultural_translation #interpretation

**Contradictions/Tensions**:
- Hutcheon's "creative freedom" vs. Owen's "source respect"
- Resolution: Balance fidelity to essence with medium needs
- Literal adaptation vs. free interpretation
- Resolution: Context-dependent (fan expectations, source prestige, creative goals)

**Practical Applications**:
1. Analyze source material thoroughly (Owen)
2. Identify essential elements to preserve
3. Embrace creative interpretation (Hutcheon)
4. Compress using cinematic storytelling (Seger)
5. Research period and context (Owen)
6. Consider cultural translation needs (Hutcheon)
7. Solve medium-specific problems (Seger)

**Examples**:
- Faithful adaptations: To Kill a Mockingbird
- Interpretive adaptations: Blade Runner (from Do Androids Dream?)
- Play to film: Doubt
- True story: The Social Network

**Tags**: #adaptation #hutcheon #owen #seger #source_material #fidelity #interpretation #medium_translation

## ANTAGONIST

**Universal Definition**: The character or force that opposes the protagonist's desire and goal, creating conflict and driving dramatic action forward.

**Source Coverage**: McKee (Story), Field (Screenplay), Truby (Anatomy), Seger (Making Great)

**Text-by-Text Analysis**:

**McKee - Story**:
- Antagonist necessary for conflict
- Not necessarily "villain" - just opposition
- Can be person, society, nature, machine, self
- Best antagonists are as complex as protagonists
- Antagonist forces protagonist's choices
- Drives protagonist to reveal true character

**Field - Screenplay**:
- Antagonist opposes protagonist's dramatic need
- Creates obstacles protagonist must overcome
- External antagonist most common
- Internal antagonist possible (self-conflict)
- Clear opposition strengthens structure

**Truby - Anatomy of a Story**:
- "Opponent" preferred term
- Opponent fundamental to premise
- Best opponents share same goal as protagonist
- Character web includes opponent relationships
- Moral weakness in protagonist mirrors opponent
- Opponent forces protagonist's self-revelation

**Seger - Making a Good Script Great**:
- Antagonist must be worthy opponent
- Weak antagonist = weak story
- Dimensionalize antagonist (not flat villain)
- Antagonist has own motivation and goal
- Opposition clarifies protagonist's journey

**Related Concepts**: #protagonist #conflict #opposition #opponent #character_web #dramatic_need #obstacle

**Contradictions/Tensions**:
- McKee/Truby emphasis on complex antagonist vs. simpler genre antagonists
- Resolution: Complexity level depends on story type and genre

**Practical Applications**:
1. Define antagonist's goal (often same as protagonist)
2. Develop antagonist as fully as protagonist
3. Create worthy opposition (matched strength)
4. Use antagonist to test protagonist
5. Avoid flat "evil" villains without motivation

**Examples**:
- Complex antagonists: Anton Chigurh (No Country for Old Men), Nurse Ratched (One Flew Over Cuckoo's Nest)
- Simple antagonists: Genre films (action villains)
- Internal antagonists: Alcoholism (Leaving Las Vegas)

**Tags**: #character #character_foundation #antagonist #opponent #conflict #opposition #mckee #field #truby #seger

## BACKSTORY

**Universal Definition**: The history and past experiences of characters that inform their present behavior, motivations, and decisions, typically revealed selectively rather than through exposition dumps.

**Source Coverage**: Truby (Anatomy), Seger (Making Great), Field (Screenplay)

**Text-by-Text Analysis**:

**Truby - Anatomy of a Story**:
- Backstory as character's ghost (wound from past)
- Reveals in context of present action
- Backstory connects to character's weakness
- Don't frontload backstory
- Reveal when dramatically necessary
- Backstory deepens character psychology

**Seger - Making a Good Script Great**:
- Backstory provides character depth
- Reveal through action, not exposition
- Spread backstory throughout script
- Use "character spine" to organize backstory
- Avoid backstory dumps in opening
- Backstory should illuminate present conflict

**Field - Screenplay**:
- Backstory creates character biography
- Essential for writer to know fully
- Reveal selectively to audience
- Backstory informs character choices
- Can use flashback if necessary
- Don't overuse backstory exposition

**Related Concepts**: #character #exposition #character_biography #ghost #wound #character_history

**Practical Applications**:
1. Write complete backstory for yourself (even if never revealed)
2. Reveal backstory only when necessary for present action
3. Show backstory through behavior, not explanation
4. Connect backstory to character's present weakness
5. Spread backstory revelation throughout script

**Examples**:
- Casablanca: Rick's backstory with Ilsa revealed gradually
- Breaking Bad: Walt's backstory illuminates present choices
- Good Will Hunting: Will's abuse backstory revealed strategically

**Tags**: #character #character_development #backstory #ghost #exposition #truby #seger #field
