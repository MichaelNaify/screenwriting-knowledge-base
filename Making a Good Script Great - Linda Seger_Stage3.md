2.1 Module 1 — Premise as System Seed (#narrative-seed)

Seger defines the premise as “the spine of purpose.” In PK terms, it is a compressed algorithm containing story’s DNA: character, conflict, and outcome in potential form.

Example: “Pride comes before a fall when a brilliant surgeon refuses help.”
This single sentence encodes moral argument (pride), transformation trajectory (fall → humility), and genre expectation (melodrama / tragedy).

Seger’s exercise — rewrite premise five times to expose latent themes — becomes in PK language a premise-mutation loop, a generator for ideational breadth.

⸻

2.2 Module 2 — Structure as Causality Map (#story-architecture)

Seger distills structure to “the ordering of events that creates emotional logic.” She inverts the common notion that structure follows story; instead, story is the perception of structure in motion.

PK Implementation: Structure = temporal data model connecting turning-point nodes via cause-effect vectors. Each node is validated for “necessity and inevitability” — Seger’s litmus for good design.

Three tests of structural integrity:
	1.	Momentum: Does each beat increase pressure?
	2.	Causality: Does each choice force the next event?
	3.	Climax Coherence: Is final action the only possible resolution?

⸻

2.3 Module 3 — Character as Transformational Vector (#character-system)

Seger’s two variables—want and need—define vector direction and magnitude. Want drives plot; need drives theme. Their divergence creates dramatic voltage.

Character = Σ (choices under pressure).
Transformation = ∫ (conflict × time ) dt.

When integrated with Truby’s moral argument and McKee’s value shift, this becomes a computable trajectory for character development tracking across drafts.

Seger’s rewriting focus—“Clarify the arc by clarifying the need”—is encoded as PK function arc_calibrate(character_vector).

⸻

2.4 Module 4 — Conflict as Entropy Engine (#conflict-architecture)

Conflict is the heat that drives the system. Seger’s insight: conflict must escalate through escalating value stakes, not merely obstacle size.

Three diagnostic rules:
	1.	Every scene contains a choice with consequence.
	2.	Opposition must be specific and competent.
	3.	Conflict should mirror theme at micro level.

PK renders this as an entropy curve monitoring tension variance. Flat line = rewrite required.

⸻

2.5 Module 5 — Theme as Value Algorithm (#moral-calculus)

Theme is not message but math: Δ (value) / time. Each beat calculates change in human understanding. Seger’s method—identify what the story is about after each draft—creates iterative convergence on truth.

In PK analytics, theme is verified when value trajectory and character trajectory align within ± 5 % variance at climax. This metric ensures ethical and emotional coherence.

⸻

2.6 Module 6 — Dialogue as Signal Layer (#surface-communication)

§3 STRUCTURAL DYNAMICS AND SCENE PHYSICS

Seger’s contribution to cinematic structure lies not in redefining acts but in energizing them.
Structure, for her, is less a static scaffold than a fluid pressure system that breathes with emotional rhythm.  Each act is an expansion-contraction cycle: tension builds, releases, and rebuilds at higher amplitude.
Stage 3 codifies this dynamic in analytic and procedural form so it can be measured, predicted, and re-engineered.

⸻

3.1 From Blueprint to Dynamic Model (#energy-architecture)

In Seger’s framing, structure equals energy management.
The writer must calibrate escalation so that narrative voltage rises in precise proportion to the audience’s emotional endurance.

Act	Field’s Function	Seger’s Energetic Translation	PK Parameter
I	Exposition	Charge accumulation	E₁ = setup potential
II	Confrontation	Energy transfer / dissipation	ΔE = conflict output
III	Resolution	Re-integration / release	E₃ = new equilibrium

The writer’s task becomes voltage regulation—ensuring each beat contributes to a rising differential between expectation and fulfillment.
Over-compression yields melodrama; under-pressure yields boredom.

⸻

3.2 Beat Economy (#scene-function)

Seger defines a beat as the smallest unit of dramatic change.  Field describes plot points; McKee tracks value shifts; Seger diagnoses beat economy:

“A scene earns its place when something changes that cannot change back.”

PK quantification:

beat_efficiency = (value_shift / scene_length)
target_range = 0.85–1.00

Below 0.85 → rewrite required.

By logging beat density across pages, PK visualizes rhythm as a waveform—revealing structural drag zones before human readers feel them.

⸻

3.3 Turning Points as Decision Nodes (#causal-mechanics)

Every turning point, for Seger, must arise from choice under pressure, not coincidence.
She differentiates three causal classes:

Type	Description	Diagnostic Question
Structural	Act-break reorientation	“Does this decision redefine goal or stakes?”
Emotional	Mid-scene reversal	“Does feeling invert or deepen?”
Moral	Climax transformation	“Does choice reveal new self-knowledge?”

PK integration treats these as decision-tree nodes:
decision_strength = motivation × consequence × irreversibility.
Scripts with low decision_strength trigger system flag “fake turning point.”

⸻

3.4 Scene Physics (#micro-structure)

Seger likens scenes to mini-stories—each with beginning, middle, and end, each producing an energy pulse.
Stage 3 formalizes this as:

Scene = (setup + tension + reversal + outcome)

	•	Setup → establish orientation and desire.
	•	Tension → apply friction via obstacle.
	•	Reversal → unexpected variable alters course.
	•	Outcome → shift in power or emotion.

Each variable is scored 0–1 in PK analytics; aggregate scene score < 2.8 triggers diagnostic “flat scene.”
The rewrite mandate: add reversal or increase tension amplitude.

⸻

3.5 Rhythm and Cadence (#temporal-design)

Seger’s rhythm principle: variety equals vitality.
If scenes are identical in length, tone, or pace, narrative heart rate collapses.

PK applies scene-duration variance σ < 1.5 pages as optimal; greater uniformity signals mechanical pacing.
Seger’s heuristic “fast scenes feel faster after a slow one” becomes algorithmic modulation within PK’s temporal sequencing engine.

⸻

3.6 Subplot Integration (#harmonic-structure)

Subplots act as harmonic frequencies supporting the main melody.
Seger cautions: “Every subplot must resolve a facet of the same theme.”
PK harmonics analysis compares value direction vectors between main and secondary arcs:
alignment_index = cos(θ)
where θ = angle between theme vectors.
Values ≥ 0.9 indicate thematic resonance; < 0.6 denotes narrative noise.

⸻

3.7 Transitions and Momentum (#flow-dynamics)

Transitions convert momentum between scenes.
Seger recommends cause-effect overlaps—end of one scene sparking the next without editorial gaps.
PK’s momentum continuity index measures temporal gap size and causal clarity; stable flows cluster near 1.0.
Discontinuity, intentional or not, must produce designed disorientation; else flagged as “momentum leak.”

⸻

3.8 The Midpoint and Reversal (#symmetry-point)

Seger champions the midpoint not as obligatory twist but as mirror moment—the protagonist’s misbelief exposed.
PK expresses midpoint efficiency as ratio of pre- and post-midpoint goal variance:
variance ≤ 5 % indicates symmetrical balance; greater disparity yields tonal bifurcation.

⸻

3.9 Climax as System Resolution (#entropy-collapse)

Seger’s climax is the narrative’s thermodynamic release: maximum energy converted to meaning.
She distinguishes mechanical climax (physical event) from spiritual climax (internal realization).
The most potent endings synchronize both; PK flags mismatches when emotional payoff precedes mechanical closure.

3.10 Resolution and Aftermath (#equilibrium-reset)

Resolution restores order but must carry residual motion—a sense of continuing life.
Seger instructs: “Endings open doors rather than close them.”

4.1 From Trait to Trajectory (#character-vector)

Seger rejects the “shopping-list” approach to characterization (eye color, hobbies, etc.) in favor of functional vectors—each choice defined by force and direction.

character_vector = (desire – fear) × time

	•	Desire = what drives behavior (conscious goal).
