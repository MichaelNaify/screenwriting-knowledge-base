# LIBRARY 1: CHARACTER REVELATION TECHNIQUES

## Overview

Character revelation = the controlled release of information about who a character truly is. Your texts offer 30+ distinct techniques for revealing character, each with specific strengths and optimal use cases.

---

## 1.1 BACKSTORY REVELATION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Direct Exposition Dump
- **Sources:** Field, Seger, Truby
- **Function:** Rapid information delivery early in script
- **When to Use:**
  - Opening 10 pages when audience needs baseline knowledge
  - Complex premises requiring historical context
  - Genres where setup efficiency matters (thriller, sci-fi)
- **Execution Method:**
  - Single concentrated scene (monologue, interview, flashback montage)
  - Visual evidence (photos, news reports, documents)
  - Dialogue with new character asking questions
- **Advantages:** Efficiency, clarity, audience oriented quickly
- **Risks:** Passive storytelling, may stop dramatic momentum
- **Field's Principle:** "First 10 pages tell reader everything"
- **Quality Test:** Does exposition serve immediate story need or just writer's desire to explain?

---

### Technique: Gradual Revelation Through Behavior
- **Sources:** McKee, Truby, Seger, Owen
- **Function:** Show character history through present actions
- **When to Use:**
  - Mystery/thriller where past is plot-relevant
  - Character's history explains current dysfunction
  - Dramatic irony desired (audience knows more than character)
- **Execution Method:**
  - Behavioral tics revealing trauma (Truby's "ghost")
  - Reaction to specific triggers
  - Repetitive patterns suggesting unresolved past
  - Objects/spaces character avoids or seeks
- **Truby's Framework:** Ghost → Need → Revelation cycle
- **McKee's Principle:** "True character revealed through choice under pressure"
- **Advantages:** Active storytelling, integrates past with present action
- **Risks:** Can be too subtle, audience may miss connections
- **Calibration:** Every 15 pages, add one behavioral clue to backstory

---

### Technique: Flashback Interruption
- **Sources:** Field, Williams, Seger
- **Function:** Direct visualization of formative past events
- **When to Use:**
  - Past event is central mystery or trauma
  - Present action triggers vivid memory
  - Visual impact of past outweighs tell-don't-show rule
- **Execution Variants:**
  1. **Involuntary memory** (trauma trigger)
  2. **Voluntary recollection** (character chooses to remember)
  3. **Unreliable memory** (distorted by emotion/time)
  4. **Parallel cutting** (past/present mirror each other)
- **Field's Structure Placement:**
  - Early Act I: Establish ghost/wound
  - Midpoint: Reveal deeper layer of past
  - Act III: Final recontextualization of past
- **Advantages:** Visceral, emotional, cinematic
- **Risks:** Stops forward momentum, can feel manipulative
- **Williams's Adaptation Principle:** Flashback should reveal what present action cannot

---

### Technique: Dialogue Confession
- **Sources:** Seger, McKee, Chitlik
- **Function:** Character actively shares their history
- **When to Use:**
  - Intimacy/trust breakthrough moment
  - Relationship deepening beat
  - Character reaches point where holding secret costs more than revealing
- **McKee's Scene Value:** Shift from concealment → vulnerability
- **Execution Requirements:**
  - Emotional stakes high enough to justify confession
  - Listener's reaction matters as much as content
  - Confession changes relationship dynamic
- **Seger's Dialogue Layers:**
  1. Surface: What character says happened
  2. Subtext: What it meant to them
  3. Revelation: What they learned about themselves
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Confession is decision, not convenience
  - Timing feels earned, not expositional
  - Listener's response creates new conflict or alliance

---

### Technique: Environmental Archaeology
- **Sources:** Truby, McKee, Seger
- **Function:** Physical spaces reveal character history
- **When to Use:**
  - Character returns to significant location
  - Investigation/discovery plot structure
  - Visual storytelling preferred over dialogue
- **Truby's Symbol Web:** Locations embody psychological states
- **Execution Elements:**
  - Objects preserved or destroyed tell story
  - Spatial relationships reveal power dynamics
  - Physical decay mirrors emotional state
  - Hidden spaces contain hidden truths
- **Example Pattern:**
  - Character enters childhood home
  - Room unchanged = arrested development
  - Room destroyed = trauma externalized
  - Room transformed = growth already begun

## 1.2 CHARACTER ARC CONSTRUCTION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Binary Opposition Arc (McKee Method)
- **Source:** McKee (Story)
- **Function:** Character moves between positive/negative value poles
- **Core Structure:**
  ```
  Starting Value State → Crisis Forces Choice → Opposite Value State
  Example: Fear/Courage, Cynicism/Hope, Isolation/Connection
  ```
- **When to Use:**
  - Clear moral dimension to story
  - Audience needs to measure growth objectively
  - Genre requires satisfying transformation (drama, rom-com)
- **McKee's Scene-Level Implementation:**
  - Every scene shifts character slightly along value spectrum
  - Midpoint: Character achieves false version of positive pole
  - Crisis: Must choose between comfort and truth
  - Climax: Embodies opposite value through action
- **Advantages:** Clear, measurable, emotionally satisfying
- **Risks:** Can feel schematic if not deeply motivated

---

### Technique: Moral Argument Arc (Truby Method)
- **Source:** Truby (Anatomy of a Story)
- **Function:** Character proves/disproves specific thesis about how to live
- **Core Structure:**
  ```
  Flawed Philosophy → Consequences Test Theory → Revised Understanding
  ```
- **Truby's 7-Step Integration:**
  1. **Weakness:** Character's wrong way of living
  2. **Need:** What must change to become whole
  3. **Desire:** External goal pursued
  4. **Opponent:** Embodies opposite philosophy
  5. **Battle:** Philosophies clash
  6. **Self-Revelation:** Character sees truth
  7. **New Equilibrium:** Lives according to new understanding
- **When to Use:**
  - Philosophical/ethical dimensions central to story
  - Character's worldview is the true antagonist
  - Multiple characters represent different life philosophies
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Opponent's philosophy must be equally valid (not just "evil")
  - Revelation feels earned through experience, not told
  - Final action embodies new philosophy

---

### Technique: Three-Dimensional Change (Seger Method)
- **Source:** Seger (Making a Good Script Great)
- **Function:** Character transforms across multiple dimensions simultaneously
- **Three Dimensions:**
  1. **External/Behavioral:** What character does
  2. **Internal/Psychological:** How character thinks/feels
  3. **Spiritual/Philosophical:** What character believes
- **Seger's Integration Principle:** All three must align at climax
- **Example Mapping:**
  ```
  Start → End
  External: Isolated → Connected to community
  Internal: Fearful → Confident
  Spiritual: "I'm worthless" → "I have value"
  ```
- **When to Use:**
  - Complex character-driven drama
  - Literary/prestige projects
  - Long-form narrative (limited series, feature with depth)
- **Execution Guidelines:**
  - Act I: Establish all three dimensions in conflict
  - Act II: Address each dimension in separate sequences
  - Act III: Synchronize all three in single climactic action

---

### Technique: Flat Arc/Change-Agent Character
- **Sources:** Snyder, Vorhaus, Blake
- **Function:** Character stays constant; world changes around them
- **When to Use:**
  - Character embodies story's thesis from start
  - Other characters need to change, not protagonist
  - Genre conventions (action hero, comedy protagonist)
- **Snyder's Beat Sheet Adaptation:**
  - Character has truth but world rejects it
  - Act II: Character's constancy forces others to choose
  - Climax: World transforms to match character's vision
- **Comedy Application (Vorhaus):**
  - Comic protagonist = stable delusional system
  - Supporting cast = reality testing that system
  - Resolution = world accepts or rejects protagonist's reality
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Character's unchanging quality must be tested severely
  - Other characters' arcs compensate for flat protagonist
  - Final action proves character's philosophy was right all along

## 1.3 CHARACTER VOICE DIFFERENTIATION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Diction Variation (Vocabulary Differentiation)
- **Sources:** Seger, Shatz, Blake
- **Function:** Each character uses distinct word choices
- **Parameters to Vary:**
  1. **Register:** Formal vs informal language
  2. **Vocabulary size:** Simple vs complex words
  3. **Technical jargon:** Specialized vs general language
  4. **Cultural markers:** Regional/ethnic/generational phrases
- **Seger's Implementation:**
  - Create word list for each major character
  - Ban certain words/phrases for specific characters
  - Track vocabulary across drafts for consistency
- **Comedy Amplification (Shatz):**
  - Verbal tics = personality compression
  - Malapropisms reveal education/status
  - Overly formal/informal in wrong context = humor
- **Quality Test:** Cover character names; dialogue alone reveals speaker

---

### Technique: Syntax Patterns (Sentence Structure)
- **Sources:** Seger, McKee, Chitlik
- **Function:** Character-specific sentence rhythms and structures
- **Variables to Modulate:**
  1. **Sentence length:** Short/clipped vs long/flowing
  2. **Complexity:** Simple vs compound vs complex structures
  3. **Interruption:** Completes thoughts vs trails off
  4. **Questions vs statements:** Confidence level
- **McKee's Subtext Principle:**
  - Syntax reveals thought process
  - Fragmented = anxiety, confusion
  - Over-complete = controlling, pedantic
  - Questions = uncertainty, manipulation
- **Execution Method:**
  - Write 5 versions of same information in different character voices
  - Analyze syntax patterns that emerge
  - Standardize patterns for consistency

---

### Technique: Rhythm and Tempo (Speech Pace)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Blake, Johnstone (Impro)
- **Function:** Characters speak at different speeds/energies
- **Tempo Variables:**
  - **Fast talkers:** Nervous, manic, intellectual, avoiding
  - **Slow talkers:** Thoughtful, confident, menacing, simple
  - **Variable:** Adaptive, strategic, emotionally responsive
- **Comedy Timing Integration (Shatz):**
  - Rhythm mismatch = comedy (fast/slow pairing)
  - Acceleration through scene = escalation
  - Sudden stops = punctuation for laugh
- **Johnstone's Status Application:**
  - High status = controls rhythm of conversation
  - Low status = adapts to others' rhythm
  - Status shifts = rhythm shifts
- **Script Notation:**
  - Use punctuation to indicate rhythm (ellipses, dashes, periods)
  - Stage directions for key tempo moments
  - Length of speeches suggests natural pace

---

### Technique: Attitude and Worldview (Perspective Signaling)
- **Sources:** Truby, McKee, Seger
- **Function:** Dialogue reveals character's philosophy
- **McKee's Character Dimension:** "Deep character" = worldview + values
- **Truby's Integration:** Attitude = surface expression of moral argument
- **Implementation Vectors:**
  1. **Optimism vs cynicism** in word choice
  2. **Agency vs victimhood** in sentence construction
  3. **Connection vs isolation** in pronoun use (we/I/they)
  4. **Past vs future** orientation in verb tense
- **Example Differentiation:**
  ```
  Optimist: "We'll figure something out."
  Pessimist: "There's no way this works."
  Realist: "We need more information first."
  ```
- **Consistency Rule:** Character's attitude should be traceable through every line

# LIBRARY 2: DIALOGUE CONSTRUCTION TECHNIQUES

## 2.1 SUBTEXT GENERATION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Say the Opposite (Irony Method)
- **Sources:** McKee, Seger, Shatz
- **Function:** Character says opposite of what they mean
- **When to Use:**
  - Character cannot/won't express true feelings
  - Social context prevents directness
  - Comedy through politeness/rudeness mismatch
- **McKee's Principle:** "Subtext is what's not said"
- **Execution Requirements:**
  1. **Establish context:** Why character can't be direct
  2. **Make intent clear:** Audience must understand true meaning
  3. **Create tension:** Gap between words/intent generates energy
- **Comedy Application (Shatz):**
  - Excessive politeness masking rage
  - False enthusiasm revealing disinterest
  - Literal interpretation of ironic statement
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Body language contradicts words
  - Other character's reaction reveals truth
  - Subsequent action proves actual intent

---

### Technique: Deflection/Evasion (Avoidance Method)
- **Sources:** Seger, McKee, Chitlik
- **Function:** Character actively avoids topic
- **When to Use:**
  - Character has secret they're protecting
  - Emotional vulnerability too painful
  - Power dynamic requires maintaining mystery
- **Seger's Dialogue Layers:**
  - Surface: What character says instead
  - Subtext: What they're avoiding
  - Revelation: Why they can't go there
- **Execution Patterns:**
  1. **Topic change:** Abrupt shift to unrelated subject
  2. **Deflecting question:** Answer with different question
  3. **Humor deflection:** Joke to avoid serious topic
  4. **Attack deflection:** Criticize questioner
  5. **Physical deflection:** Leave conversation
- **Chitlik's Rewrite Test:** Does evasion create more interest than revelation would?

---

### Technique: Controlled Omission (Compression Method)
- **Sources:** Seger, Shatz, McKee
- **Function:** Essential information deliberately left out
- **When to Use:**
  - Information reveal is plot point
  - Character testing another character
  - Dramatic irony desired (audience knows, character doesn't)
- **Seger's Rule:** "Write the space between words"
- **Compression Algorithm:**
  1. Write fully explicit version
  2. Remove every line that states obvious
  3. Remove every adjective that shows feeling
  4. Let behavior carry meaning
- **Shatz's Comedy Variant:** Omit setup, deliver punchline, audience fills gap
- **Quality Test:** Omission creates productive confusion, not bewilderment

---

### Technique: Behavioral Contradiction (Action vs Words)
- **Sources:** McKee, Field, Seger
- **Function:** Character's actions contradict their dialogue
- **McKee's Principle:** "True character revealed in action, not dialogue"
- **When to Use:**
  - Character is self-deceptive
  - Character is lying (to others or self)
  - Gap between identity and behavior is theme
- **Execution Examples:**
  ```
  Says: "I don't care about her."
  Does: Checks phone constantly for her messages.

  Says: "I'm brave."
  Does: Avoids every confrontation.

  Says: "Family is everything."
  Does: Never visits, always working.
  ```
- **Field's Structure Placement:**
  - Act I: Establish contradiction
  - Act II: Consequences of contradiction
  - Act III: Force character to choose (words or actions?)

## 2.2 RHYTHM AND TEMPO TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Stichomythia (Rapid Exchange)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Blake, McKee
- **Function:** Fast back-and-forth single lines
- **When to Use:**
  - High-tension confrontation
  - Comic energy/escalation
  - Intellectual sparring
  - Battle of wills
- **Structure Pattern:**
  ```
  A: Short statement.
  B: Short response.
  A: Shorter response.
  B: Shortest response.
  [Pause]
  A or B: Longer break-rhythm line = turning point
  ```
- **Shatz's Comedy Timing:** Each line shortens until impossible to continue = explosion
- **McKee's Value Shift:** Each line must change power balance slightly
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Escalating intensity through shortening
  - Clear power shifts with each exchange
  - Inevitable conclusion/explosion
- **Common Errors:**
  - Lines too long (should be 3-7 words each)
  - No escalation (just trading information)
  - Goes on too long (should be 8-12 exchanges max)

---

### Technique: Rhythmic Variation (Pace Modulation)
- **Sources:** Seger, Shatz, Chitlik
- **Function:** Mix fast and slow passages for contrast
- **Seger's Principle:** "Variety equals vitality"
- **Pattern Formula:**
  ```
  Slow (Setup) → Fast (Conflict) → Slow (Aftermath)
  Long (Monologue) → Short (Reaction) → Medium (Resolution)
  ```
- **When to Use:**
  - Controlling scene energy
  - Preventing monotony
  - Emphasizing key moments through contrast
- **Execution Guidelines:**
  - No two adjacent scenes with same rhythm
  - Fast scenes feel faster after slow ones
  - Vary speech length within single scene
- **Shatz's Comedy Rule:** Rhythm break = surprise = laughter opportunity

---

### Technique: Pause and Silence (Negative Space)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Johnstone, Seger
- **Function:** Absence of dialogue carries meaning
- **Types of Silence:**
  1. **Pregnant pause:** Unspoken understanding
  2. **Awkward silence:** Social discomfort = comedy
  3. **Power silence:** Strategic withholding
  4. **Processing silence:** Character absorbing information
  5. **Emotional silence:** Too much to express in words
- **Shatz's Timing Mathematics:**
  - 0.3-0.6 seconds = clean laugh beat
  - 1-2 seconds = awkward comedy
  - 3+ seconds = dramatic weight/menace
- **Johnstone's Status Rule:** High status controls when silence breaks
- **Script Notation:**
  - [beat] = short pause (0.5 sec)
  - [pause] = medium pause (1-2 sec)
  - [long silence] = extended (3+ sec)

## 2.3 COMEDY DIALOGUE TECHNIQUES

### Technique: The Rule of Three (Pattern + Surprise)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Vorhaus, Blake
- **Function:** Establish pattern, break it on third iteration
- **Psychological Basis:**
  - First: Introduction
  - Second: Confirmation of pattern
  - Third: Expectation → Surprise
- **Execution Formulas:**

  **A. Escalation Pattern:**
  ```
  Small → Medium → Huge
  "I'm hungry" → "I'm starving" → "I'm eating the furniture"
  ```

  **B. Repetition + Twist:**
  ```
  Statement → Statement → Opposite
  "I love you" → "I love you" → "I love cake more"
  ```

  **C. Build + Undercut:**
  ```
  Setup → Setup → Deflation
  "Epic" → "Legendary" → "Okay, it was fine"
  ```

- **Vorhaus's T+P+D Formula:** Truth + Pain + Distance = third beat reveals
- **Quality Markers:**
  - First two items clearly establish pattern
  - Third breaks pattern while staying logical
  - Timing gap before third item builds anticipation

---

### Technique: Callback (Temporal Loop)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Vorhaus, Blake
- **Function:** Reference earlier joke/moment for compounded effect
- **Shatz's Code:** `callback = reference(previous beat)`
- **When to Use:**
  - Establishing running gag
  - Creating structure across long narrative
  - Paying off seemingly random earlier moment
- **Execution Requirements:**
  1. **Initial plant:** Moment must be distinctive/memorable
  2. **Time gap:** Sufficient distance for surprise (20+ pages)
  3. **Contextual shift:** New context reframes original moment
  4. **Recognition:** Clear enough for audience to connect
- **Blake's Comedy Structure:**
  - Plant callbacks in Act I "Fun and Games"
  - Pay off in Act II escalation
  - Final callback in resolution = full-circle closure
- **Vorhaus's Principle:** "Recognition + Difference = Pleasure × 2"

---

### Technique: Misdirection (Late Predicate Reveal)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Vorhaus
- **Function:** Delay key information to create surprise
- **Shatz's Linguistics:** `reverse(parse)` = reinterpret all that came before
- **Structure Pattern:**
  ```
  Setup: "I saw the most beautiful thing today..."
  Delay: [Description building expectation]
  Reveal: "...it was a sandwich."
  ```
- **Comedy Value:** Gap between expected/actual meaning
- **Execution Guidelines:**
  - Build rich, detailed description first
  - Withhold key contextual word
  - Reveal reframes everything retrospectively
- **Quality Test:** Audience should want to re-hear joke after reveal

---

### Technique: Double Entendre (Dual Semantic Pathway)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Blake
- **Function:** Line carries two simultaneous meanings
- **Shatz's Code:** `if ambiguity = maintained > threshold`
- **When to Use:**
  - Innuendo required (sexual, dark)
  - Character saying one thing, meaning another
  - Comedy through misunderstanding
- **Execution Requirements:**
  1. **Both meanings must be clear** to audience
  2. **Context supports both** interpretations
  3. **Neither undermines dramatic needs** of scene
- **Blake's Status Work:** Low-status character doesn't get innuendo = comedy
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Works on first hearing and on reflection
  - Neither meaning feels forced
  - Innocence/knowledge gap between characters

# LIBRARY 3: SCENE CONSTRUCTION TECHNIQUES

## 3.1 SCENE OPENING TECHNIQUES

### Technique: In Media Res Start (Field/McKee)
- **Sources:** Field, McKee, Seger
- **Function:** Begin scene mid-action/mid-conversation
- **McKee's Principle:** "Cut in late, get out early"
- **When to Use:**
  - Scene's setup is obvious/unimportant
  - Momentum matters more than orientation
  - Genre demands pace (action, thriller)
- **Execution Method:**
  - Cut first 3-5 lines that establish who/where/why
  - Begin at moment of first conflict/surprise
  - Context becomes clear through dialogue/action
- **Field's Structure Benefit:** Gains 2-3 pages per act = tighter script
- **Advantages:** Efficiency, energy, respects audience intelligence
- **Risks:** Disorientation if context too obscure

---

### Technique: Atmospheric Establishment (Truby/Seger)
- **Sources:** Truby, Seger, Williams
- **Function:** Open with location/mood before character action
- **When to Use:**
  - Setting is character (horror, western, period)
  - Mood establishment is priority
  - Contrast with character's entrance desired
- **Truby's Story World:** Location embodies theme
- **Execution Elements:**
  - Visual details that suggest history
  - Sound design establishing tone
  - Weather/time reflecting emotional state
  - Character enters as disruption or extension
- **Seger's Integration:** Opening image should connect to theme
- **Quality Test:** Atmosphere serves story, not just description

---

### Technique: Visual Hook (Snyder)
- **Sources:** Snyder, Field, Seger
- **Function:** Arresting image that captures attention immediately
- **Snyder's "Opening Image" Principle:** First image = story's thesis
- **When to Use:**
  - Visual medium (film vs TV where dialogue dominates)
  - Genre promises spectacle
  - Establishing contrast for character arc
- **Execution Requirements:**
  - Image should be filmable, not abstract
  - Must contain seeds of story/theme
  - Should invert by "Final Image" for symmetry
- **Examples by Genre:**
  - Drama: Protagonist in defining environment
  - Comedy: Character in escalating crisis
  - Thriller: Ominous normal about to break
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Memorable without dialogue
  - Suggests larger story
  - Functions dramatically, not just aesthetically

---

## 3.2 SCENE ESCALATION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Progressive Complication (McKee)
- **Source:** McKee (Story)
- **Function:** Each beat raises stakes/difficulty
- **McKee's Gap Mechanics:**
  ```
  Character's Plan → Unexpected Result → Revised Plan → Worse Result
  ```
- **Escalation Dimensions:**
  1. **External stakes:** What's at risk increases
  2. **Internal stakes:** Emotional vulnerability deepens
  3. **Moral stakes:** Ethical clarity decreases
  4. **Temporal stakes:** Time pressure intensifies
- **Scene Structure Pattern:**
  ```
  Beat 1: Character acts
  Beat 2: Obstacle appears
  Beat 3: Character adapts
  Beat 4: Worse obstacle appears
  Beat 5: Character forced to desperate action
  ```
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Each complication feels organic, not manufactured
  - Character's responses become more revealing
  - No option is repeated (forcing innovation)

---

### Technique: Emotional Amplification (Seger)
- **Source:** Seger (Making a Good Script Great, Advanced Screenwriting)
- **Function:** Increase emotional intensity through scene
- **Seger's Energy Curve Principle:**
  ```
  Beginning Intensity → Rising Intensity → Peak → Resolution
  ```
- **Execution Methods:**
  1. **Volume increase:** Literal or figurative loudness
  2. **Proximity increase:** Physical closeness/distance shifts
  3. **Revelation increase:** Truths get more personal
  4. **Vulnerability increase:** Defenses strip away
- **Integration with Dialogue:**
  - Sentences shorten as emotion intensifies
  - Pauses lengthen or disappear
  - Rhythm becomes irregular/unpredictable
- **Quality Test:** Peak emotion should be at/near scene climax

---

### Technique: Comic Escalation (Shatz/Vorhaus)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Vorhaus, Blake
- **Function:** Compound misunderstandings/errors exponentially
- **Vorhaus's Formula:** Each complication ×1.5 funnier than previous
- **Structure Pattern:**
  ```
  Normal → Slight Problem → Problem Compounds → 
  Desperate Solution → Worse Problem → Chaos → Collapse
  ```
- **Shatz's Mechanics:**
  - Each beat adds new variable to chaos equation
  - Character's solutions create next problem
  - Authority figure approaches = ticking clock
- **Blake's Physical Comedy Rule:**
  - Visual complications more effective than verbal
  - Each physical action should be reversible (for callback)
  - Final beat = character accepts/embraces chaos
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Logic chain is traceable (not random)
  - Each beat funnier than last
  - Collapse feels inevitable in retrospect

---

## 3.3 SCENE ENDING TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Button (Turn and Out)
- **Sources:** McKee, Seger, Field
- **Function:** Final beat shifts value, creating momentum to next scene
- **McKee's Principle:** "Every scene must turn value at least once"
- **Execution Pattern:**
  ```
  Scene builds to moment → Reversal → End immediately
  ```
- **Field's Cut Rule:** "Get out when scene's purpose is achieved"
- **Button Types:**
  1. **Question button:** Ends with unanswered question
  2. **Action button:** Physical choice = commitment
  3. **Reveal button:** Information changes everything
  4. **Emotion button:** Feeling shifts and holds
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Value shift is clear and significant
  - Audience propelled to want next scene
  - No "trailing" dialogue after button

---

### Technique: Cliffhanger (Suspense Extension)
- **Sources:** Field, Seger, Williams
- **Function:** End at moment of maximum tension/uncertainty
- **When to Use:**
  - Act breaks
  - Episode endings (TV)
  - Mid-sequence momentum
  - Building dread/anticipation
- **Structure Types:**

  **A. Physical Danger:**
  Character in immediate peril, outcome unknown

  **B. Decision Point:**
  Character must choose, we don't see choice

  **C. Revelation Withheld:**
  Information about to be revealed, cut before reveal

  **D. Confrontation Interrupted:**
  Key conflict beginning, cut before resolution
  
- **Field's Timing:** Major cliffhangers at pp. 30, 60, 90 (act breaks)
- **Quality Test:** Cliffhanger must matter to story, not just manipulate

---

### Technique: Comic Punctuation (Shatz/Vorhaus)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Vorhaus, Blake
- **Function:** End scene with laugh beat
- **Types of Comic Endings:**
  1. **Visual gag button:** Physical joke ends scene
  2. **Dialogue topper:** Final line gets laugh
  3. **Callback button:** Reference to earlier joke
  4. **Reaction shot button:** Character's face = commentary
  5. **Ironic juxtaposition:** Cut to opposite situation
- **Shatz's Timing Rule:** 
  - Beat → Pause (0.5 sec) → Cut = clean laugh
  - No dialogue after laugh line
- **Vorhaus's Test:** If it doesn't get laugh, it's wrong button
- **Blake's Status Rule:** Low status character should get final word = comedy

# LIBRARY 4: STRUCTURE BUILDING TECHNIQUES

## 4.1 ACT I CONSTRUCTION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: World + Character + Problem (Field)
- **Source:** Field (Screenplay)
- **Function:** Efficient setup of story elements
- **Field's First-10-Pages Rule:**
  - Page 1-3: Who is protagonist?
  - Page 3-7: What is their normal world?
  - Page 7-10: What problem disrupts world?
- **Execution Checklist:**
  - [ ] Protagonist introduced with defining action
  - [ ] World established through behavior + environment
  - [ ] Problem shown (not told) through event
  - [ ] Stakes clear: what happens if problem unsolved?
- **Quality Markers:**
  - All three elements visible by page 10
  - Each element connected to others
  - Setup implies what Act II will test

---

### Technique: Design Principle + Ghost (Truby)
- **Source:** Truby (Anatomy of a Story)
- **Function:** Establish character's wound and world's theme
- **Truby's Opening Strategy:**
  - **Designing Principle:** One sentence capturing story's essence
  - **Ghost:** Past event causing character's weakness
  - **Integration:** Ghost explains protagonist's flaw in this world
- **Execution Pattern:**
  ```
  Pages 1-5: Character's current coping mechanism (flaw visible)
  Pages 5-10: Hint of what broke them (ghost)
  Pages 10-15: World that will test this flaw (design principle)
  ```
- **Quality Test:** Flaw + Ghost + Theme should feel inevitable together

---

### Technique: Catalyst + Debate (Snyder)
- **Source:** Snyder (Save the Cat!)
- **Function:** Problem introduced, character hesitates
- **Snyder's Beat Placement:**
  - **Catalyst (p. 12):** Inciting incident
  - **Debate (pp. 12-25):** Should protagonist engage?
- **Why Debate Matters:**
  - Proves stakes are serious (hesitation = fear)
  - Allows audience to understand resistance
  - Makes eventual commitment feel earned
- **Execution Variants:**
  1. **External debate:** Character discusses with others
  2. **Internal debate:** Character weighs pros/cons alone
  3. **Action debate:** Character tries to avoid, forced back
  4. **Time debate:** World won't wait for decision
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Valid reasons to refuse call to action
  - Escalating pressure forces decision
  - Break into Act II = crossing point of no return

---

## 4.2 ACT II CONSTRUCTION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Midpoint Mirror (Field/McKee)
- **Sources:** Field, McKee, Seger, Snyder
- **Function:** Scene/revelation that shifts protagonist's understanding
- **Field's Page 60 Principle:** Midpoint = highest point or lowest point
- **McKee's Value Shift:** False victory → true defeat, or vice versa
- **Snyder's Types:**
  1. **False Victory:** Protagonist thinks they've won (haven't)
  2. **False Defeat:** Protagonist thinks they've lost (haven't)
- **Execution Requirements:**
  - Significant information revelation
  - Requires protagonist to revise strategy completely
  - Second half feels like different movie/show
- **Seger's Energy Principle:** Midpoint = story's moral center
- **Quality Test:** Script should feel unbalanced without midpoint

---

### Technique: Progressive Obstacles (McKee/Truby)
- **Sources:** McKee, Truby, Field
- **Function:** Each obstacle more difficult than last
- **McKee's Complication Principle:**
  ```
  Obstacle 1: External problem
  Obstacle 2: External problem + internal doubt
  Obstacle 3: External problem + internal doubt + moral dimension
  ```
- **Truby's Opponent Evolution:**
  - Early Act II: Opponent underestimated
  - Mid Act II: Opponent reveals deeper threat
  - Late Act II: Opponent attacks protagonist's core weakness
- **Sequence Structure (Field):**
  - Sequence 1: Protagonist attacks problem directly (fails)
  - Sequence 2: Protagonist tries different approach (fails worse)
  - Sequence 3: Protagonist tries desperate measure (catastrophic failure)
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Each obstacle requires new character resources
  - Failures reveal character more than successes
  - Final obstacle forces protagonist to change approach entirely

---

### Technique: Subplot Weaving (Seger)
- **Source:** Seger (Advanced Screenwriting)
- **Function:** Integrate B-story for thematic resonance
- **Seger's Strand Matrix:**
  ```
  Main Plot: External problem/journey
  B-Story: Relationship that embodies theme
  C-Story: Counter-thematic subplot
  D-Story: Visual/symbolic motif
  ```
- **Integration Rules:**
  - Main plot scene → B-story scene → Main plot scene (alternation)
  - Every subplot connects to same theme from different angle
  - Subplots must resolve in Act III
- **Snyder's "B-Story" Placement:**
  - Introduced after Break into Two
  - Provides emotional/thematic counterpoint to main plot
  - Climaxes separately but influences main plot resolution
- **Quality Test:** Remove subplot; if main plot unchanged, subplot is decorative

---

## 4.3 ACT III CONSTRUCTION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Dark Night + Breakthrough (Snyder/Truby)
- **Sources:** Snyder, Truby, McKee
- **Function:** Protagonist's lowest point → revelation → new approach
- **Snyder's Beat Structure (pp. 75-85):**
  - **All Is Lost (p. 75):** Complete failure, strategy exhausted
  - **Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75-85):** Reflection, seems hopeless
  - **Break into Three (p. 85):** Insight/revelation → new synthesis
- **Truby's Self-Revelation Integration:**
  - Dark Night = forced to see self clearly
  - Breakthrough = understand what must change internally
  - Action = embody new understanding
- **McKee's Value System:**
  - Dark Night = negative value pole fully experienced
  - Breakthrough = discover how to access positive pole
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Dark Night must feel genuinely insurmountable
  - Breakthrough comes from character, not convenient help
  - New approach integrates all that character learned

---

### Technique: Climax Unification (Field/McKee/Truby)
- **Sources:** Field, McKee, Truby, Seger
- **Function:** External and internal arcs resolve simultaneously
- **Field's Structure:**
  - External climax: Protagonist confronts main problem
  - Internal climax: Protagonist becomes who they needed to be
  - Unified climax: Internal change enables external victory
- **McKee's Value Synchronization:**
  ```
  External Action + Internal State = Same Value
  Example: 
  - External: Defeats villain
  - Internal: Overcomes fear
  - Unity: Defeats villain BECAUSE overcomes fear
  ```
- **Truby's Moral Argument:**
  - Battle = external expression of internal change
  - Self-revelation moment happens DURING battle
  - New equilibrium = living according to new philosophy
- **Seger's Three Dimensions:**
  - Behavioral, psychological, spiritual all shift in same action
  - Climax = all dimensions align for first time
- **Quality Test:** Remove internal arc; does external climax still satisfy? (Should not)

---

### Technique: Resolution Velocity (Chitlik/Seger)
- **Sources:** Chitlik, Seger, Field
- **Function:** Efficient wrap-up after climax
- **Chitlik's Rule:** "Denouement should be 1/10 length of setup"
- **What to Include:**
  - Immediate aftermath of climactic action
  - Key relationship resolution (if not part of climax)
  - New equilibrium state (protagonist in changed world)
  - Thematic punctuation (final statement of meaning)
- **What to Exclude:**
  - Explaining what audience already understood
  - Showing every character's future
  - Rehashing earlier events
- **Field's Page Count:**
  - Setup (Act I): 25-30 pages
  - Resolution (after climax): 2-5 pages maximum
- **Seger's Energy Principle:**
  - Emotional gradient should slope gently down
  - Not flat-line stop (too abrupt)
  - Not long tail (exhausting)
- **Snyder's "Final Image":**
  - Mirror of Opening Image showing transformation
  - Visual proof of character change
  - Thematic punctuation

# LIBRARY 5: REWRITING TECHNIQUES

## 5.1 DIAGNOSTIC TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Value Shift Audit (McKee)
- **Source:** McKee (Story)
- **Function:** Track value changes to identify flat/redundant scenes
- **Execution Method:**
  1. List every scene
  2. Identify opening value state (+/–/neutral)
  3. Identify closing value state (+/–/neutral)
  4. Calculate: Same value = 0, Shift = 1
  5. Target: 90%+ of scenes = 1
- **Problem Identification:**
  - Scene with 0 = cut or add turning point
  - Multiple 0s in sequence = structural problem
  - Value shifts in wrong direction = tone problem
- **McKee's Rule:** "No scene without change"
- **Integration with Seger:** Combine with beat economy calculation

---

### Technique: Beat Density Scan (Seger)
- **Source:** Seger (Making a Good Script Great)
- **Function:** Calculate scene efficiency
- **Seger's Formula:**
  ```
  Beat Efficiency = (Number of Value Shifts) / (Scene Length in Pages)
  Target Range: 0.85-1.00
  ```
- **Execution Steps:**
  1. Count significant beats in each scene
  2. Measure scene length
  3. Calculate ratio
  4. Flag scenes below 0.85
- **Problem Identification:**
  - Low ratio = too much talking, not enough action
  - Very high ratio (>1.5) = too rushed, no breathing room
- **Rewrite Strategy:**
  - Low: Add conflict/complication/reversal
  - High: Extend key beats for emotional resonance

---

### Technique: Transition Flow Analysis (Chitlik)
- **Source:** Chitlik (Rewrite)
- **Function:** Identify momentum leaks between scenes
- **Chitlik's Momentum Continuity Principle:**
  - Energy should flow scene-to-scene with minimal loss
  - Each scene should raise question next scene answers
- **Audit Method:**
  1. Read scene endings + next scene beginnings
  2. Rate connection: Strong/Medium/Weak/None
  3. Target: 80%+ Strong or Medium
- **Problem Patterns:**
  - Weak: Scenes don't connect causally
  - None: Random scene order, restructure needed
- **Repair Strategies:**
  - Add connecting dialogue at scene end
  - Reorder scenes for better flow
  - Cut scenes that don't connect

---

## 5.2 STRUCTURAL REPAIR TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Setup-Payoff Ledger (Field/Seger)
- **Sources:** Field, Seger
- **Function:** Ensure every setup pays off, every payoff is setup
- **Execution Method:**
  1. Create two-column list:
     - Column A: All setups (promises, questions, objects, skills)
     - Column B: Page where each pays off
  2. Target: 95%+ of setups have payoffs
  3. Reverse audit: All payoffs have setups
- **Problem Identification:**
  - Setup without payoff = delete or add payoff
  - Payoff without setup = add setup or delete payoff
  - Setup too close to payoff = spread further apart
- **Field's Timing Rules:**
  - Minor payoff: 15-25 pages after setup
  - Major payoff: 30-60 pages after setup
  - Act III payoffs: Setup in Act I or earlier Act II
- **Seger's Thematic Payoff:**
  - Emotional payoffs must also pay off thematically
  - Object/skill payoffs should embody character arc

---

### Technique: Sequence Restructure (Field/Chitlik)
- **Sources:** Field, Chitlik
- **Function:** Reorganize scene order for maximum impact
- **Field's Sequence Definition:**
  - Sequence = 8-15 minute narrative unit
  - Each sequence has mini-arc (setup, complication, resolution)
  - Feature = 8-10 sequences
- **Restructure Process:**
  1. Break script into sequences
  2. Evaluate each sequence's function
  3. Test alternate sequence orders
  4. Optimize for causality and escalation
- **Chitlik's Causality Test:**
  - Can Scene A and Scene B be swapped without breaking logic?
  - If yes: weak causality, strengthen connections
  - If no: sequence locked, good structure
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Each sequence raises stakes from previous
  - Sequences can be summarized in one sentence
  - Removing any sequence creates plot hole

---

## 5.3 CHARACTER REVISION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Arc Calibration (Seger/Truby)
- **Sources:** Seger, Truby
- **Function:** Ensure character arc is traceable and complete
- **Seger's Three-Dimension Check:**
  1. Map external behavior change: Before → After
  2. Map internal psychological change: Before → After
  3. Map philosophical change: Before → After
  4. Verify all three shift in same direction at climax
- **Truby's Self-Revelation Audit:**
  - Mark page where character learns weakness
  - Mark page where character faces weakness
  - Mark page where character overcomes weakness
  - Gaps should be: Learn (Act I) → Face (Mid-Act II) → Overcome (Act III)
- **Repair Strategies:**
  - Missing dimension: Add scenes showing that arc
  - Unearned change: Add complications showing why change is hard
  - Unclear change: Sharpen contrasts between before/after states

---

### Technique: Voice Differentiation Pass (Chitlik)
- **Source:** Chitlik (Rewrite)
- **Function:** Ensure each character has distinct voice
- **Chitlik's Seven-Pass Method - Dialogue Pass:**
  - Read through entire script for one character only
  - Mark every line that could be said by different character
  - Rewrite those lines to be character-specific
  - Repeat for each major character
- **Execution Tools:**
  1. **Diction list:** Unique words/phrases per character
  2. **Syntax pattern:** Sentence structures per character
  3. **Attitude marker:** Worldview expressed per character
  4. **Rhythm signature:** Speech pace per character
- **Quality Test:**
  - Cover character names
  - If you can identify speaker by dialogue alone = pass
  - If confused about speaker = needs revision

---

## 5.4 DIALOGUE TIGHTENING TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Compression Drill (Shatz/Seger)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Seger
- **Function:** Remove redundancy, increase density
- **Shatz's Algorithm:** "Halve dialogue until funny survives"
- **Execution Method:**
  1. Take any speech longer than 3 lines
  2. Cut it in half
  3. Test: Does scene still work?
  4. If yes: keep shorter version
  5. If no: restore minimum needed for clarity
- **Seger's Variant:** "Cut on the second line you like"
  - Don't keep every good line
  - Keep the BEST line only
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Subtext increases as text decreases
  - Rhythm improves (less is more)
  - Actor delivery options expand

---

### Technique: Subtext Amplification (McKee/Seger)
- **Sources:** McKee, Seger
- **Function:** Increase gap between words and meaning
- **McKee's Principle:** "Subtext is everything"
- **Execution Method:**
  1. Identify key emotional scene
  2. Write dialogue explicitly stating emotions
  3. Rewrite: Characters say opposite or deflect
  4. Add: Physical action contradicts words
  5. Test: Is emotion clearer now?
- **Seger's Five-Layer Test:**
  - Does line work on:
    1. Literal level (surface information)
    2. Emotional level (feeling expressed)
    3. Relationship level (status/power)
    4. Subtext level (hidden meaning)
    5. Thematic level (story's big idea)
  - Target: 3+ layers per important line
- **Repair Strategy:** If too explicit, make indirect

# LIBRARY 6: COMEDY WRITING TECHNIQUES

## 6.1 JOKE CONSTRUCTION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Setup + Misdirection + Punchline (Shatz)
- **Source:** Shatz (Comedy Writing Secrets)
- **Function:** Basic joke structure
- **Three Components:**
  1. **Setup:** Establish expectation/pattern
  2. **Misdirection:** Lead audience toward false conclusion
  3. **Punchline:** Reveal unexpected truth
- **Shatz's Formula:**
  ```
  Comic Effect = (Expectation × Delay × Reversal) / Recognition
  ```
- **Execution Guidelines:**
  - Setup must be clear and quick (2-4 sentences max)
  - Misdirection should feel natural, not forced
  - Punchline works best when shortest element
- **Timing Principle:**
  - Delay between setup and punchline = tension
  - Too short: no anticipation
  - Too long: forgetting setup
  - Sweet spot: 3-7 seconds for standup, 15-30 seconds for scene

---

### Technique: Incongruity Bridging (Vorhaus/Shatz)
- **Sources:** Vorhaus (Comic Toolbox), Shatz
- **Function:** Juxtapose incompatible elements logically
- **Vorhaus's T+P+D Formula:**
  - **Truth:** Relatable human situation
  - **Pain:** Vulnerability or flaw
  - **Distance:** Enough exaggeration for safety
- **Incongruity Types:**
  1. **Verbal:** Formal language + crude subject
  2. **Physical:** Dignified person + undignified action
  3. **Situational:** High-status + low-status context
  4. **Logical:** Rigorous reasoning → absurd conclusion
- **Execution Method:**
  - Identify two normally-separate categories
  - Find logical bridge connecting them
  - Escalate incongruity while maintaining logic
- **Shatz's Incongruity Bridge:** "Surprise without confusion"
- **Quality Test:** Audience should think "That's ridiculous AND that makes sense"

---

### Technique: Character as Comedy (Blake/Vorhaus)
- **Sources:** Blake (Writing the Comedy Movie), Vorhaus
- **Function:** Base humor in character essence, not just situations
- **Blake's Principle:** "Comedy is character in action"
- **Character Comedy Sources:**
  1. **Flaw amplification:** Normal human weakness × 10
  2. **Obsession comedy:** Character cares too much about trivial thing
  3. **Delusion comedy:** Gap between self-perception and reality
  4. **Competence mismatch:** Wrong person for the job
- **Vorhaus's Comic Character Checklist:**
  - [ ] Clear, singular obsession
  - [ ] Blind spot preventing self-awareness
  - [ ] Consistent behavioral pattern
  - [ ] Exaggerated but relatable
- **Integration with Structure:**
  - Act I: Establish comic flaw
  - Act II: Flaw causes escalating problems
  - Act III: Character either accepts or transcends flaw

---

## 6.2 COMIC SCENE TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Status Transactions (Johnstone)
- **Source:** Johnstone (Impro)
- **Function:** Generate comedy through status shifts
- **Johnstone's Status Principle:**
  - Every interaction = status transaction
  - Comedy = status shifts opposite to expectation
- **Four Status Moves:**
  1. **Offer Status:** Compliment/elevate other = softens scene
  2. **Grab Status:** Interrupt/dominate = creates tension
  3. **Reverse Status:** Unexpected humility = triggers laughter
  4. **Collapse Status:** Public failure = cathartic release
- **Execution Pattern:**
  ```
  Setup: Establish status hierarchy (high/low)
  Complication: Threat to that hierarchy
  Reversal: Status inverts
  Comedy: Larger the gap, louder the laugh
  ```
- **Blake's Application:**
  - High status character reduced to low status = comedy gold
  - Low status character attempting high status = cringe comedy
  - Rapid status oscillation = energy and surprise

---

### Technique: Escalating Absurdity (Blake/Shatz)
- **Sources:** Blake, Shatz, Vorhaus
- **Function:** Build comedy through compounding logic
- **Structure Pattern:**
  ```
  Normal → Slightly Off → Clearly Absurd → Completely Insane → 
  Embrace Chaos → Collapse
  ```
- **Execution Rules:**
  1. Each beat ×1.5 more absurd than previous
  2. Internal logic must remain consistent
  3. Character treats absurdity as normal
  4. Supporting cast reacts to absurdity
  5. Collapse when system can't escalate further
- **Blake's Principle:** "The larger the stakes, the funnier the collapse"
- **Shatz's Timing:** Escalation should take 3-5 minutes screen time
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Each escalation feels inevitable in retrospect
  - Character's choices (not random events) drive escalation
  - Collapse provides catharsis not frustration

---

## 6.3 COMEDY DIALOGUE TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Malapropism and Word Play (Shatz)
- **Source:** Shatz (Comedy Writing Secrets)
- **Function:** Generate humor through language distortion
- **Types:**
  1. **Malapropism:** Wrong word, similar sound
     - "I'm not superstitious, but I'm a little stitious"
  2. **Spoonerism:** Swap initial sounds
     - "You have hissed all my mystery lectures"
  3. **Pun:** Multiple meanings of same word
     - "Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana"
  4. **Double entendre:** Innocent + suggestive meanings
- **Shatz's Code:** `phoneme shift → semantic gap`
- **Character Integration:**
  - Reveals education level
  - Shows status (low-status = malapropisms)
  - Creates consistent comic voice
- **Quality Test:** Should reveal character while generating laugh

---

### Technique: Deadpan Delivery (Shatz/Johnstone)
- **Sources:** Shatz, Johnstone, Vorhaus
- **Function:** Contrast between content and delivery
- **Principle:** Say absurd things in normal tone, normal things in absurd tone
- **Johnstone's Status Application:**
  - Deadpan = high status (emotional control)
  - Reactive = low status (emotional chaos)
  - Comedy = deadpan character amid chaos
- **Script Notation:**
  - Stage direction: "(matter-of-factly)" or "(flatly)"
  - Content does comic work, not performance
- **Execution Requirements:**
  - Context must be chaotic or emotional
  - Deadpan character seems oblivious or suppressed
  - Works best with specific, concrete language
- **Shatz's Cognitive Effect:** "Emotional flatness → audience projection"

# LIBRARY 7: ADAPTATION TECHNIQUES

## 7.1 SOURCE MATERIAL SELECTION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Fidelity vs. Freedom Matrix (Hutcheon)
- **Source:** Hutcheon (A Theory of Adaptation)
- **Function:** Determine how closely to follow source
- **Hutcheon's Fidelity Spectrum:**
  ```
  Literal → Faithful → Interpretive → Transformative → Inspired
  ```
- **Selection Factors:**
  1. **Source prestige:** Classic = more fidelity pressure
  2. **Medium differences:** Some elements untranslatable
  3. **Audience familiarity:** Known source = fidelity expectations
  4. **Adaptive purpose:** Honor source vs. use as springboard
- **Execution Method:**
  - Map source elements by adaptability (easy/medium/hard)
  - Decide fidelity level per element, not globally
  - Communicate choices to stakeholders early
- **Hutcheon's Principle:** "Adaptation is not a failed copy but a different text"

---

### Technique: Core Extraction (Williams/Owen)
- **Sources:** Williams, Owen
- **Function:** Identify essential elements that must transfer
- **Williams's Adaptation Questions:**
  1. What is story fundamentally about? (theme)
  2. What makes source unique? (voice/world)
  3. What produces emotional impact? (mechanism)
  4. What will audience expect? (contract)
- **Owen's Practitioner Method:**
  - Read source without note-taking
  - Write one-paragraph summary from memory
  - That paragraph = core essence
  - Everything else = negotiable
- **Quality Test:** If core elements removed, is it still same story?

---

## 7.2 MEDIUM TRANSLATION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Showing vs. Telling Conversion (Owen/Seger)
- **Sources:** Owen, Seger
- **Function:** Convert novel's interior monologue to cinema's action
- **Owen's Rule:** "Film is language of glance and gesture"
- **Translation Strategies:**

  **A. Internalization → Externalization:**
  ```
  Novel: "She felt betrayed and angry"
  Screen: She slams door, silent meal, avoids eye contact
  ```

  **B. Thought → Dialogue:**
  ```
  Novel: [Internal struggle about choice]
  Screen: Character discusses with friend or antagonist
  ```

  **C. Narration → Visual Motif:**
  ```
  Novel: Narrator describes isolation
  Screen: Empty frame compositions, lone figure in crowds
  ```

  **D. Exposition → Discovery:**
  ```
  Novel: Author explains backstory
  Screen: Character encounters artifact revealing past
  ```

- **Seger's Visual Density Principle:** Every scene should have filmable image
- **Quality Test:** If page has no action/dialogue/image, how to adapt?

---

### Technique: Compression and Selection (Williams)
- **Source:** Williams (Screen Adaptation Beyond the Basics)
- **Function:** Reduce 400-page novel to 110-page screenplay
- **Williams's Compression Strategies:**
  1. **Composite characters:** Merge 2-3 characters into 1
  2. **Scene compression:** Multiple source scenes → 1 screen scene
  3. **Subplot elimination:** Keep only subplots serving main theme
  4. **Time compression:** Years → months, months → weeks
  5. **Action compression:** Summarize through montage
- **Selection Criteria:**
  - **Essential:** Drives plot or reveals character
  - **Important:** Enriches but not essential
  - **Optional:** Nice but cuttable
  - **Expendable:** Delete without loss
- **Target Ratios:**
  - Novel: 50-60% of original content
  - Play: 70-80% of original content
  - True story: 30-40% of actual events
- **Quality Test:** Does compression maintain emotional truth?

---

## 7.3 ETHICAL ADAPTATION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Representation Audit (Owen/Seger)
- **Sources:** Owen, Seger
- **Function:** Ensure adaptation respects source's cultural context
- **Audit Questions:**
  1. Does source depict marginalized groups?
  2. How does source handle that depiction?
  3. What was acceptable when written vs. now?
  4. Can adaptation improve representation while honoring source?
- **Owen's Practitioner Ethics:**
  - Responsibility to source
  - Responsibility to subjects depicted
  - Responsibility to contemporary audience
- **Adaptation Strategies:**
  - **Faithful:** Keep problematic elements but frame critically
  - **Modernize:** Update attitudes to contemporary standards
  - **Contextualize:** Add framing that acknowledges issues
  - **Reimagine:** Change setting/characters to avoid problems
- **Seger's Principle:** "Truth to emotion" can justify changes
- **Quality Test:** Does adaptation cause harm to real people?

# LIBRARY 8: PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE TECHNIQUES

## 8.1 COLLABORATION TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Writers' Room Dynamics (Sacks/Gordon)
- **Sources:** Sacks (Poking a Dead Frog), Gordon
- **Function:** Optimize collaborative writing process
- **Room Roles:**
  1. **Room Runner:** Manages process, decides disputes
  2. **Story Breaker:** Focuses on structure/logic
  3. **Joke Writer:** Optimizes humor/dialogue
  4. **Character Voice:** Ensures character consistency
  5. **Contrarian:** Pressure-tests all ideas
- **Gordon's Boot Camp Collaborative Model:**
  - **Week 1-2:** Individual brainstorming
  - **Week 3-4:** Group pitch and selection
  - **Week 5-6:** Individual drafting
  - **Week 7-8:** Group rewrite sessions
  - **Week 9-12:** Rotation of leadership roles
- **Sacks's Room Etiquette:**
  - Build on others' ideas ("Yes, and...")
  - Separate idea from person (critique idea, not originator)
  - Fail publicly without shame (iteration = normal)
  - Preserve hierarchy while welcoming all voices

---

### Technique: Feedback Translation (Chitlik/Seger)
- **Sources:** Chitlik, Seger
- **Function:** Convert notes into actionable revisions
- **Chitlik's Note Categories:**
  1. **Diagnosis:** Note identifies problem accurately
  2. **Prescription:** Note suggests solution (may/may not work)
  3. **Symptom:** Note describes effect, not cause
- **Translation Process:**
  1. Collect all notes without responding
  2. Categorize: Diagnosis / Prescription / Symptom
  3. For symptoms: Trace to root cause
  4. For diagnoses: Accept and solve
  5. For prescriptions: Extract underlying concern, find own solution
- **Seger's Principle:** "All notes are data about audience reception"
- **Quality Markers:**
  - Multiple notes about same area = real problem
  - Isolated note from one person = possibly ignore
  - Note contradicts story's intent = clarify intent, not change

---

## 8.2 PROFESSIONAL DISCIPLINE TECHNIQUES

### Technique: Daily Page Quota (Gordon/Sacks)
- **Sources:** Gordon (Writers Boot Camp), Sacks
- **Function:** Maintain consistent output
- **Gordon's Boot Camp Standard:**
  - 2 pages minimum per day
  - 14 pages per week
  - Quality emerges through quantity
- **Sacks's Professional Habits:**
  - Fixed writing hours (not "when inspired")
  - Visible progress tracking (calendar X's)
  - Separation of writing and editing days
- **Execution Strategies:**
  - Morning pages: Write before other tasks
  - Time-boxing: Write for 90 minutes, stop regardless
  - Scene-completion: Finish current scene before stopping
- **Quality Over Perfection:** 2 rough pages better than 0 perfect pages

---

### Technique: Iterative Deadline System (Gordon/Chitlik)
- **Sources:** Gordon, Chitlik
- **Function:** Use deadlines as creative forcing function
- **Gordon's 12-Week Cycle:**
  - Week 4: Outline due
  - Week 8: First draft due
  - Week 12: Final draft due
  - Intermediate checkpoints create urgency
- **Chitlik's Seven-Pass Schedule:**
  - Pass 1-2: 1 week
