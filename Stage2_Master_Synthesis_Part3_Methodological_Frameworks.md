## OVERVIEW

Part 3 provides **practical decision-making frameworks** for combining the different theoretical approaches from your 24 source texts. Rather than treating each methodology as competing or mutually exclusive, this section shows how to **synthesize complementary strengths** while avoiding methodological conflicts.

**What This Section Accomplishes**:
- Identifies which methodologies work best together
- Provides decision frameworks for choosing approaches
- Shows how to combine structural systems (Field + Truby + McKee)
- Integrates comedy methodologies with drama structure
- Offers diagnostic troubleshooting using multiple lenses
- Creates adaptation-specific integration strategies

**Why Integration Matters**:
Writers often encounter contradictory advice when studying multiple texts. McKee emphasizes value shifts in every beat; Field focuses on plot points at specific pages; Truby insists on organic character web design; Vorhaus demands comic premise as foundation. These aren't competing truths—they're complementary perspectives that can be systematically integrated.

## SECTION 1: THE INTEGRATION CHALLENGE

### 1.1 Why Writers Struggle with Multiple Methodologies

**The Babel Tower Problem**: Each theorist creates their own vocabulary:
- Field's "plot points" ≈ McKee's "turning points" ≈ Truby's "revelation moments"
- Snyder's "Fun and Games" ≈ Field's "Act II obstacles" ≈ McKee's "progressive complications"
- Truby's "moral argument" ≈ McKee's "controlling idea" ≈ Snyder's "theme stated"

**The Contradictory Imperatives Problem**: Different systems emphasize different priorities:
- **Field**: Structure first → hit your plot points on exact pages
- **McKee**: Values first → every scene must turn positive/negative
- **Truby**: Character web first → design opposition matrix before plotting
- **Vorhaus**: Comic premise first → identify truth + pain before structure
- **Seger**: Diagnosis first → identify what's broken before redesigning

**The Authority Problem**: Each theorist presents their system as *the* way:
- McKee: "Story is about a character who wants something and is having difficulty getting it"
- Truby: "Story is about somebody who has a need and doesn't know it"
- Snyder: "Story is about the promise of the premise delivered in Fun and Games"

All three are right—they're describing different aspects of the same phenomenon.

### 1.2 The Resolution: Complementary Lenses Model

**Think of methodologies as diagnostic tools, not religious doctrines.**

Each system provides a different **diagnostic lens**:
- **Field's lens**: Structural integrity (pacing, act breaks, momentum)
- **McKee's lens**: Scene-level dynamics (value shifts, turning points)
- **Truby's lens**: Thematic coherence (moral argument, character web)
- **Snyder's lens**: Commercial viability (genre expectations, beat timing)
- **Seger's lens**: Problem diagnosis (what's broken, how to fix it)
- **Vorhaus's lens**: Comic mechanics (truth + pain, game of the scene)

**Integration Principle**: Use multiple lenses sequentially or in combination, depending on your project's needs and your current development stage.

## SECTION 2: STRUCTURAL SYNTHESIS FRAMEWORK
### Combining Field, Snyder, McKee, and Truby

### 2.1 The Four-Layer Structural Model

Think of story structure as having **four simultaneous layers**, each requiring different methodological attention:

**Layer 1: MACRO-STRUCTURE (Field + Snyder)**
- **What it governs**: Overall story shape, act breaks, major turning points
- **Time scale**: Entire screenplay (110-120 pages)
- **Primary tools**: Field's three-act paradigm, Snyder's 15-beat structure
- **Key questions**: 
  - Does Act I end around page 25-30 with a clear decision?
  - Does Act II have a midpoint reversal around page 55?
  - Does Act III begin around page 85-90 with final commitment?

**Layer 2: SEQUENCE STRUCTURE (Field + McKee)**
- **What it governs**: 8-10 page story units building to turning points
- **Time scale**: 8-15 page sequences (8-10 per screenplay)
- **Primary tools**: Field's sequence concept, McKee's scene sequence analysis
- **Key questions**:
  - Does each sequence have its own beginning, middle, end?
  - Does each sequence build tension toward a mini-climax?
  - Do sequences track rising stakes and complication?

**Layer 3: SCENE STRUCTURE (McKee + Truby)**
- **What it governs**: Individual scene construction and value shifts
- **Time scale**: 2-5 page scenes (40-60 per screenplay)
- **Primary tools**: McKee's value-shift analysis, Truby's revelation moments
- **Key questions**:
  - Does the scene open with one value charge and close with opposite?
  - Does conflict emerge through action, not exposition?
  - Does the scene advance plot AND reveal character?

**Layer 4: BEAT STRUCTURE (McKee + Vorhaus)**
- **What it governs**: Moment-to-moment action/reaction within scenes
- **Time scale**: 3-8 lines of dialogue or action (5-10 beats per scene)
- **Primary tools**: McKee's beat-by-beat analysis, Vorhaus's comic rhythm
- **Key questions**:
  - Does each beat change something (action, knowledge, emotion)?
  - Do beats build through conflict rather than exposition?
  - Is there rhythm variation (fast/slow, tense/release)?

### 2.2 How to Use the Four-Layer Model

**PHASE 1: Planning (Use Field + Truby + Snyder)**

**Step 1**: Identify your **premise** (Truby's moral contradiction)
- What value conflict drives your story?
- Example: *The Big Sick* - "Tradition vs. Authenticity"

**Step 2**: Map to **genre** (Snyder's 10 genres)
- Which genre engine fits your premise?
- Example: *The Big Sick* = "Buddy Love" (romantic comedy structure)

**Step 3**: Apply **beat sheet** (Snyder's 15 beats)
- Create initial structural roadmap hitting Snyder's 15 beats
- Don't worry about exact page counts yet—focus on story logic

**Step 4**: Design **character web** (Truby's opposition matrix)
- Who represents each pole of your value conflict?
- How do supporting characters test the protagonist's moral choice?

**Step 5**: Map **three-act structure** (Field's paradigm)
- Where does Act I end? (The protagonist commits to pursuit)
- Where's the midpoint? (False victory or false defeat)
- Where does Act II end? (Final commitment to transformation)

**PHASE 2: Drafting (Use McKee + Vorhaus)**

**For Each Sequence**:
1. **Define sequence goal** (McKee): What value does this sequence move from/to?
2. **Outline scenes** (McKee): How many scenes needed? Each with own value shift?
3. **If comedy, identify game** (Vorhaus): What's the comic premise of this sequence?

**For Each Scene**:
1. **Opening value** (McKee): Start with clear positive or negative charge
2. **Conflict through beats** (McKee): Build through action/reaction, not exposition
3. **Closing value** (McKee): End with opposite charge from opening
4. **If comedy, identify truth + pain** (Vorhaus): What human truth is being exaggerated?

**PHASE 3: Revision (Use Seger + McKee + Truby)**

**Macro Revision** (Seger's diagnostic approach):
1. **Test act breaks** (Field): Do they occur at right moments with sufficient force?
2. **Test controlling idea** (McKee): Can you state your story's meaning in one sentence?
3. **Test moral argument** (Truby): Do all characters serve the thematic exploration?
4. **Test genre delivery** (Snyder): Does "Fun and Games" deliver the premise promise?

**Scene Revision** (McKee + Seger):
1. **Value shift audit**: Does every scene turn positive↔negative?
2. **Purpose audit** (Seger): Does scene have single clear purpose or multiple competing?
3. **Conflict audit**: Is conflict present in action or only in dialogue?

### 2.3 Integration Example: *Get Out* (2017)

**How Jordan Peele Used Multiple Methodologies**:

**Field's Structure**:
- **Act I** (0-25 min): Setup of Chris/Rose relationship, tension with family visit
- **Plot Point 1** (≈25 min): Arrival at Armitage house, "no go back" moment
- **Act II A** (25-55 min): Escalating strangeness, hypnosis, party discomfort
- **Midpoint** (≈55 min): "Get out!" warning from Georgina triggers investigation
- **Act II B** (55-85 min): Discovery of photos, auction reveal, paralysis attempt
- **Plot Point 2** (≈85 min): Chris trapped, learns full horror of transplant plan
- **Act III** (85-104 min): Escape, confrontation, survival

**Snyder's Beats**:
- **Opening Image**: Chris packing, subtle anxiety about meeting white girlfriend's parents
- **Theme Stated**: "They don't know I'm Black?" / "Does that matter?"
- **Fun and Games**: The promise of social horror—microaggressions, Coagula cult hints
- **Midpoint**: "Get out!" crystallizes from subtext to text
- **All Is Lost**: Chris sedated, paralyzed, trapped in Sunken Place
- **Dark Night**: "You're one of the good ones" speech—full horror revealed

**McKee's Values**:
- **Central value**: Freedom ↔ Enslavement (literal body theft as ultimate colonization)
- **Scene-level shifts**: Every interaction turns from surface courtesy to underlying menace
- **Progressive complications**: Each "comfort" revealed as trap

**Truby's Moral Argument**:
- **Premise**: "Liberal racism is as dangerous as overt racism because it denies Black autonomy"
- **Need**: Chris needs to trust his instincts about racial threat
- **Revelation**: Rose's betrayal forces recognition that "color-blind" ideology is predatory
- **Opponent**: Not external villain but internalized self-doubt ("Am I being paranoid?")

**Result**: One of the most structurally sophisticated horror-comedies in cinema, working simultaneously on four methodological levels.

## SECTION 3: COMEDY INTEGRATION FRAMEWORK
### Combining Vorhaus, Holloway, Blake, and Rosenfield with Drama Structure

### 3.1 The Comedy-Drama Hybrid Challenge

**The Core Problem**: Comedy and drama have seemingly opposite structural requirements:

**Drama Requirements** (McKee, Field, Truby):
- **Escalating stakes**: Each complication must be worse than the last
- **Emotional investment**: Audience must care deeply about outcome
- **Cathartic transformation**: Protagonist fundamentally changes
- **Causal logic**: Every event follows from character choice

**Comedy Requirements** (Vorhaus, Holloway, Blake):
- **Emotional distance**: Too much investment kills laughter
- **Pattern repetition**: Comedy requires predictable rhythms
- **Exaggeration over realism**: Logic must bend for comic effect
- **Happy restoration**: Characters often remain lovably flawed

**The Integration Question**: How do you maintain dramatic stakes while preserving comic distance?

### 3.2 The Three Comedy Integration Models

**MODEL 1: COMEDY-DOMINANT (Vorhaus + Field)**
*Used for: Pure comedies, farce, romantic comedies*

**Structure**:
- **Act I**: Establish comic premise (Vorhaus) within setup (Field)
- **Act II**: Deliver "game of the scene" variations with escalation
- **Act III**: Restore order with minimal transformation

**Key Principle**: Comic premise > character arc
- The situation is more important than character growth
- Escalation comes from situation complication, not character crisis
- Resolution restores equilibrium rather than transforming protagonist

**Integration Technique**:
1. **Use Field for macro-pacing**: Hit act breaks at standard pages
2. **Use Vorhaus for scene design**: Each scene explores comic premise variation
3. **Use Snyder's "Fun and Games"**: Let Act II be premise exploration, not crisis
4. **Minimize McKee's value shifts**: Don't turn every scene—let comedy breathe

**Example**: *Groundhog Day*
- **Comic premise** (Vorhaus): Narcissist forced to repeat day until he learns empathy
- **Field structure**: Clean three-act breaks (Loop begins, Rita becomes focus, Escape achieved)
- **Snyder's Fun and Games**: Act II is pure premise variation (suicide attempts, seduction attempts, self-improvement)
- **Limited McKee values**: Scenes don't all turn—many just riff on premise

**MODEL 2: DRAMA-DOMINANT (McKee + Vorhaus)**
*Used for: Dramedies, dark comedies, comedy-dramas*

**Structure**:
- **Act I**: Establish dramatic stakes with comic relief
- **Act II**: Build dramatic tension with comic releases
- **Act III**: Deliver cathartic transformation with comic perspective

**Key Principle**: Character arc > comic premise
- Character transformation is primary; comedy provides tonal relief
- Escalation comes from character crisis with comic coping mechanisms
- Resolution transforms protagonist while maintaining comic worldview

**Integration Technique**:
1. **Use McKee for scene design**: Every scene must turn values
2. **Use Vorhaus for comic releases**: Insert comic beats to prevent melodrama
3. **Use Truby for moral argument**: Comedy serves thematic exploration
4. **Apply Field's structure**: Standard dramatic act breaks with comic flavor

**Example**: *Little Miss Sunshine*
- **Dramatic premise**: Family's dysfunction threatens to destroy them
- **Comic premise** (Vorhaus): Absurdist beauty pageant as pressure test
- **McKee structure**: Every scene turns family dynamics from hope↔despair
- **Truby's moral argument**: "Success" culture vs. authentic connection
- **Comedy's function**: Provides distance from painful family truths

**MODEL 3: ALTERNATING MODES (McKee + Vorhaus + Seger)**
*Used for: Genre hybrids, tonal shifts, sophisticated comedies*

**Structure**:
- **Act I**: Establish tonal hybrid (comedy + horror, comedy + thriller)
- **Act II**: Alternate between comic and dramatic modes
- **Act III**: Synthesize both modes for unique resolution

**Key Principle**: Mode switching > consistency
- Genre expectations create comic/dramatic oscillation
- Escalation comes from genre collision (comedy makes horror worse)
- Resolution satisfies both genre requirements simultaneously

**Integration Technique**:
1. **Map genre requirements** (Snyder): Identify both genres' expectations
2. **Design scene sequences** (McKee): Alternate comic/dramatic values
3. **Use Seger's scene purpose audit**: Each scene clear in its mode
4. **Calibrate comic distance** (Vorhaus): Adjust distance as stakes rise

**Example**: *Get Out*
- **Horror structure** (McKee): Every scene turns from safety→danger
- **Comedy structure** (Vorhaus): Social satire and code-switching humor
- **Genre hybrid**: Comedy in Act I creates distance for Act III horror
- **Tonal calibration**: Comedy fades as horror escalates (planned shift)

### 3.3 Comedy Integration Decision Tree

**START: What's your primary goal?**

**If PURE LAUGHS are primary:**
→ Use **Comedy-Dominant Model**
→ Vorhaus + Field + Snyder
→ Emphasize premise variation over transformation
→ Maintain emotional distance throughout
→ Examples: *Anchorman*, *Airplane!*, *The Hangover*

**If EMOTIONAL CATHARSIS is primary:**
→ Use **Drama-Dominant Model**
→ McKee + Truby + Field (with Vorhaus for relief)
→ Emphasize character arc over premise
→ Build empathy despite comic elements
→ Examples: *The Big Sick*, *Manchester by the Sea*, *Little Miss Sunshine*

**If GENRE SYNTHESIS is primary:**
→ Use **Alternating Modes Model**
→ McKee + Vorhaus + Seger (for mode management)
→ Design tonal oscillation strategically
→ Satisfy multiple genre expectations
→ Examples: *Get Out*, *Sorry to Bother You*, *Parasite*

**DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION 1**: Can the protagonist fail and still get laughs?
- **Yes** → Comedy-Dominant (failure is funny)
- **No** → Drama-Dominant (failure is painful)

**DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION 2**: Does the premise exhaust itself or deepen?
- **Exhausts** → Comedy-Dominant (variations on premise)
- **Deepens** → Drama-Dominant (premise reveals character)

**DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION 3**: What's your target emotional endpoint?
- **Restored order, lightness** → Comedy-Dominant
- **Transformation, growth** → Drama-Dominant
- **Synthesis, catharsis through comedy** → Alternating Modes

### 3.4 Practical Comedy Integration Techniques

**TECHNIQUE 1: The Vorhaus-McKee Hybrid Beat**
*For scenes that need both comic energy and dramatic momentum*

**Structure**:
1. **Opening value** (McKee): Establish positive or negative charge
2. **Comic complication** (Vorhaus): Introduce exaggerated obstacle
3. **Action through beats** (McKee): Characters pursue goal through conflict
4. **Comic escalation** (Vorhaus): Each beat heightens the absurdity
5. **Value reversal** (McKee): Scene ends with opposite charge
6. **Comic release** (Vorhaus): Final beat provides laugh *and* story turn

**Example**: *Bridesmaids* airplane scene
- **Opening value**: Hope (Annie will impress Rhodes)
- **Comic complication**: Annie gets drunk/drugged
- **Action beats**: Escalating inappropriate behavior
- **Comic escalation**: Each action worse than last (singing, dancing, fighting)
- **Value reversal**: Disaster (plane makes emergency landing)
- **Comic release**: Annie's "I'm ready to party!" as cops board (laugh + story turn)

**TECHNIQUE 2: The Truby-Vorhaus Character Web**
*For ensemble comedies with thematic depth*

**Integration**:
1. **Identify moral argument** (Truby): What value conflict drives the story?
2. **Design character web** (Truby): Each character represents different value position
3. **Assign comic attitudes** (Vorhaus): Each character has distinct worldview filter
4. **Create opposition matrix** (Truby): Characters' goals conflict
5. **Design comic collisions** (Vorhaus): Attitudes clash for comedy
6. **Track moral evolution** (Truby): Comedy serves thematic exploration

**Example**: *The Office* (US)
- **Moral argument**: Corporate efficiency vs. human connection
- **Character web**: 
  - Michael (connection through approval-seeking)
  - Dwight (efficiency through rigid rules)
  - Jim (detachment through irony)
  - Pam (connection through authenticity)
- **Comic attitudes**: Each character's worldview creates automatic comedy
- **Opposition matrix**: Goals constantly clash
- **Comic collisions**: Attitude differences generate office conflicts
- **Moral evolution**: Characters move toward integrated positions

**TECHNIQUE 3: The Field-Vorhaus Escalation Ladder**
*For maintaining comic energy while building stakes*

**Structure**:
1. **Establish baseline** (Act I): Normal level of comic consequence
2. **First escalation** (Act II A): Raise stakes but maintain comic distance
3. **Midpoint crisis** (Act II midpoint): Biggest laugh *and* highest stakes
4. **Second escalation** (Act II B): Comedy fades as stakes become real
5. **Crisis point** (Act III): Minimal comedy, maximum stakes
6. **Resolution** (Act III): Comedy returns with new equilibrium

**Example**: *Borat*
- **Baseline** (Act I): Innocent foreigner makes cultural mistakes (safe distance)
- **First escalation** (Act II A): Mistakes become more offensive (still funny)
- **Midpoint**: Rodeo speech and nude hotel fight (peak comedy + growing stakes)
- **Second escalation** (Act II B): Real consequences emerge (less funny)
- **Crisis**: Pamela Anderson confrontation (stakes highest, comedy lowest)
- **Resolution**: Return to Kazakhstan (comedy restored, lesson learned)

## SECTION 4: CHARACTER DEVELOPMENT SYNTHESIS
### Combining McKee, Truby, Seger, and Field

### 4.1 The Four Character Dimensions

Different methodologies emphasize different aspects of character. Effective integration requires attending to all four:

**DIMENSION 1: WANT (Field + McKee + Snyder)**
- **What the character pursues consciously**
- **External, visible goal**
- **Drives plot forward**
- **What**: McKee calls this "conscious desire"
- **Why it matters**: Without clear want, audience has no rooting interest

**DIMENSION 2: NEED (Truby + McKee)**
- **What the character actually requires for fulfillment**
- **Internal, often unconscious**
- **Drives character arc**
- **What**: Truby calls this "psychological need"; McKee calls it "unconscious desire"
- **Why it matters**: Want without need = plot without transformation

**DIMENSION 3: FLAW (Snyder + Seger + Truby)**
- **What prevents character from achieving need**
- **Behavioral or psychological weakness**
- **Must be overcome for growth**
- **What**: Snyder calls this "Six Things That Need Fixing"; Seger calls it "fatal flaw"
- **Why it matters**: Flaw creates internal obstacle making external goal difficult

**DIMENSION 4: GHOST (Truby + Seger)**
- **Past event that created the flaw**
- **Wound that shaped current behavior**
- **Often revealed at midpoint or crisis**
- **What**: Truby's "ghost" = Seger's "backstory wound"
- **Why it matters**: Provides causal logic for current behavior

### 4.2 The Integrated Character Development System

**STEP 1: Define the Want-Need Gap (McKee + Truby)**

**The Gap Principle**: The distance between what character wants and what they need generates the character arc.

**Small Gap** = Minimal transformation:
- Want: "Win the race"
- Need: "Believe in myself"
- Example: Most sports movies (inspirational, not transformative)

**Large Gap** = Major transformation:
- Want: "Get rich"
- Need: "Find authentic connection"
- Example: *Jerry Maguire*, *The Wolf of Wall Street*, *Trading Places*

**Opposite Gap** = Ironic transformation:
- Want: "Destroy my enemy"
- Need: "Forgive my enemy"
- Example: *Unforgiven*, *Gran Torino*, *Three Billboards*

**Integration Technique**:
1. **State the want clearly** (Field): What does character pursue?
2. **Identify the need** (Truby): What would truly fulfill them?
3. **Measure the gap** (McKee): How far apart are want and need?
4. **Design revelation moments** (Truby): When does character glimpse the need?
5. **Create crisis choice** (McKee): Force choice between want and need

**STEP 2: Design the Flaw Architecture (Snyder + Seger + Truby)**

**Snyder's "Six Things That Need Fixing"**:
- Not just *one* flaw, but a *cluster* of related issues
- Creates multi-dimensional character quickly
- Each "thing" can be addressed separately in different scenes

**Seger's Fatal Flaw Principle**:
- One flaw must be *central* to the character's inability to achieve their goal
- Supporting flaws orbit around this central flaw
- Fatal flaw must be *addressed* (not necessarily fixed) for story resolution

**Truby's Flaw-Need Connection**:
- Flaw = behavioral manifestation of unmet need
- Need = what would cure the flaw
- Character unconsciously protects the flaw to avoid facing the need

**Integration Example**: *The 40-Year-Old Virgin*

**Want**: Have sex (lose virginity)

**Need**: Authentic intimate connection (not just physical)

**Central Flaw**: Avoidance of vulnerability through collection/isolation

**Supporting Flaws**:
1. Social awkwardness (can't talk to women naturally)
2. Obsessive hobbies (uses collections as emotional armor)
3. Risk-aversion (afraid to put himself out there)
4. Romanticization (puts women on pedestals)
5. Self-protection (uses humor to deflect intimacy)
6. Inexperience (lack of practice makes failure self-fulfilling)

**Flaw-Need Connection**: Andy collects toys because he's afraid of messy human connection. Overcoming flaw (selling collection) enables need (authentic relationship with Trish).

**STEP 3: Build the Character Web (Truby + McKee)**

**Truby's Character Web**: All characters represent different approaches to the story's central value conflict.

**Integration with McKee**: Each character should represent different position on the controlling idea's value axis.

**Character Web Design Process**:

1. **Identify controlling idea** (McKee): "X value charges positive/negative because Y"
   - Example (*Breaking Bad*): "Power corrupts when used to protect ego"

2. **Map value positions** (Truby): Create spectrum of attitudes toward that value
   - Far Positive: Jesse (power through authentic connection)
   - Moderate Positive: Hank (power through rule of law)
   - Neutral: Skyler (power situationally, conflicted)
   - Moderate Negative: Saul (power through corruption, survival)
   - Far Negative: Gus (power through ruthless control)

3. **Assign methods** (Truby): How does each character pursue their version?
   - Walt: Power through chemistry expertise + deception
   - Jesse: Power through loyalty + emotion
   - Gus: Power through discipline + terror
   - Hank: Power through justice + persistence

4. **Design conflicts** (Truby + McKee): Each character relationship tests protagonist
   - Walt vs. Jesse: Manipulation vs. authentic connection
   - Walt vs. Skyler: Deception vs. honesty
   - Walt vs. Hank: Criminal vs. law
   - Walt vs. Gus: Amateur vs. professional

5. **Create moral pressure** (Truby): Supporting characters force protagonist to choose
   - Every character interaction should push protagonist toward their need
   - Protagonist resists, defending their flaw
   - Escalating pressure eventually forces transformation or destruction

**STEP 4: Map the Transformation Arc (McKee + Truby + Seger)**

**The Change or Not-Change Question**: Does your protagonist transform or maintain their essential nature?

**Change Arc** (Truby's "new equilibrium"):
- **Act I**: Establish flaw and ghost
- **Act II A**: External pursuit exposes flaw
- **Midpoint**: Glimpse of need (false victory or defeat)
- **Act II B**: Flaw prevents success; crisis looms
- **Crisis**: Choose between want and need
- **Climax**: Act on need, abandon want (or vice versa)
- **Resolution**: New equilibrium with flaw addressed

**Static Arc** (McKee's "world must change"):
- **Act I**: Establish character's essential nature
- **Act II A**: World challenges that nature
- **Midpoint**: Character refuses to change
- **Act II B**: World escalates pressure
- **Crisis**: Character faces ultimate test
- **Climax**: Character remains true; world changes around them
- **Resolution**: Character vindicated or destroyed, but unchanged

**Integration Technique**:

**For Change Arcs** (Use Truby + Seger):
1. **Define clear need** (Truby): What must character learn?
2. **Design revelation cascade** (Truby): What insights lead to change?
3. **Apply Seger's transformation test**: Can you show before/after difference visually?

**For Static Arcs** (Use McKee + Seger):
1. **Define core principle** (McKee): What does character believe that won't change?
2. **Test principle repeatedly** (McKee): Each scene challenges that belief
3. **Show consistent behavior** (Seger): Character makes same choice each time

### 4.3 Character Integration Example: *Paddington 2*

**Demonstrates perfect synthesis of all four methodologies:**

**WANT (Field/McKee)**: Buy pop-up book for Aunt Lucy's birthday

**NEED (Truby)**: Show that kindness matters even when unrewarded

**FLAW (Snyder)**: Naive optimism (sees good in everyone, vulnerable to exploitation)

**GHOST (Truby)**: Aunt Lucy's teaching ("If we're kind and polite, the world will be right")

**CHARACTER WEB (Truby):**
- **Paddington**: Kindness as principle
- **Phoenix Buchanan**: Narcissism disguised as charm
- **Mr. Curry**: Cynicism and suspicion
- **Brown Family**: Kindness with boundaries
- **Prisoners**: Lost goodness waiting to be awakened

**CONTROLLING IDEA (McKee)**: "Kindness triumphs because it transforms communities"

**TRANSFORMATION ARC (Integrated)**:

**Act I**: 
- Paddington's kindness establishes character (want) but makes him vulnerable (flaw)
- Ghost established (Aunt Lucy's teaching)
- Inciting incident: Pop-up book appears (want)

**Act II A**:
- Paddington's pursuit of want (odd jobs to buy book)
- Flaw tested (various mishaps from naive trust)
- Phoenix exploits flaw (frames Paddington)

**Midpoint**:
- False defeat: Paddington imprisoned
- Glimpse of need: Prison community begins to transform through his kindness

**Act II B**:
- Want seems lost (book stolen, Paddington in jail)
- Need becomes visible (prisoners becoming better people)
- Flaw reaches crisis (will Paddington become cynical?)

**Crisis**:
- Choice: Maintain kindness despite injustice, or become bitter
- Paddington chooses principle over self-interest (escapes to clear name, not flee)

**Climax**:
- Kindness proves itself (community rallies to save him)
- Phoenix defeated by own narcissism
- Controlling idea proven: kindness transforms communities

**Resolution**:
- Want achieved differently than expected (pop-up book comes to him)
- Need fulfilled (entire London demonstrates kindness matters)
- Flaw revealed as strength (naive optimism changed the world)
- New equilibrium: Paddington vindicated, community transformed

**Why This Works**: Perfect balance of Field's structure, McKee's values, Truby's moral argument, and Snyder's character arc beats.

## SECTION 5: GENRE DECISION FRAMEWORK
### When to Use Which Methodology

### 5.1 The Genre-Methodology Compatibility Matrix

Different story types require different methodological emphasis:

| **GENRE** | **PRIMARY METHOD** | **SECONDARY METHOD** | **TERTIARY METHOD** | **RATIONALE** |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|-------------------|--------------|
| **Action/Thriller** | Field + Snyder | McKee (scenes) | Truby (antagonist) | Structure and pacing paramount; less character depth required |
| **Drama** | McKee + Truby | Field (structure) | Seger (revision) | Character transformation and moral argument central |
| **Comedy** | Vorhaus + Snyder | Field (structure) | McKee (some scenes) | Comic premise must drive; structure provides shape but flexibility needed |
| **Romance** | Snyder + McKee | Truby (relationship web) | Field (pacing) | Beat sheet ensures emotional milestones; dual transformation arcs |
| **Horror** | McKee + Field | Snyder (beats) | Truby (theme) | Value shifts create terror; structure controls pacing |
| **Mystery** | Field + McKee | Truby (revelation design) | Snyder (beats) | Plot points = clues; scenes reveal information strategically |
| **Sci-Fi/Fantasy** | Field + Truby | McKee (values) | Snyder (genre expectations) | World-building requires structure; theme crucial for meaningful speculation |
| **Biopic** | McKee + Truby | Seger (shaping reality) | Field (compression) | Must find thematic through-line in real events; moral argument shapes selection |
| **Ensemble** | Truby + McKee | Field (structure) | Seger (balancing threads) | Character web design essential; value shifts track multiple arcs |
| **Adaptation** | Hutcheon + Owen | Seger (diagnosis) | McKee (medium-specific) | Understand source; diagnose what must change; execute medium translation |

### 5.2 Methodology Selection Decision Tree

**QUESTION 1: Is this primarily a PLOT story or a CHARACTER story?**

**PLOT-DRIVEN** (External conflict, action emphasis):
→ **Primary**: Field + Snyder (structure rules)
→ **Secondary**: McKee (scene dynamics)
→ **Approach**: Rigorous beat-sheet planning, external stakes, clear plot points
→ **Examples**: *Die Hard*, *The Fugitive*, *Mad Max: Fury Road*

**CHARACTER-DRIVEN** (Internal conflict, transformation emphasis):
→ **Primary**: Truby + McKee (character and values)
→ **Secondary**: Seger (diagnostic revision)
→ **Approach**: Design character web first, scenes driven by internal pressure
→ **Examples**: *Manchester by the Sea*, *Moonlight*, *Lady Bird*

**QUESTION 2: Does your story have a SINGLE protagonist or ENSEMBLE cast?**

**SINGLE PROTAGONIST**:
→ **Use**: Standard Field/McKee/Truby integration
→ **Focus**: Clear want-need gap, single transformation arc
→ **Structure**: Traditional three-act with protagonist in every sequence

**ENSEMBLE CAST**:
→ **Use**: Truby (character web) + McKee (multiple value lines)
→ **Challenge**: Track 3-7 character arcs simultaneously
→ **Structure**: Interwoven sequence design, multiple climaxes
→ **Technique**: Each character represents different value position

**QUESTION 3: Is the primary appeal GENRE SATISFACTION or GENRE SUBVERSION?**

**GENRE SATISFACTION** (Deliver expected pleasures):
→ **Use**: Snyder (beat sheet) + Field (structure)
→ **Approach**: Identify genre's "promise of the premise" and deliver it
→ **Example**: *John Wick* (action), *Knives Out* (mystery), *To All the Boys* (romance)

**GENRE SUBVERSION** (Challenge expectations):
→ **Use**: McKee (values) + Truby (moral argument) + Seger (diagnosis)
→ **Approach**: Understand genre deeply to subvert intelligently
→ **Example**: *Cabin in the Woods* (horror), *Deadpool* (superhero), *Get Out* (horror-comedy)

**QUESTION 4: Are you WRITING original or ADAPTING existing material?**

**ORIGINAL MATERIAL**:
→ **Use**: Any primary methodology (Field/McKee/Truby/Snyder)
→ **Freedom**: Choose system matching your creative process

**ADAPTATION**:
→ **Must Use**: Hutcheon (adaptation theory) + Owen (transcoding)
→ **Then Add**: Seger (diagnosis of what must change) + McKee (medium-specific craft)
→ **Critical**: Understand source's appeal before determining fidelity approach

### 5.3 Genre-Specific Integration Guides

**GUIDE 1: ACTION/THRILLER**

**Methodology Mix**: 60% Field/Snyder, 30% McKee, 10% Truby

**Why This Mix**:
- Action demands rigorous structure and pacing
- External stakes and clear plot points paramount
- Character can be simpler (archetypal is fine)
- Theme present but not foregrounded

**Planning Phase**:
1. **Snyder beat sheet**: Map all 15 beats with external action focus
2. **Field plot points**: Identify exact escalation moments
3. **McKee scenes**: Ensure each action sequence has beginning/middle/end with value shift
4. **Minimal Truby**: Simple character web (protagonist, antagonist, mentor, ally)

**Execution**:
- **Act I**: Establish hero's capability + inciting incident (clear stakes)
- **Act II**: Escalating action setpieces, each harder than last
- **Act III**: Final confrontation with clear winner/loser

**Common Mistakes**:
- ❌ Over-complicating character psychology (action audience wants clarity)
- ❌ Slow pacing to "develop character" (develop character through action)
- ❌ Unclear spatial geography in action scenes (McKee: location must be clear)

**Success Examples**: *Die Hard*, *The Raid*, *Mission: Impossible - Fallout*

**GUIDE 2: CHARACTER DRAMA**

**Methodology Mix**: 50% McKee/Truby, 30% Seger, 20% Field

**Why This Mix**:
- Internal transformation is the story
- Value shifts and moral argument drive everything
- Structure matters but serves character, not vice versa
- Revision crucial for finding the truth

**Planning Phase**:
1. **Truby character web**: Design comprehensive relationship matrix first
2. **McKee controlling idea**: Define clear thematic statement
3. **Truby's 22 steps**: Map character's internal journey
4. **Field structure**: Ensure three-act shape, but pages flexible

**Execution**:
- **Act I**: Establish wound/ghost and current stasis
- **Act II**: Pressure wound through escalating relationships
- **Act III**: Force choice between flaw and growth

**Common Mistakes**:
- ❌ Mistaking stasis for subtlety (character must still make choices)
- ❌ Forgetting external stakes (internal journey needs external pressure)
- ❌ Over-explaining psychology (show through behavior, not dialogue)

**Success Examples**: *Manchester by the Sea*, *Lady Bird*, *Moonlight*, *Roma*

**GUIDE 3: COMEDY**

**Methodology Mix**: 60% Vorhaus, 25% Snyder/Field, 15% McKee

**Why This Mix**:
- Comic premise and truth+pain drive everything
- Structure provides shape but can't kill laughs
- Some scenes don't need to "turn" (can just be funny)
- Genre expectations crucial (Snyder's genres)

**Planning Phase**:
1. **Vorhaus comic premise**: Identify truth + pain first
2. **Vorhaus game of the scene**: How will premise generate variations?
3. **Snyder beat sheet**: Map genre expectations (rom-com, farce, satire)
4. **Field structure**: Ensure act breaks exist but serve comedy

**Execution**:
- **Act I**: Establish comic world rules and premise
- **Act II**: "Fun and Games" - deliver premise variations
- **Act III**: Restore order with minimal "learning"

**Common Mistakes**:
- ❌ Forcing dramatic transformation (comedy often resists growth)
- ❌ Making every scene turn (kills rhythm, exhausts audience)
- ❌ Ignoring setup/payoff (comedy demands rigorous planting)

**Success Examples**: *Groundhog Day*, *The Big Sick*, *Bridesmaids*, *Game Night*

**GUIDE 4: ENSEMBLE DRAMA**

**Methodology Mix**: 50% Truby, 35% McKee, 15% Field

**Why This Mix**:
- Character web design is essential infrastructure
- Multiple value lines must be tracked simultaneously
- Structure needs to serve multiple arcs without losing coherence
- Theme must unify disparate storylines

**Planning Phase**:
1. **Truby character web**: Map all relationships and value positions
2. **McKee controlling idea**: Define unifying thematic statement
3. **McKee value lines**: Track 3-7 simultaneous character arcs
4. **Field sequences**: Design interwoven story threads

**Execution**:
- **Act I**: Introduce all major characters, establish web
- **Act II**: Alternate focus, showing how characters intersect and pressure each other
- **Act III**: Multiple climaxes converge on unified theme

**Common Mistakes**:
- ❌ Treating storylines as separate (they must interconnect)
- ❌ Equal screen time for all (some characters should be more prominent)
- ❌ Losing thematic coherence (every thread must serve controlling idea)

**Success Examples**: *Magnolia*, *Short Cuts*, *Crash*, *Babel*, *The Big Chill*

## SECTION 6: DIAGNOSTIC TROUBLESHOOTING
### Using Seger's Approach with Multiple Methodologies

### 6.1 The Diagnostic Philosophy

Linda Seger's approach is uniquely valuable because it's **problem-focused rather than theory-focused**. Where other methodologies say "here's how to build a story," Seger says "here's how to fix what's broken."

**The Integration Advantage**: Once you've diagnosed the problem using Seger's framework, you can apply the appropriate methodology to fix it.

### 6.2 Seger's Diagnostic Categories Mapped to Methodologies

**CATEGORY 1: STRUCTURAL PROBLEMS**

**Seger's Diagnosis**: "The story sags in the middle" / "Act breaks are soft" / "Pacing feels off"

**Apply These Methodologies**:
1. **Field**: Check plot point placement and strength
   - Is Plot Point 1 around page 25-30 with sufficient force?
   - Is there a clear midpoint reversal around page 55?
   - Does Plot Point 2 around page 85-90 drive toward climax?

2. **Snyder**: Audit beat sheet compliance
   - Does "Fun and Games" deliver the premise promise (pages 30-55)?
   - Is there an "All Is Lost" moment (page 75-85)?
   - Does "Break into Three" provide clear final act direction?

3. **McKee**: Check sequence construction
   - Does each 8-10 page sequence have its own mini-arc?
   - Are sequences building toward climaxes?
   - Is there escalation between sequences?

**CATEGORY 2: CHARACTER PROBLEMS**

**Seger's Diagnosis**: "Protagonist isn't likable" / "Character change feels unearned" / "Unclear what character wants"

**Apply These Methodologies**:
1. **Snyder**: Evaluate "Save the Cat" moment
   - Does opening establish rooting interest?
   - Are "Six Things That Need Fixing" clear?
   - Is character's want stated explicitly?

2. **Truby**: Audit character web design
   - Is the need (psychological requirement) clear?
   - Does the ghost (wound) explain current behavior?
   - Do supporting characters represent different value positions?

3. **McKee**: Check want-need gap
   - Is there sufficient distance between conscious desire and unconscious need?
   - Does the gap create meaningful tension?
   - Does crisis force choice between want and need?

**CATEGORY 3: SCENE PROBLEMS**

**Seger's Diagnosis**: "Scenes feel talky" / "Nothing happens" / "Unclear what scene accomplishes"

**Apply These Methodologies**:
1. **Seger's Scene Purpose Audit**:
   - Does scene have one clear purpose or multiple competing purposes?
   - Is purpose accomplished through action or just dialogue?
   - Can scene be cut without losing story information?

2. **McKee's Value Shift Test**:
   - Does scene open with positive or negative charge?
   - Does it close with opposite charge?
   - If not, why does this scene exist?

3. **Field's Sequence Test**:
   - Does scene belong in current sequence or should it move?
   - Does scene build toward sequence's mini-climax?
   - Is scene properly positioned for pacing?

**CATEGORY 4: DIALOGUE PROBLEMS**

**Seger's Diagnosis**: "Dialogue is on-the-nose" / "Characters sound same" / "Too much exposition"

**Apply These Methodologies**:
1. **McKee's Subtext Principle**:
   - Is there gap between what's said and what's meant?
   - Are characters pursuing goals through dialogue or just explaining?
   - Does each character want something from the other?

2. **Truby's Character Voice**:
   - Does each character's value position create distinct speech patterns?
   - Are contradictions revealing character complexity?
   - Is dialogue serving moral argument?

3. **Vorhaus's Comic Dialogue** (if comedy):
   - Is there attitude clash creating humor?
   - Are characters using misdirection rather than direct statement?
   - Is timing (beats, pauses) marked?

**CATEGORY 5: THEMATIC PROBLEMS**

**Seger's Diagnosis**: "Theme feels preachy" / "Unclear what story is about" / "Theme and plot disconnect"

**Apply These Methodologies**:
1. **McKee's Controlling Idea**:
   - Can you state story's meaning in one sentence (value + cause)?
   - Does climax prove this controlling idea?
   - Are all elements serving this idea?

2. **Truby's Moral Argument**:
   - Is there clear value conflict (virtue vs. virtue)?
   - Do characters represent different positions on this conflict?
   - Does protagonist's journey test these positions?

3. **Field's Integration Test**:
   - Does theme emerge from action or is it imposed through dialogue?
   - Are plot events naturally revealing thematic point?
   - Does resolution embody theme without explaining it?

### 6.3 The Seger Diagnostic Workflow

**PHASE 1: IDENTIFY THE PROBLEM CATEGORY**

Read your draft and identify which category feels weakest:
- [ ] Structure (pacing, act breaks, momentum)
- [ ] Character (likability, arc, want/need)
- [ ] Scene (purpose, conflict, progression)
- [ ] Dialogue (subtext, voice, exposition)
- [ ] Theme (clarity, integration, expression)

**PHASE 2: APPLY APPROPRIATE METHODOLOGY**

Based on category, choose diagnostic tool:
- **Structure** → Field + Snyder
- **Character** → Truby + McKee + Snyder
- **Scene** → McKee + Seger
- **Dialogue** → McKee + Truby + Vorhaus
- **Theme** → McKee + Truby

**PHASE 3: DIAGNOSE SPECIFIC ISSUE**

Use methodology's tools to pinpoint exact problem:
- Where specifically does problem occur? (page numbers, scenes)
- What is the story element supposed to accomplish?
- Why isn't it working currently?
- What would success look like?

**PHASE 4: PRESCRIPTION**

Apply methodology's principles to fix:
- What needs to be added?
- What needs to be removed?
- What needs to be moved?
- What needs to be strengthened?

**PHASE 5: TARGETED REWRITE**

Execute the fix:
- Rewrite only the problematic element
- Test against diagnostic criteria
- Check for ripple effects on connected elements
- Validate that problem is resolved

### 6.4 Diagnostic Example: *Juno* Script Development

**INITIAL PROBLEM** (from actual development notes):
"Juno's character feels too smart-alecky and alienating. Hard to root for her despite being pregnant teen."

**SEGER DIAGNOSIS**: Character problem - likability issue

**APPLIED METHODOLOGIES**:

**Snyder's "Save the Cat"**:
- **Diagnosis**: No early moment establishing Juno's goodness beneath the snark
- **Prescription**: Add humanizing moment in Act I
- **Solution**: Juno agrees to give baby to Vanessa despite own pain (selfless choice)

**Truby's Character Need**:
- **Diagnosis**: Want (handle pregnancy) was clear, but need (grow up, accept maturity) underdeveloped
- **Prescription**: Strengthen ghost (childhood ending) and revelation moments
- **Solution**: Enhanced relationship with dad showing fear of losing childhood

**McKee's Value Shifts**:
- **Diagnosis**: Scenes with Mark were charming but didn't turn values (stasis)
- **Prescription**: Each Mark scene must shift Juno's view of adulthood
- **Solution**: Mark scenes now progress from "adults are cool" → "adults are confused" → "adulthood is scary"

**RESULT**: 
- Character maintained distinctive voice (snark preserved)
- Added emotional accessibility (humanizing beats)
- Clarified transformation arc (childhood → maturity)
- Maintained comedy while building empathy

## SECTION 7: ADAPTATION-SPECIFIC INTEGRATION
### Hutcheon + Owen + McKee + Seger

### 7.1 The Adaptation Challenge

Adaptation requires unique methodological integration because you're solving two simultaneous problems:
1. **Understanding the source material** (what makes it work in its original medium?)
2. **Translating to new medium** (how to preserve essential while transforming form?)

### 7.2 The Hutcheon-Owen-McKee Integration Model

**PHASE 1: SOURCE ANALYSIS (Hutcheon)**

**Hutcheon's Three-Lens Analysis**:

1. **As Product**: What is this text/film/performance?
   - Genre, structure, style
   - Key scenes, characters, themes
   - What makes it distinctive?

2. **As Process**: How was this created?
   - Author's intent, historical context
   - Medium-specific affordances
   - Production constraints

3. **As Reception**: How is this experienced?
   - Audience expectations
   - Cultural knowledge required
   - Emotional/intellectual engagement

**Application to Adaptation**:
- What elements does audience *expect* to see in adaptation?
- What elements are *essential* to the experience?
- What elements are *medium-specific* and must transform?

**PHASE 2: FIDELITY DECISION (Owen)**

**Owen's Three Adaptation Archetypes**:

**Archetype 1: The Transposition** (Literal fidelity)
- **Goal**: Reproduce source as closely as possible
- **When to use**: Source is beloved, visual, already cinematic
- **Risk**: May feel stagey or reverential
- **Examples**: *Harry Potter* early films, *The Lord of the Rings*

**Archetype 2: The Commentary** (Critical fidelity)
- **Goal**: Engage with source while offering perspective
- **When to use**: Source has cultural significance worth examining
- **Risk**: May alienate purists
- **Examples**: *Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet*, *Adaptation*, *The Handmaid's Tale* (series)

**Archetype 3: The Analogy** (Thematic fidelity)
- **Goal**: Preserve core theme/spirit, transform specifics
- **When to use**: Source structure won't translate, but theme resonates
- **Risk**: May feel disconnected from source
- **Examples**: *Apocalypse Now* (from *Heart of Darkness*), *O Brother Where Art Thou* (from *The Odyssey*)

**Decision Framework**:
1. **Assess source's cultural status**: Beloved classic, genre, or cult?
2. **Identify essential elements**: What must be preserved?
3. **Choose fidelity approach**: Transposition, Commentary, or Analogy?

**PHASE 3: TRANSCODING (McKee + Owen)**

**McKee's Medium-Specific Craft**:

**Problem**: Novels have interiority; films have exteriority
- **Novel strength**: Direct access to thoughts, extensive backstory
- **Film strength**: Visual action, present-moment behavior

**Transcoding Strategies**:

1. **Internality → Visuality**
   - **Novel**: "She thought about her mother's warning"
   - **Film**: Character fingers necklace (object = memory trigger)
   - **Technique**: Use objects, gestures, environments to externalize thought

2. **Past → Present**
   - **Novel**: "She had always feared intimacy after her divorce"
   - **Film**: Show fear *now* through behavior in relationship scene
   - **Technique**: Demonstrate causality through current action, not exposition

3. **Description → Action**
   - **Novel**: "He was a controlling, narcissistic man"
   - **Film**: Show him correcting waiter, criticizing date's order, checking phone
   - **Technique**: Translate adjectives into verbs

4. **Summary → Scene**
   - **Novel**: "Over the next months, they fell in love"
   - **Film**: Select 3-4 specific moments that dramatize falling in love
   - **Technique**: Use McKee's scene construction for each moment

**PHASE 4: COMPRESSION (Field + Seger)**

**The Compression Problem**: Most novels are too long for film (3-hour reading time vs. 120-page screenplay).

**Field's Structural Compression**:
1. **Identify essential plot points**: What turns must exist for story to work?
2. **Combine characters**: Merge supporting characters serving similar functions
3. **Condense sequences**: Multiple source scenes → one film scene
4. **Cut subplots**: Eliminate threads that don't serve controlling idea

**Seger's Diagnostic Compression**:
1. **Scene purpose audit**: Does each scene accomplish one clear goal?
2. **Character function audit**: Does each character serve unique function?
3. **Thematic focus audit**: Does each element serve controlling idea?

**Integration Example**: *No Country for Old Men* (Coen Brothers)

**Source**: Cormac McCarthy novel (300+ pages)
**Adaptation**: 122-page screenplay

**Hutcheon Analysis**:
- **As Product**: Literary thriller + philosophical meditation on violence and fate
- **As Process**: McCarthy exploring aging, evil, chaos through crime narrative
- **As Reception**: Readers expect McCarthy's prose style, violent set pieces, ambiguous ending

**Owen Archetype Choice**: **Transposition** (preserve as much as possible)
- Why: McCarthy's prose already cinematic (sparse, visual, present-tense)
- Essential elements: Llewelyn's choice, Anton's implacability, Bell's disillusionment
- McCarthy's dialogue is perfect as-written (preserve)

**Transcoding Decisions**:

1. **Internality → Visuality**:
   - **Novel**: Extensive Bell internal monologues
   - **Film**: Bell's voiceover at beginning/end only; rest shown through behavior
   - **Why**: Too much voiceover would slow thriller pacing

2. **Compression**:
   - **Cut**: Subplot with hitchhiker (doesn't serve controlling idea)
   - **Condensed**: Multiple desert chase scenes → two key sequences
   - **Preserved**: All major plot turns (Llewelyn finds money, Anton kills Carla Jean)

3. **Medium Leverage**:
   - **Added**: Coin toss scene expanded with visual tension (light, shadow, sound design)
   - **Why**: Film can build suspense through time, image, sound (not possible in prose same way)

**McKee Value Analysis**:
- **Controlling Idea**: "Evil prevails because chaos is implacable"
- **Every scene** tests this idea through Llewelyn's choices (order) vs. Anton's randomness (chaos)
- **Climax**: Llewelyn dies off-screen (chaos wins)
- **Resolution**: Bell retires (order retreats)

**Result**: Near-perfect adaptation balancing fidelity and medium-specificity

### 7.3 Adaptation Decision Tree

**QUESTION 1: What is your source material?**

**Novel/Literary Fiction**:
→ **Primary challenge**: Interiority → Exteriority
→ **Use**: McKee (externalize through action) + Owen (transcoding)
→ **Technique**: Visual metaphors, behavioral subtext

**Stage Play**:
→ **Primary challenge**: Theatrical space → Cinematic space
→ **Use**: Field (open up locations) + McKee (add visual dynamism)
→ **Technique**: Move from single locations, add action scenes

**Memoir/Non-fiction**:
→ **Primary challenge**: Reality → Dramatic structure
→ **Use**: Truby (find moral argument) + Field (impose three-act shape)
→ **Technique**: Select events serving thematic point

**Comic Book/Graphic Novel**:
→ **Primary challenge**: Static images → Motion
→ **Use**: McKee (action/behavior) + Snyder (genre expectations)
→ **Technique**: Preserve visual style while creating temporal flow

**Video Game**:
→ **Primary challenge**: Interactivity → Passive viewing
→ **Use**: Field (structure) + McKee (character focus)
→ **Technique**: Player agency → protagonist choice

**QUESTION 2: What is the source's cultural status?**

**Beloved Classic** (Jane Austen, *Lord of the Rings*, *Harry Potter*):
→ **Approach**: Transposition (high fidelity)
→ **Audience expectation**: See what they imagined
→ **Risk**: Alienate fans with changes

**Genre Piece** (pulp, detective, romance):
→ **Approach**: Improve on source (add depth, style)
→ **Audience expectation**: Fresh take on familiar elements
→ **Freedom**: Change specifics while honoring genre

**Cult Favorite** (indie comics, foreign films, obscure novels):
→ **Approach**: Introduce to wider audience
→ **Audience expectation**: Preserve essence, accessible to newcomers
→ **Balance**: Satisfy fans, welcome new viewers

**Personal Story** (memoir, biography):
→ **Approach**: Interpretive (find the meaning)
→ **Audience expectation**: Truth with dramatic shape
→ **Responsibility**: Honor subject while crafting drama

**QUESTION 3: Why adapt this source?**

**Commercial Potential** (built-in audience, franchise potential):
→ **Priority**: Preserve recognizable elements (characters, key scenes, catchphrases)
→ **Use**: Snyder (genre satisfaction) + Field (commercial structure)

**Artistic Vision** (director/writer passion project):
→ **Priority**: Personal interpretation over fidelity
→ **Use**: McKee (controlling idea) + Truby (thematic depth)

**Social Relevance** (timely themes, cultural conversation):
→ **Priority**: Update context, preserve core message
→ **Use**: Hutcheon (commentary approach) + Truby (moral argument)

## SECTION 8: INTEGRATION DECISION TREES

### 8.1 Decision Tree 1: Choosing Your Primary Methodology

**START: What stage are you in?**

**🌱 CONCEPT/PLANNING STAGE**:
└─ **Question**: Do you have a premise or just a situation?
   ├─ **Premise** (clear thematic question): Use **Truby** to develop premise into designing principle
   │  └─ Build character web around premise
   └─ **Situation** (interesting setup): Use **Vorhaus** to find truth + pain
      └─ Develop comic premise or dramatic stakes

**📝 OUTLINING STAGE**:
└─ **Question**: Are you structurally confident or uncertain?
   ├─ **Confident**: Use **McKee** to design scenes with value shifts
   │  └─ Focus on causality and progression
   └─ **Uncertain**: Use **Snyder** to map 15-beat template
      └─ Fill in beats with scene ideas

**✍️ DRAFTING STAGE**:
└─ **Question**: Are you fast-drafting or crafting carefully?
   ├─ **Fast-drafting**: Use **Field** for structural guardrails
   │  └─ Hit plot points at approximate pages, refine later
   └─ **Careful crafting**: Use **McKee** for scene-by-scene construction
      └─ Ensure each scene turns values before moving on

**🔧 REVISION STAGE**:
└─ **Question**: Is something "off" or is it just needs polish?
   ├─ **Something's off**: Use **Seger** to diagnose problem
   │  └─ Apply appropriate methodology to fix
   └─ **Just needs polish**: Use **McKee** + **Truby** for thematic depth
      └─ Strengthen subtext, moral argument, character depth

### 8.2 Decision Tree 2: Troubleshooting Common Problems

**PROBLEM: "My second act sags"**

└─ **Diagnosis Question**: Is it pacing or content?
   ├─ **Pacing** (scenes drag, nothing happens):
   │  └─ **Apply**: Field's sequence structure
   │     ├─ Break Act II into 4 sequences with mini-climaxes
   │     └─ Ensure each sequence builds to turning point
   ├─ **Content** (boring, repetitive):
   │  └─ **Apply**: McKee's progressive complications
   │     ├─ Escalate stakes in each scene
   │     └─ Introduce new obstacles, don't repeat
   └─ **Both** (slow AND boring):
      └─ **Apply**: Snyder's "Fun and Games" + "Bad Guys Close In"
         ├─ Pages 30-55: Deliver premise promise
         └─ Pages 55-75: External pressure increases

**PROBLEM: "My protagonist isn't likable"**

└─ **Diagnosis Question**: Is character too passive, too mean, or too opaque?
   ├─ **Too Passive** (reactive, no agency):
   │  └─ **Apply**: Field's dramatic need
   │     └─ Give character clear, urgent want they actively pursue
   ├─ **Too Mean** (unlikable behavior, no redeeming qualities):
   │  └─ **Apply**: Snyder's "Save the Cat"
   │     ├─ Add early humanizing moment
   │     └─ Show character's good heart beneath flaws
   └─ **Too Opaque** (unclear motivation, mysterious):
      └─ **Apply**: Truby's need + ghost
         ├─ Reveal psychological wound explaining behavior
         └─ Show internal struggle (wants security, needs growth)

**PROBLEM: "My scenes feel talky"**

└─ **Diagnosis Question**: Is it exposition, lack of conflict, or both?
   ├─ **Exposition** (characters explaining things):
   │  └─ **Apply**: McKee's show-don't-tell
   │     ├─ Convert exposition to discovery
   │     └─ Reveal information through action/behavior
   ├─ **Lack of Conflict** (characters agree, chat):
   │  └─ **Apply**: McKee's gap principle
   │     ├─ Each character wants something from the other
   │     └─ Create obstacle to that want within scene
   └─ **Both** (expository AND conflict-free):
      └─ **Apply**: Seger's scene purpose audit
         ├─ If scene's only purpose is exposition, cut it
         └─ Integrate essential info into conflict-driven scene

**PROBLEM: "The ending feels unearned"**

└─ **Diagnosis Question**: Is the climax too easy or too sudden?
   ├─ **Too Easy** (protagonist wins without sufficient struggle):
   │  └─ **Apply**: McKee's crisis choice
   │     ├─ Force dilemma (both options have cost)
   │     └─ Make protagonist sacrifice something real
   ├─ **Too Sudden** (transformation happens without buildup):
   │  └─ **Apply**: Truby's revelation cascade
   │     ├─ Plant 3-4 insight moments across Act II
   │     └─ Each insight moves protagonist toward change
   └─ **Both** (sudden AND easy):
      └─ **Apply**: McKee's controlling idea + Truby's moral argument
         ├─ Ensure climax *proves* your theme through hard choice
         └─ Make cost of transformation real and visible

### 8.3 Decision Tree 3: Comedy-Specific Decisions

**START: What kind of comedy are you writing?**

**🎭 PURE COMEDY** (Laughs primary goal):
└─ **Use**: Vorhaus (comic premise) + Snyder (beat structure) + Field (pacing)
   └─ **Emphasis**: Game of the scene, premise variations, comic distance

**💕 ROMANTIC COMEDY**:
└─ **Use**: Snyder (rom-com beats) + Truby (dual character arcs) + Vorhaus (comic obstacles)
   └─ **Emphasis**: Relationship milestones, meet-cute, forced proximity, obstacles

**🖤 DARK COMEDY**:
└─ **Use**: McKee (values) + Vorhaus (comic distance) + Truby (moral argument)
   └─ **Emphasis**: Serious themes with comic perspective, tonal management

**😱 HORROR-COMEDY**:
└─ **Use**: McKee (genre) + Vorhaus (comic relief) + Snyder (genre hybrid)
   └─ **Emphasis**: Tonal shifts, escalation, genre expectations

**🎪 FARCE**:
└─ **Use**: Vorhaus (escalation) + Field (compression) + Snyder (timing)
   └─ **Emphasis**: Doors slamming, misunderstandings compounding, physical comedy

**PROBLEM: "Comedy kills the drama" or "Drama kills the comedy"**

└─ **Apply**: Calibrated distance model
   ├─ **Map emotional spectrum**: Where on scale from pure comedy to pure drama?
   ├─ **Design tonal shift**: Plan where tone transitions (usually Act II B)
   ├─ **Test each scene**: Is this scene comic, dramatic, or hybrid?
   └─ **Adjust distance**: More stakes = less distance; more comedy = more distance

## SECTION 9: PRACTICAL APPLICATION EXAMPLES

### 9.1 Example 1: Feature Comedy Development Using Integrated Approach

**PROJECT**: Original romantic comedy feature

**STAGE 1: CONCEPT (Truby + Vorhaus)**

**Truby's Premise Development**:
- **Moral contradiction**: "Honesty vs. Social Harmony"
- **Value conflict**: Should we tell painful truths or kind lies?

**Vorhaus's Comic Premise**:
- **Truth**: People prefer comforting lies to harsh truths
- **Pain**: What if someone couldn't lie anymore?
- **Comic premise**: "*Liar Liar* meets rom-com" - Woman cursed to tell truth must navigate dating

**STAGE 2: CHARACTER (Truby + McKee + Snyder)**

**Protagonist - JESS**:
- **Want** (McKee): Find lasting love, get married
- **Need** (Truby): Stop hiding behind social niceties, be authentic
- **Flaw** (Snyder): People-pleasing (says what others want to hear)
- **Ghost** (Truby): Childhood - parents' divorce after years of "everything's fine"

**Love Interest - MARCUS**:
- **Want**: Find genuine connection (tired of shallow dating)
- **Function** (Truby): Represents value of authenticity
- **Obstacle**: Can't tell if women are being real or performing

**Antagonist/Foil - RACHEL** (Jess's sister):
- **Want**: Keep family peace at all costs
- **Function** (Truby): Represents extreme people-pleasing
- **Represents**: The path Jess is on if she doesn't change

**STAGE 3: STRUCTURE (Snyder + Field + Vorhaus)**

**Snyder's 15 Beats**:

1. **Opening Image** (p. 1): Jess lying to everyone (friend, boss, date) in montage
2. **Theme Stated** (p. 5): Friend: "You're too nice. When will you say what you really think?"
3. **Setup** (p. 1-10): Establish Jess's people-pleasing in work, dating, family
4. **Catalyst** (p. 12): Meet Marcus at wedding; instant attraction but awkward interaction
5. **Debate** (p. 12-25): Should Jess pursue Marcus? She wants to but fears vulnerability
6. **Break into Two** (p. 25): Mysterious woman at wedding gives Jess "gift" - she can only tell truth for 30 days
7. **B-Story** (p. 30): Marcus subplot - they start dating just as curse begins
8. **Fun and Games** (p. 30-55): **COMIC PREMISE DELIVERED**
   - Truth-telling creates havoc at work (insults boss, gets fired)
   - Truth-telling with Marcus creates unexpected intimacy (refreshing honesty)
   - Truth-telling with family exposes years of buried resentments
9. **Midpoint** (p. 55): False victory - Marcus loves her honesty; relationship deepening
10. **Bad Guys Close In** (p. 55-75): Truth backfires
    - Jess tells Marcus his restaurant idea is terrible (hurts him)
    - Truth reveals she's been judging everyone
    - Realizes radical honesty ≠ cruelty
11. **All Is Lost** (p. 75): Marcus breaks up with her; family stops speaking to her
12. **Dark Night** (p. 75-85): Jess realizes problem isn't lying vs. truth - it's authentic kindness
13. **Break into Three** (p. 85): Curse ends; Jess has choice again
14. **Finale** (p. 85-110): Jess makes amends with authentic but kind truth
    - Apologizes to boss (gets job back through honesty + humility)
    - Reconciles with family (honest but loving)
    - Grand gesture with Marcus (truth: "I was scared of being known")
15. **Final Image** (p. 110): Jess speaking truth with kindness; Marcus proposes

**Field's Three Acts**:
- **Act I** (pp. 1-25): Setup people-pleasing pattern; meet Marcus; curse begins
- **Act II A** (pp. 25-55): Truth creates comic havoc; Marcus relationship deepens
- **Midpoint** (p. 55): False victory - honesty seems like solution
- **Act II B** (pp. 55-85): Truth without kindness destroys relationships
- **Act III** (pp. 85-110): Jess learns authentic kindness; wins Marcus back

**STAGE 4: SCENE DESIGN (McKee + Vorhaus)**

**Example Scene: First Date After Curse (Act II A, p. 35-38)**

**McKee's Scene Construction**:
- **Opening value**: Anxious (Jess worried curse will ruin date)
- **Jess's want**: Have successful date with Marcus
- **Marcus's want**: Get to know real Jess
- **Conflict**: Curse forces brutal honesty
- **Turning point**: Marcus responds positively to honesty
- **Closing value**: Relieved/Hopeful (honesty creates intimacy)

**Vorhaus's Comic Structure**:
- **Game of the scene**: "Tell me about yourself" meets "I have to tell the truth"
- **Truth + Pain**: People want authenticity but also want flattery
- **Comic beats**:
  1. Marcus: "You look beautiful." / Jess: "You're just saying that."
  2. Marcus: "No, really!" / Jess: "I spent 3 hours on this outfit and I still hate it."
  3. Marcus laughs, disarmed by honesty
  4. Marcus: "What do you do for fun?" / Jess: "I pretend to like things to seem interesting."
  5. Marcus: "Okay, what do you ACTUALLY like?" / Jess: (curse forces honesty) "Judging people."
  6. Beat. Marcus laughs. "Finally, someone honest!"
  7. Their conversation becomes genuinely connected through honesty

**Result**: Scene delivers comedy (truth-telling creates awkwardness) AND drama (honesty creates intimacy), advancing both comic premise and character arc.

**STAGE 5: REVISION (Seger + McKee + Truby)**

**First Draft Problems** (identified in coverage):
1. **Problem**: "Protagonist learns lesson too early; Act III feels redundant"
   - **Seger diagnosis**: Structural - midpoint is the climax
   - **Fix using Field**: Shift revelation - Jess thinks honesty is the answer at midpoint, but Act II B shows honesty without kindness is cruel; real lesson is authentic kindness

2. **Problem**: "Marcus feels underwritten; not clear why he falls for Jess"
   - **Truby diagnosis**: Character web - Marcus's need/ghost underdeveloped  
   - **Fix using Truby**: Add Marcus backstory - his ex was performative, exhausting; Jess's honesty (even when awkward) is refreshing contrast

3. **Problem**: "Family subplot distracts from romance"
   - **Seger diagnosis**: Competing purposes - trying to do too much
   - **Fix using McKee**: Align family subplot with controlling idea; family scenes must advance romantic relationship somehow (Marcus sees how Jess handles family crisis)

4. **Problem**: "Some scenes just chat; no value turn"
   - **McKee diagnosis**: Scenes lack conflict and value shift
   - **Fix using McKee**: Audit every scene; ensure each turns positive↔negative; cut or combine scenes that don't turn

**Final Polish** (McKee + Truby):
- **McKee's controlling idea audit**: "Authentic kindness triumphs because it balances truth with compassion"
- **Truby's moral argument check**: Does every character represent position on honesty vs. kindness spectrum?
- **Snyder's genre delivery**: Does "Fun and Games" (Act II A) deliver on premise promise (truth-telling comedy)?

**RESULT**: Structurally sound romantic comedy that delivers laughs while earning emotional catharsis through character transformation.

## SECTION 10: SUMMARY & SELECTION GUIDE

### 10.1 Quick Reference: When to Use Which Methodology

**USE FIELD WHEN**:
- ✅ You need structural clarity and pacing
- ✅ You're writing plot-driven genre stories
- ✅ You want clear three-act shape with identifiable turning points
- ✅ You're stuck in the middle and need act break guidance
- ❌ Don't use Field alone for character-driven dramas (needs McKee/Truby)

**USE MCKEE WHEN**:
- ✅ You need scene-level craft and value dynamics
- ✅ You're writing character-driven dramas
- ✅ You want thematic depth and controlling idea clarity
- ✅ Your scenes feel flat or purposeless
- ❌ Don't use McKee alone for pure comedy (may kill rhythm)

**USE TRUBY WHEN**:
- ✅ You need organic character web design
- ✅ You're exploring moral/thematic questions
- ✅ You want sophisticated opposition and character relationships
- ✅ You're writing ensemble pieces
- ❌ Don't use Truby alone for fast-paced action (may over-complicate)

**USE SNYDER WHEN**:
- ✅ You want clear commercial structure with beat sheet
- ✅ You're writing within established genres
- ✅ You need genre-specific guidance (his 10 genres)
- ✅ You want marketable "Save the Cat" likability
- ❌ Don't use Snyder alone for experimental or art films (too formulaic)

**USE VORHAUS WHEN**:
- ✅ You're writing comedy of any kind
- ✅ You need comic premise and game of the scene
- ✅ Your comedy feels too serious or preachy
- ✅ You want to understand truth + pain dynamics
- ❌ Don't use Vorhaus alone for straight drama (needs McKee/Truby)

**USE SEGER WHEN**:
- ✅ You're in revision and something feels "off"
- ✅ You need to diagnose specific problems
- ✅ You're working with existing draft that needs repair
- ✅ You want scene-by-scene purpose audit
- ❌ Don't use Seger alone for initial planning (diagnostic, not generative)

**USE HUTCHEON + OWEN WHEN**:
- ✅ You're adapting existing material
- ✅ You need to understand source text deeply
- ✅ You're making fidelity decisions
- ✅ You're transcoding between media
- ❌ Don't use adaptation theory for original material

### 10.2 Recommended Integration Combinations

**COMBINATION 1: "The Architect"** (Heavy Planning)
- **Phase 1**: Truby (character web, premise)
- **Phase 2**: Snyder (15-beat outline)
- **Phase 3**: McKee (scene-by-scene value design)
- **Phase 4**: Seger (revision/diagnosis)
- **Best for**: Writers who need comprehensive roadmap before drafting

**COMBINATION 2: "The Builder"** (Iterative Development)
- **Phase 1**: Field (three-act structure only)
- **Phase 2**: Draft quickly without overthinking
- **Phase 3**: McKee + Seger (scene-level revision)
- **Phase 4**: Truby (deepen character/theme)
- **Best for**: Writers who prefer discovery through drafting

**COMBINATION 3: "The Specialist"** (Genre-First)
- **Phase 1**: Snyder (identify genre, beat sheet)
- **Phase 2**: Field (ensure structure hits genre requirements)
- **Phase 3**: McKee (craft individual scenes)
- **Phase 4**: Seger (polish)
- **Best for**: Writers focused on commercial genre work

**COMBINATION 4: "The Artist"** (Theme-First)
- **Phase 1**: McKee (controlling idea)
- **Phase 2**: Truby (moral argument, character web)
- **Phase 3**: Field (structure serves theme, not formula)
- **Phase 4**: Seger (ensure clarity without preachiness)
- **Best for**: Writers focused on thematic exploration

**COMBINATION 5: "The Comedian"** (Comedy-First)
- **Phase 1**: Vorhaus (comic premise, truth + pain)
- **Phase 2**: Snyder (beat sheet with comedy emphasis)
- **Phase 3**: McKee (selected scenes for dramatic weight)
- **Phase 4**: Seger (tonal management)
- **Best for**: Comedy writers balancing laughs and story

### 10.3 Final Integration Principles

**PRINCIPLE 1: COMPLEMENTARY, NOT CONTRADICTORY**
- Don't choose ONE methodology and reject others
- Different systems illuminate different dimensions
- Use appropriate tool for each challenge

**PRINCIPLE 2: ADAPT, DON'T ADOPT**
- No methodology is scripture
- Understand principles, then adapt to your project
- Rules should serve the story, not vice versa

**PRINCIPLE 3: DIAGNOSE BEFORE PRESCRIBING**
- When stuck, identify WHAT is broken (use Seger)
- Then choose appropriate methodology to fix it
- Don't apply random fixes hoping something works

**PRINCIPLE 4: SEQUENCE MATTERS**
- Some methodologies work better in planning vs. revision
- Truby + Snyder = planning; Seger + McKee = revision
- Understand which tools work at which stage

**PRINCIPLE 5: KNOW YOUR OWN PROCESS**
- Planners: Use Truby/Snyder heavily before drafting
- Discovery writers: Use Field/McKee in revision
- Some writers need more structure, others less
- Adapt integration to YOUR creative process

**PRINCIPLE 6: GENRE SHAPES METHODOLOGY**
- Action/thrillers: Field + Snyder
- Dramas: McKee + Truby
- Comedies: Vorhaus + Snyder
- Let genre needs determine methodological emphasis

## CONCLUSION

The goal of methodological integration is not to create a rigid system, but to give you **fluency across multiple perspectives**. Think of Field, McKee, Truby, Snyder, Vorhaus, Seger, Hutcheon, and Owen as colleagues in a writers' room, each offering different insights:

- **Field** says: "Where are your act breaks? Is your structure sound?"
- **McKee** says: "Do your scenes turn values? Does your story have meaning?"
- **Truby** says: "What's your moral argument? How do characters represent values?"
- **Snyder** says: "Are you delivering genre expectations? Is your hero likable?"
- **Vorhaus** says: "Where's the truth and pain? What's the game of the scene?"
- **Seger** says: "What's broken? Let's diagnose before rewriting."
- **Hutcheon** says: "How does your source material function? What must you preserve?"
- **Owen** says: "What's your fidelity approach? How will you transcode?"

The master screenwriter **listens to all these voices** and synthesizes their wisdom into a coherent creative vision. You don't need to use every methodology on every project—but knowing when to consult which colleague makes you a more sophisticated storyteller.

**Remember**: 
- Methodologies are **lenses**, not **laws**
- Integration means **synthesis**, not **syncretism**
- The goal is **flexibility**, not **formulaic adherence**

Use these frameworks to see more clearly, but trust your instincts about what your specific story needs.
