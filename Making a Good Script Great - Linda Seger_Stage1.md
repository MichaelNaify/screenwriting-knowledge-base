EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (≈ 2 000 words)

Purpose and Scope

Linda Seger’s Making a Good Script Great remains one of the most enduring and practically influential manuals in screenwriting pedagogy. It sits midway between the technical architecture of Syd Field’s Screenplay and the narrative philosophy of Robert McKee’s Story—providing writers with a structured yet empathetic approach to the revision process. Where Field teaches construction and McKee champions theory, Seger teaches transformation: how to take a script that “works” and turn it into a script that moves people.

This Stage 1 Analysis positions Seger within the continuum of screenwriting theory, identifies her core architectural principles, and maps her distinctive pedagogical emphasis on rewriting as creative rebirth. The goal is to extract the deep conceptual and methodological systems that underlie her approach, making them interoperable within the broader PK ecosystem of screenwriting and adaptation texts.

⸻

Core Thesis

Seger’s central premise is that great screenplays emerge not from initial inspiration but through systematic revision. Her definition of rewriting is neither cosmetic nor mechanical: it is an act of re-vision—seeing the story anew. The rewriting phase exposes a writer’s conceptual weaknesses, reveals latent themes, and refines structural causality. A script’s potential is often present but obscured by clutter, confusion, or fear of change. Seger teaches how to diagnose and remove these obstacles with rigor and grace.

In her framework, rewriting = diagnosis + prescription + execution. She positions the writer as a doctor of story, the script as the patient, and the diagnostic tools as a set of questions: What is the story about? Who changes, and how? Where does it begin and end? What is its emotional spine? These questions anchor her analysis to a practical ethos of craft rather than abstract aesthetics.

2.1 Core Concept Cluster — Story Architecture

#STORY-FOUNDATION-COMPLETE

Definition:
Seger defines story as the coherent sequence of cause-and-effect events through which a character pursues a goal and undergoes change. It is not the same as plot (mechanical structure) nor theme (philosophical meaning); rather, it is the dynamic system that binds them.

Key Principle:

“Story is what happens because someone wants something badly and is having trouble getting it.”

Hierarchical Components:
	1.	#Premise: The central dramatic question that drives the entire narrative.
	2.	#Protagonist: The agent through whom the story is experienced.
	3.	#Goal: The object of desire or necessity that creates forward momentum.
	4.	#Conflict: The obstacles—internal, external, societal—that oppose the goal.
	5.	#Resolution: The fulfillment or failure of the goal revealing transformation.

Seger advocates for story development through progressive revelation: each scene must either increase tension or deepen understanding of the protagonist.

Film Illustrations: Tootsie, Witness, Chariots of Fire—each demonstrates story unity where the protagonist’s goal aligns with thematic premise.

PK Integration:
The concept functions as a node under #story-engineering and connects upward to #narrative-causality and #teleological-structure clusters.

⸻

#STORY-PREMISE-COMPLETE

Definition:
The premise articulates what the story is fundamentally about—its moral or philosophical engine. For Seger, a weak premise equals a weak story because it lacks direction.

Formula:
Premise = Conflict + Movement + Transformation

Diagnostic Questions:
	•	What is the story really about (beneath the plot)?
	•	Is the conflict dramatic and visual?
	•	Does the resolution fulfill the implied question?
	•	What human truth does the story dramatize?

Example:

Tootsie: “A man learns to be a better man by becoming a woman.”
The premise fuses irony, transformation, and moral insight.

Tag Associations:
#thematic-core #moral-premise #story-unity #narrative-teleology

⸻

#STRUCTURE-DYNAMICS-COMPLETE

Definition:
Structure is the pattern of rising, turning, and resolving action that creates emotional momentum. Seger aligns with classical three-act form but allows for flexible interpretation.

Three Core Movements:
	1.	Beginning: Establish situation and goal (#setup).
	2.	Middle: Escalate conflict and reveal hidden needs (#confrontation).
	3.	End: Culminate action in revelation or transformation (#resolution).

Each turning point is a decision moment—a moral or psychological choice altering story direction.

Seger’s Addition:
Every structural beat should externalize an inner evolution. Turning points are psychological thresholds.

Film Examples:
Casablanca, Witness, Ordinary People—used to show cause-and-effect logic and escalating stakes.

PK Crosslinks:
#dramatic-structure → Field (mechanics)
#organic-growth → Truby (integration)
#value-shift → McKee (thematic polarity)

⸻

#TURNING-POINTS-COMPLETE

Definition:
Turning points are the hinges of change in a story; they occur when decisions or discoveries redirect the character’s trajectory.

Types Identified by Seger:
	1.	Reversal: New obstacle or information flips expectations.
	2.	Revelation: Hidden truth changes understanding.
	3.	Decision: Choice commits protagonist to new path.

Diagnostic Rules:
	•	Must arise from character motivation, not coincidence.
	•	Must change the story’s emotional stakes.
	•	Should occur approximately every 20–25 minutes of screen time in standard pacing.

Tag Associations:
#causality #reversal #choice #psychological-momentum

2.2 Character Cluster

#CHARACTER-SYSTEM-COMPLETE

Definition:
Characters are the vehicles through which theme and story are expressed. Each must serve a structural and emotional function.

Core Principle:

“The audience does not fall in love with the plot; they fall in love with the people who live it.”

Key Subcomponents:
	•	#Protagonist: Central agent of change.
	•	#Antagonist: Embodied opposition testing the protagonist’s values.
	•	#Supporting-Characters: Mirrors or contrasts revealing facets of the hero.
	•	#Relationship-Arcs: Emotional ecosystems illustrating theme.

Seger’s Psychology of Character:
	•	Every major character has want, need, and fear.
	•	The gap between want and need defines inner conflict.
	•	Secondary characters exist to pressure or reveal this gap.

Film Examples: Ordinary People, The Verdict, Erin Brockovich.

PK Integration:
#character-psychology #moral-argument #emotional-architecture

⸻

#CHARACTER-TRANSFORMATION-COMPLETE

Definition:
Transformation is the visible manifestation of the protagonist’s internal evolution. The story’s meaning lies in this movement, not its events.

Three Phases:
	1.	#Setup-of-Need: Show initial imbalance or flaw.
	2.	#Catalyst-of-Change: External pressures trigger introspection.
	3.	#Resolution-of-Insight: New behavior confirms growth.

Seger’s Distinction:
Change is emotional truth, not plot twist. Transformation may be subtle or monumental, but it must be earned.

Diagnostic Questions:
	•	What belief does the character hold that the story challenges?
	•	How is that belief altered by experience?
	•	Is the transformation visible and credible?

Film Examples: Witness (John Book’s humility), Tootsie (Michael’s empathy).

PK Mapping:
#character-arc #psychological-realism #thematic-resolution

⸻

#CONFLICT-SYSTEM-COMPLETE

Definition:
Conflict is the pressure cooker of drama. It must operate at multiple levels—external (action), internal (emotion), and interpersonal (relationships).

Seger’s Tri-Level Conflict Model:

Level	Description	Example
External	Physical obstacles or antagonists	Police pursuit in The Fugitive
Internal	Moral/psychological struggle	Guilt in Ordinary People
Relational	Emotional tension between allies	Family opposition in Dead Poets Society

Diagnostic Lens:
Conflict reveals theme; if conflict is trivial, theme is trivial.

Tag Associations:
#dramatic-conflict #multi-layered-tension #emotional-causality

2.3 Theme and Moral Architecture

#THEME-AS-MORAL-ARGUMENT-COMPLETE

Definition:
Theme expresses the story’s moral logic—what the writer believes about human behavior. Seger insists that theme must emerge from plot and character, not be superimposed.

Formula:
Theme = Character Transformation + Moral Choice + Consequence

Seger’s Distinction:
Theme is the writer’s worldview made manifest through the protagonist’s choices. It should not preach; it should demonstrate.

Diagnostic Questions:
	•	What value system is at stake?
	•	How are choices tested and consequences dramatized?
	•	Does resolution affirm or challenge audience expectations?

PK Crosslinks:
#moral-argument (Truby) | #value-change (McKee) | #aesthetic-ethics

⸻

#PREMISE-TO-THEME-LINKAGE

Seger teaches writers to ensure every structural decision serves the theme. This linkage guarantees organic unity.

Example:
Chariots of Fire — Premise: “Faith and ambition in conflict.”
Theme: “Integrity outweighs victory.”
Every subplot reinforces this moral tension.

2.4 The Rewriting Process

#REWRITING-METHODOLOGY-COMPLETE

Definition:
Rewriting is an iterative cycle of discovery, evaluation, and refinement that transforms the initial draft into an emotionally coherent screenplay.

Five-Step Method:
	1.	Concept Re-Evaluation: Reassess premise clarity.
	2.	Structural Diagnosis: Test logic and pacing.
	3.	Character Calibration: Strengthen motivation and arc.
	4.	Scene Rewriting: Cut, condense, or combine for momentum.
	5.	Dialogue Refinement: Replace exposition with behavior.

Seger’s Guiding Maxim:

“You cannot fix the words until you fix the story.”

Rewriting is triage: fix the most foundational problems first.

Tag Integration:
#revision-ecology #diagnostic-pedagogy #narrative-polish

⸻

#SCENE-ANALYSIS-COMPLETE

Definition:
Every scene is a miniature story with its own beginning, middle, and end. Seger calls this the “micro-structure of drama.”

Checklist:
	•	What is the scene’s purpose? (#story-function)
	•	Whose point of view dominates? (#perspective)
	•	What changes emotionally? (#value-shift)
	•	Does it end differently than it began? (#progression)

Film Examples: Witness (barn-raising sequence), Tootsie (final reveal).

PK Mapping:
#scene-dynamics #micro-structure #emotional-geometry

⸻

#DIALOGUE-REWRITING-COMPLETE

Definition:
Dialogue must reveal character and advance conflict without exposition. The best dialogue is compressed emotion—the visible tip of submerged tension.

Rules of Thumb:
	•	Avoid redundancy: if it’s clear from action, cut the line.
	•	Seek rhythm and subtext over explanation.
	•	Conflict within dialogue > information exchange.
	•	Silence is dialogue’s highest form.

Example:
In Ordinary People, silence between mother and son communicates estrangement more powerfully than words.

Tags:
#subtext #economy #verbal-action #listening-as-dramaturgy

⸻

#TRANSITION-TECHNIQUES-COMPLETE

Definition:
Transitions carry emotional and visual energy between scenes. Poor transitions break flow; great ones create rhythm and inevitability.

Methods Identified:
	•	Visual Match Cut: Object or motion continuity.
	•	Emotional Echo: Scene ends and next begins on same emotional tone.
	•	Contrast Cut: Juxtaposition for irony or shock.
	•	Thematic Bridge: Connects ideas, not actions.

Seger’s Maxim:

“If your script feels choppy, look at your transitions.”

PK Cluster:
#continuity #film-grammar #editing-consciousness

	•	Identify story premise.

	•	Purpose, progress, pivot.
	•	Value shift (+/- matrix per McKee).
	•	Visual continuity and emotional flow.

Stage 5 → Dialogue Audit
	•	Cut redundancy.
	•	Convert exposition → behavior.
	•	Heighten subtext.

Abstract Summary

Linda Seger’s Making a Good Script Great codifies the art of rewriting as a structured act of creative transformation.  Her thesis—a script becomes great when its structure, character, and theme align through deliberate revision—redefines screenwriting as an iterative craft rather than a flash of genius.

Across five analytical strata, Seger’s pedagogy unfolds:
	1.	Story Architecture – Establishes causal integrity between plot and emotion.  Story equals character in motion; conflict and transformation are its engines.
	2.	Character System – Introduces the triadic model of want, need, fear; internal tension generates external plot.
	3.	Theme Integration – Aligns moral argument with dramatic consequence; theme discovered through rewriting, not imposed.
	4.	Rewriting Diagnostics – Five-phase methodology (concept → structure → character → scene → dialogue) establishing professional workflow.
	5.	Creative Psychology – Encourages emotional resilience, collaboration literacy, and empathy as pillars of sustainable craft.

Seger’s innovations transform the solitary struggle of writing into a reproducible professional process.  Her contribution lies not in inventing new structures but in teaching writers to see the old ones more clearly—to detect the invisible geometry beneath intuition.
