#### **#PALIMPSESTIC-RECEPTION-PHENOMENOLOGY**

**MASTER TAG:** #Oscillation-Experience-Complete

**DEFINITION (Phenomenological):**
**Palimpsestic reception** describes the experience of **knowing audiences** who engage with adaptations while simultaneously maintaining awareness of source text(s). This creates a doubled consciousness—oscillating between memory (of source) and perception (of adaptation)—that constitutes adaptation's distinctive phenomenology.

**ETYMOLOGICAL FOUNDATION:**

**Palimpsest:** Greek *palímpsēstos* = "scraped again"
- Original: Medieval manuscript where earlier text scraped off, new text written over
- Traces of original remain visible beneath
- Metaphor: Adaptation as textual palimpsest where "source text ghosts through"

**THEORETICAL LINEAGE:**

**Sarah Dillon, *The Palimpsest* (2007):**
- Palimpsest as temporal paradox: "Earlier" text appears "on top" in memory
- Reading palimpsests requires simultaneous attention to multiple layers
- **Hutcheon adapts:** Audience experience as temporal layering

**Gérard Genette, *Palimpsests: Literature in the Second Degree* (1982):**
- *Hypotext* (source) and *hypertext* (derived work) coexist in reading
- Transformation and imitation as palimpsestic operations
- **Hutcheon's addition:** Emphasizes experiential dimension Genette undertheorizes

**Maurice Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945):**
- Perception always involves **memory** (past experience shapes present perception)
- Temporality of consciousness: retention, perception, protention
- **Hutcheon applies:** Adaptation reception as special case of perceptual temporality

**Edmund Husserl, *The Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness* (1893-1917):**
- **Retention:** Immediate past held in consciousness
- **Protention:** Anticipation of immediate future
- **Primal impression:** Present moment
- **Hutcheon's insight:** Knowing audiences experience extended retention (source text memory) alongside perception

**THE OSCILLATION PHENOMENON:**

**STRUCTURE OF EXPERIENCE:**

```
TEMPORAL LAYERS IN ADAPTATION RECEPTION:

Layer 1: PRESENT PERCEPTION
↓ (What I'm experiencing NOW)
↓ Watching Pride & Prejudice film (2005)
↓ Seeing Keira Knightley's Elizabeth
↓ Hearing Dario Marianelli's score
↓
Layer 2: ACTIVATED MEMORY  
↓ (What I REMEMBER from before)
↓ Novel's Elizabeth as imagined
↓ BBC's Jennifer Ehle Elizabeth (1995)
↓ Austen's prose style
↓
Layer 3: COMPARATIVE CONSCIOUSNESS
↓ (Oscillation between layers)
↓ "This Elizabeth is younger"
↓ "The ending is changed"
↓ "Darcy's first proposal happens differently"
↓
Layer 4: EVALUATIVE RESPONSE
↓ (Judgment emerging from comparison)
↓ "I prefer this interpretation because..."
↓ "The film loses Austen's irony but gains..."
↓ "This makes me rethink the novel..."
```

**PHENOMENOLOGICAL CHARACTERISTICS:**

**1. DOUBLED CONSCIOUSNESS:**
- Not sequential (first remember, then perceive)
- **Simultaneous:** Memory and perception coexist
- Creates unique cognitive load (pleasurable or frustrating depending on execution)

**2. INVOLUNTARY ACTIVATION:**
- Knowing audiences **cannot help** comparing
- Memory intrudes unbidden
- Even when trying to experience adaptation "on its own terms," source text persists

**3. VARIABLE INTENSITY:**
- **Strong oscillation:** When adaptation diverges dramatically (triggers active comparison)
- **Weak oscillation:** When adaptation closely follows source (memory confirms perception)
- **Optimal oscillation:** Enough similarity for recognition, enough difference for interest

**4. TEMPORAL PARADOX:**
- **Chronologically:** Source came first, adaptation came second
- **Phenomenologically:** In memory, source may feel "on top" during adaptation viewing
- **Dillon's insight:** Earlier text appears later in consciousness

**5. PRODUCTIVE INSTABILITY:**
- Oscillation doesn't resolve into single interpretation
- **Both** texts remain active in consciousness
- Meaning emerges from **dialogue** between them

**TYPES OF KNOWING AUDIENCES:**

**CATEGORY 1: SOURCE-FAMILIAR (Most common)**
- Read novel before seeing film
- Source text strongly encoded in memory
- Most intense oscillation experience
- Risk: Disappointment if expectations unmet

**CATEGORY 2: ADAPTATION-FIRST (Increasingly common)**
- See film, then read novel
- **Reverse palimpsest:** Novel read through film's "lens"
- Different kind of oscillation (novel feels like "adaptation" of film)
- Can appreciate both more fully

**CATEGORY 3: MULTI-ADAPTATION AWARE**
- Experienced multiple versions (stage, film, miniseries, etc.)
- **Layered palimpsest:** Multiple prior texts activated simultaneously
- Most complex oscillation (comparing across many versions)
- Expertise enables sophisticated comparison

**CATEGORY 4: GENRE-SAVVY (Parallel knowing)**
- May not know specific source but know genre conventions
- Oscillation between adaptation and **generic expectations**
- Different knowledge structure but similar comparative consciousness

**CATEGORY 5: UNKNOWING (Baseline for comparison)**
- No prior knowledge of source
- **No oscillation** (just direct experience)
- Different pleasures available (surprise, discovery)
- Later becoming "knowing" changes relationship to adaptation

**PLEASURES OF PALIMPSESTIC RECEPTION:**

**PLEASURE 1: RECOGNITION**
- "I know this moment!"
- Satisfaction of seeing imagined scenes visualized
- Nostalgia for first encounter with story
- **Comfort** of familiar narrative

**PLEASURE 2: SURPRISE**
- "They changed it!"
- Delight in unexpected interpretations
- New angles on familiar material
- **Stimulation** of difference

**PLEASURE 3: CRITICAL ENGAGEMENT**
- "Why did they make that choice?"
- Active interpretation of adaptive decisions
- Intellectual pleasure of analysis
- **Mastery** through understanding

**PLEASURE 4: ENRICHED UNDERSTANDING**
- "I never thought of it that way"
- Adaptation illuminates source
- Source illuminates adaptation
- **Depth** through intertextual dialogue

**PLEASURE 5: COMMUNAL DISCOURSE**
- "What did you think of the changes?"
- Shared discussion with other knowing audiences
- Social dimension of comparison
- **Connection** through fan community

**RISKS OF PALIMPSESTIC RECEPTION:**

**RISK 1: DISAPPOINTMENT**
- When adaptation fails to match imagined version
- "Ruined" the source text in memory
- Negative oscillation (constant dissatisfaction)
- Can prevent appreciation of adaptation's merits

**RISK 2: DISTRACTION**
- Excessive comparison prevents immersion
- Cannot engage with adaptation on its own terms
- Oscillation becomes obstacle rather than enhancement
- Miss what adaptation offers uniquely

**RISK 3: NOSTALGIA BIAS**
- First encounter privileged over later ones
- "Original is always better" assumption
- Prevents fair evaluation of subsequent adaptations
- Romantic ideology of originality persisting in reception

**RISK 4: COGNITIVE OVERLOAD**
- Too many prior texts activated simultaneously
- Confusion rather than enrichment
- Inability to track all comparative dimensions
- Exhaustion rather than pleasure

**CASE STUDY: *JANE EYRE* PALIMPSESTS**

**SOURCE TEXT:** Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* (1847)

**MAJOR ADAPTATIONS:**
- Orson Welles film (1943)
- Franco Zeffirelli film (1996)
- BBC miniseries (2006)
- Cary Fukunaga film (2011)
- Plus: Wide Sargasso Sea (Rhys 1966) - postcolonial rewriting

**PALIMPSESTIC EXPERIENCE OF KNOWING AUDIENCE:**

**Watching 2011 Fukunaga film:**

**PERCEPTION (What's on screen):**
- Mia Wasikowska's Jane (young, ethereal)
- Michael Fassbender's Rochester (rugged, intense)
- Non-linear structure (begins with Jane fleeing Thornfield)
- Gothic atmosphere (emphasis on Jane's trauma)
- Feminist interpretation (Jane's agency foregrounded)

**MEMORY (Prior texts activated):**
- **Brontë's novel:** First-person narration, Jane's interiority, Victorian prose
- **1996 Zeffirelli:** Romanticized, lush, more Rochester-focused
- **2006 BBC:** Literary fidelity, comprehensive, Ruth Wilson's fierce Jane
- **Wide Sargasso Sea:** Bertha's perspective (Fukunaga includes more of her)

**OSCILLATION (Comparative consciousness):**
- "This Jane feels more traumatized than Brontë's"
- "The non-linear structure emphasizes Jane's PTSD"
- "Rochester less Byronic, more naturalistic"
- "They include Bertha's perspective—nod to Rhys?"
- "Loses some of novel's religious dimension"
- "But gains contemporary feminist resonance"

**EVALUATION (Judgment):**
- Adaptation enriches understanding of Jane's psychological damage
- Contemporary audience can connect to trauma narrative
- But loses Victorian spiritual journey dimension
- Gothic atmosphere more pronounced than Brontë's
- **Conclusion:** Excellent adaptation FOR contemporary moment; different pleasures than novel

**This demonstrates palimpsestic reception's productivity:** Comparison doesn't diminish adaptation but **enriches both texts** through dialogue.

#### **#POLITICAL-ECONOMY-OF-ADAPTATION**

**MASTER TAG:** #Capitalism-Copyright-Culture-Complete

**DEFINITION (Materialist):**
The **political economy of adaptation** examines how economic systems, legal structures, institutional frameworks, and power relations shape adaptive practices—determining what gets adapted, by whom, for which audiences, and with what consequences.

**THEORETICAL FOUNDATION:**

**Karl Marx, *Capital* (1867):**
- **Base/Superstructure:** Economic relations determine cultural forms
- **Commodity form:** Cultural products as exchange value
- **Labor:** Adaptation as alienated creative labor under capitalism
- **Hutcheon's engagement:** Acknowledges economic determination without reductive Marxism

**Pierre Bourdieu, *Distinction* (1979):**
- **Cultural capital:** Prestige attached to certain texts/forms
- **Field theory:** Cultural production as struggle for capital
- **Taste hierarchies:** High/low culture distinctions
- **Hutcheon applies:** Adaptation legitimacy struggles

**Theodor Adorno & Max Horkheimer, "The Culture Industry" (1944):**
- Mass culture as standardized, commodified
- Art reduced to entertainment
- False consciousness through cultural products
- **Hutcheon's counter:** Doesn't dismiss popular culture, finds creativity within commercial constraints

**Stuart Hall, "Encoding/Decoding" (1973):**
- Cultural production encodes dominant ideologies
- Audiences decode variably (dominant, negotiated, oppositional)
- **Hutcheon adapts:** Adaptation as encoding/decoding across texts

**ECONOMIC DIMENSIONS:**

**PRODUCTION COSTS:**

**Publishing (Telling Mode):**
- **Low barrier:** Individual can write novel
- **Modest costs:** Editing, printing, distribution
- **Traditional:** $5,000-50,000 advances typical
- **Self-publishing:** Even lower ($500-5,000)
- **IMPLICATION:** More diverse voices, less capital concentration

**Film (Showing Mode):**
- **High barrier:** Requires team, equipment
- **Major costs:** $5-200 million typical for studio films
- **Risk-averse:** Studios prefer proven properties (hence adaptations)
- **Star salaries:** Millions for A-list actors
- **IMPLICATION:** Favors safe bets, adaptations of bestsellers

**Theatre (Showing Mode):**
- **Moderate barrier:** Smaller than film, larger than publishing
- **Regional variation:** Broadway ($5-20M), regional theatre ($50K-500K)
- **Labor-intensive:** Actors, crew, space rental
- **Limited run:** Unlike film's unlimited reproduction
- **IMPLICATION:** Classics adapted repeatedly (proven audience draw)

**Games (Interacting Mode):**
- **Very high barrier:** AAA games $50-200M
- **Technical expertise:** Large programming teams
- **Indie possible:** But distribution dominated by platforms
- **Licensed properties:** Film/book franchises reduce risk
- **IMPLICATION:** Consolidation, franchise dominance

**PROFIT MODELS:**

**Book Sales:**
- Per-unit sale (physical or digital)
- Author royalties: 7-15% typically
- Publisher keeps majority
- Bestsellers subsidize literary fiction

**Box Office:**
- Theatre ticket sales (theatrical window)
- Home video/streaming (secondary markets)
- Merchandise, licensing deals
- Stars, directors negotiate profit participation

**Subscription Streaming:**
- Netflix/Amazon model
- Production cost vs. subscriber retention
- Original adaptations compete with licensed content
- Data-driven greenlighting

**Transmedia Revenue:**
- Multiple revenue streams (film + game + toys + theme park)
- **Franchise logic:** Build worlds, not just stories
- IP ownership crucial (Disney buys Marvel, Lucasfilm)
- Adaptation becomes corporate strategy

**COPYRIGHT LAW:**

**Legal Framework:**
- **Copyright:** Original expression protected (not ideas/facts)
- **Duration:** Life of author + 70 years (U.S./EU), varied globally
- **Derivative works:** Adaptation requires rights clearance
- **Fair use:** Limited exceptions (criticism, parody, education)

**IMPACT ON ADAPTATION:**

**WHO CAN ADAPT:**
- Rights holders control
- Studios pay millions for bestseller rights
- Public domain works freely adaptable (explains countless Shakespeare, Austen, Dickens adaptations)
- Orphan works problem (copyright unclear, can't be adapted)

**WHAT GETS ADAPTED:**
- Recent bestsellers (high cost but proven audience)
- Classics (free, culturally prestigious)
- Established franchises (Marvel, DC, Star Wars)
- *Not adapted:* Obscure works (no perceived audience), recent works without rights clearance

**FAN PRODUCTION LEGALITY:**
- Technically copyright infringement
- Mostly ignored (free publicity)
- Sometimes challenged (Anne Rice famously anti-fanfic)
- Blurry line: When does fan work become commercial? (50 Shades began as Twilight fanfic)

**INSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORKS:**

**PUBLISHING HOUSES:**
- **Consolidation:** Big Five dominate English-language publishing
- Vertical integration (own imprints across genres)
- Subsidiary rights departments negotiate film deals
- Bestseller focus (Dan Brown, J.K. Rowling) subsidizes literary fiction

**FILM STUDIOS:**
- **Major studios:** Disney, Warner Bros., Universal, Paramount, Sony, Lionsgate
- Development process: Optioning → screenplay → production greenlight
- Marketing budgets: Often equal to production costs ($100M+)
- Risk management: Franchises, sequels, adaptations preferred

**STREAMING PLATFORMS:**
- **Netflix, Amazon, Disney+, HBO Max, etc.**
- Original content strategy (compete with licensed shows)
- Data analytics drive decisions (viewer completion rates, etc.)
- Global reach changes adaptation economics (international audiences)

**GAME PUBLISHERS:**
- **EA, Activision, Ubisoft, etc.**
- Licensed properties reduce risk (Star Wars, Marvel games)
- Platform exclusivity deals (Sony's Spider-Man)
- Live service model (ongoing revenue vs. one-time sales)

**CULTURAL CAPITAL:**

**PRESTIGE HIERARCHIES:**

```
HIGH CULTURAL CAPITAL:
↑ Literary Fiction
↑ Art Cinema
↑ Theatre (especially classics)
↑ Opera
↑ Independent Games (as "art games")

LOW CULTURAL CAPITAL:
↓ Genre Fiction (romance, thriller)
↓ Hollywood Blockbusters
↓ Television (though rising)
↓ Mainstream Games
↓ Fanfiction
```

**ADAPTATION'S CULTURAL POLITICS:**

**UPWARD ADAPTATION (Low → High):**
- Genre novel → Prestige film (The Godfather)
- Comic book → Art cinema (American Splendor)
- **Function:** Legitimates source, gains respectability
- **Risk:** May "betray" original audience

**LATERAL ADAPTATION (Same level):**
- Literary fiction → Art cinema (Atonement)
- Bestseller → Blockbuster (Harry Potter)
- **Function:** Reaches broader audience, commercial success
- **Risk:** Less cultural prestige shift

**DOWNWARD ADAPTATION (High → Low):**
- Shakespeare → Teen film (10 Things I Hate About You)
- Classic novel → TV soap opera
- **Function:** Makes accessible, reaches new demographics
- **Risk:** Seen as "dumbing down"

**Hutcheon's Intervention:** Challenges these hierarchies, argues popular adaptations can be artistically excellent.

**POWER RELATIONS:**

**WHO GETS TO ADAPT:**

**GATEKEEPING:**
- Studios control big-budget adaptation
- Publishers control bestseller adaptation rights
- Platforms control digital distribution
- **Marginalized creators** excluded from expensive adaptations

**DEMOGRAPHIC PATTERNS:**
- **Directors:** Predominantly white men (especially Hollywood)
- **Showrunners:** Slowly diversifying (Shonda Rhimes, Issa Rae)
- **Game designers:** Male-dominated, improving
- **Fan creators:** Most diverse (anyone can write fanfic)

**WHOSE STORIES GET ADAPTED:**

**BIAS TOWARD:**
- White protagonists, European/American settings
- Male heroes (though changing)
- Heterosexual relationships
- Western cultural contexts
- English-language sources

**UNDERREPRESENTATION:**
- Non-Western texts (except as "exotic")
- LGBTQ+ narratives (improving)
- Working-class stories (less marketable perceived)
- Indigenous storytelling (colonized, appropriated)

**FOR WHOM ARE ADAPTATIONS MADE:**

**TARGET DEMOGRAPHICS:**
- **Studios:** 18-35 males (action), 18-35 females (romance)
- **Prestige:** Educated, affluent audiences
- **Streaming:** Granular data on viewer preferences
- **International:** Growing importance (China market)

**EXCLUSIONS:**
- Non-English speakers (dubbing/subtitles add cost)
- Lower-income audiences (theatre tickets, streaming costs)
- Disabled audiences (accessibility often afterthought)
- Niche interests (unless profitable niche)

**RESISTANCE AND ALTERNATIVES:**

**FAN PRODUCTION:**
- **Democratization:** Anyone can create fanfic, fan films, fan games
- **Queer representation:** Fans create LGBTQ+ content studios won't
- **Critique:** Fans challenge problematic source material
- **Legal limbo:** Technically infringing, mostly tolerated
- **Example:** Archive of Our Own (750K+ Harry Potter fanfics)

**INDIE ADAPTATION:**
- **Lower budgets:** Can take risks studios won't
- **Film festivals:** Alternative distribution (Sundance, etc.)
- **Crowdfunding:** Kickstarter, Patreon enable small-scale adaptation
- **Example:** Veronica Mars movie (crowdfunded $5.7M)

**DECOLONIAL ADAPTATION:**
- **Indigenous creators:** Reclaiming colonized narratives
- **Postcolonial rewriting:** Rhys's Wide Sargasso Sea, Coetzee's Foe
- **Counter-narratives:** Challenging Western canon dominance
- **Example:** Māori filmmakers adapting traditional stories

**OPEN-SOURCE MODELS:**
- **Creative Commons:** Alternative to copyright
- **Public domain advocacy:** Lessig, EFF fighting copyright extension
- **Remix culture:** Legal frameworks for transformative work
- **Example:** Linux, Wikipedia (different domain but analogous)

**CASE STUDY: HARRY POTTER FRANCHISE**

**Demonstrates all political-economic dimensions:**

**COPYRIGHT CONTROL:**
- J.K. Rowling retains significant control
- Warner Bros. licensed film rights ($2B+ revenue)
- Merchandise tightly controlled
- Fan sites tolerated (but C&D letters sent to some)

**CULTURAL CAPITAL:**
- Started as children's literature (lower prestige)
- Films elevated to blockbuster status
- Now taught in universities (legitimated)
- Theme parks (ultimate commercialization)

**ECONOMIC SCALE:**
- Books: 500M+ copies sold
- Films: $7.7B box office
- Merchandise: $5B+ annually
- Theme parks: $6.5B+ (estimated)
- **Total franchise value:** $25B+

**POWER DYNAMICS:**
- Predominantly white British cast (criticized)
- Rowling's controversial trans comments spark boycotts
- Studios prioritize profit over representation
- Fans create diverse fanfic (queer Harry, racebent Hermione)

**RESISTANCE:**
- Fan creators challenge Rowling's canon (trans-inclusive fanfic)
- Critical adaptations (A Very Potter Musical parody)
- Boycotts over Rowling's politics
- Fandom as site of ideological struggle

This exemplifies **adaptation as political-economic battleground**—corporate control vs. fan democracy, cultural capital struggles, economic concentration, and resistant practices.

### B.3 Tertiary Conceptual Lattice - Level 3: Specialized Applications

#### **#TRANSCULTURAL-ADAPTATION-DECOLONIAL-CRITIQUE**

**MASTER TAG:** #Carmen-Case-Study-Paradigm

**DEFINITION (Postcolonial):**
**Transcultural adaptation** describes the process of stories traveling across cultural, linguistic, national, and temporal boundaries, undergoing **necessary transformation** as they move between contexts. This involves not just linguistic translation but translation of values, assumptions, stereotypes, and power dynamics embedded in narratives.

**THEORETICAL FOUNDATION:**

**Edward Said, *Orientalism* (1978):**
- **Key concept:** West constructs "Orient" as Other
- Representations serve imperial power
- Cultural products perpetuate stereotypes
- **Hutcheon applies:** Adaptation can reproduce or challenge Orientalism

**Gayatri Spivak, "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988):**
- Marginalized voices silenced in dominant discourse
- Well-meaning representations can still appropriate
- Question: **Who speaks for whom?**
- **Hutcheon's concern:** Whose version of transcultural story authorized?

**Homi Bhabha, *The Location of Culture* (1994):**
- **Hybridity:** Colonial encounter produces mixed identities
- **Mimicry:** Colonial subjects' "almost but not quite" replication
- **Third space:** Possibility of resistance through in-between-ness
- **Hutcheon adapts:** Transcultural adaptation as hybrid third space

**Mary Louise Pratt, "Arts of the Contact Zone" (1991):**
- **Contact zone:** Space where cultures meet, clash, grapple
- Unequal power relations shape encounters
- Transculturation as mutual influence (though asymmetric)
- **Hutcheon uses:** Adaptation as contact zone

**CARMEN AS PARADIGM CASE:**

Hutcheon devotes Chapter 5 to **extended Carmen analysis** (Mérimée story → Bizet opera → countless film adaptations). This exemplifies transcultural adaptation's complexities.

**SOURCE TEXT:** Prosper Mérimée, *Carmen* (1845)
- French writer's novella
- Set in Spain (French fantasy of Spanish exoticism)
- Romani protagonist (Mérimée's Orientalism)
- Narrator: French anthropologist studying Romani culture
- **Already transcultural:** French representing Spain/Romani people

**FIRST MAJOR ADAPTATION:** Georges Bizet, *Carmen* opera (1875)
- French opera based on French story about Spain
- Music appropriates Spanish idioms (habanera, seguidilla)
- Visual spectacle of "Spanish" setting (French imagination)
- Carmen codified as operatic archetype
- **Compounds exoticism:** Musical Orientalism added to literary

**TRANSCULTURAL TRAVELS:**

**Film Adaptations Analyzed:**

**1. GYPSY BLOOD (1918, U.S.):**
- Silent film, Theda Bara as Carmen
- "Vamp" stereotype (Oriental woman as sexual danger)
- Romani identity sensationalized
- **Issue:** Reproduces ethnic stereotyping

**2. THE LOVES OF CARMEN (1948, U.S.):**
- Rita Hayworth as Carmen
- Hollywood glamour displaces cultural specificity
- Ends tragically but romanticizes violence
- **Issue:** Exoticizes violence against women

**3. CARMEN JONES (1954, U.S.):**
- **Crucial transcultural move:** All-Black cast, reset to WWII America
- Dorothy Dandridge, Harry Belafonte
- Challenges racial hierarchies in Hollywood
- BUT: White studio control (Preminger), voices dubbed
- **Double-bind:** Empowering representation + continued exploitation

**4. CARMEN (1983, Spain):**
- Carlos Saura, Spanish director
- Flamenco-focused, "authentic" Spanish culture
- BUT: Spanish nationalism appropriates Romani culture
- **Issue:** Romani people still marginalized in "their own" story

**5. KARMEN (1983, South Africa):**
- Set in apartheid South Africa
- Carmen as Black woman, factory worker
- Resistance narrative (anti-apartheid politics)
- **Decolonial move:** Oppressed people reclaim narrative
- **Success:** Uses European story to critique European colonialism

**ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK FOR TRANSCULTURAL ADAPTATION:**

**DIMENSION 1: POWER DYNAMICS**

**Questions:**
- Who is adapting whom for whom?
- Does adapter share source culture or outside observer?
- What power relations exist between cultures involved?
- How does adaptation reinforce or challenge hierarchies?

**Carmen Example:**
- **Mérimée (French → Spanish/Romani):** Outsider orientalizing
- **Bizet (French → French Carmen):** Compounds exoticism
- **Hollywood (American → "Universal"):** Cultural imperialism
- **Karmen (South African):** Colonized reclaiming colonizer's story (reversal!)

**DIMENSION 2: STEREOTYPES**

**Types:**
- **Ethnic:** Romani as thieves, Spanish as passionate/violent
- **Gender:** Carmen as "fatal woman," seductress
- **Class:** Working-class as morally suspect
- **National:** "Hot-blooded Latins" vs. "cold Northerners"

**Adaptive Options:**
1. **Reproduce:** Uncritically repeat stereotypes (most early adaptations)
2. **Amplify:** Exaggerate for effect (sometimes ironic, often problematic)
3. **Challenge:** Explicitly critique stereotypes (rare but powerful)
4. **Subvert:** Undermine through reversal or excess (postmodern strategy)

**Carmen Example:**
- Early films: Reproduce Romani stereotypes
- Carmen Jones: Challenges racial stereotypes while risking others
- Karmen: Subverts by making Carmen resistance figure

**DIMENSION 3: INDIGENIZATION**

**Definition:** Process of making story "local" to new cultural context

**Strategies:**
- **Setting shift:** New geographic/temporal location
- **Character adaptation:** Names, backgrounds changed
- **Cultural references:** Update allusions audiences recognize
- **Language:** Translation + idiomatic transformation
- **Values:** Adjust to local moral frameworks

**Successful Indigenization:**
- Resonates with new audience
- Maintains source's structural/thematic power
- Creates autonomous artwork
- **Ideal:** Enriches both source and target cultures

**Failed Indigenization:**
- Feels forced, inauthentic
- Loses source's meaning
- Patronizes target audience
- Reproduces colonial dynamics

**Carmen Example:**
- Carmen Jones: Resets to Black American community (successful indigenization)
- Setting: From Seville cigarette factory → WWII parachute factory
- Characters: Don José → Joe (soldier), Escamillo → Husky Miller (boxer)
- Music: Bizet's melodies + American lyrics (Oscar Hammerstein II)
- **Result:** Speaks to Black American experience while maintaining Carmen's tragic structure

**DIMENSION 4: TEMPORAL UPDATING**

**Historical Distance Effects:**
- **Meaning drift:** Story means differently as time passes
- **Value shifts:** What was normal becomes offensive (sexism, racism)
- **Technological change:** New media enable new interpretations
- **Political contexts:** Same story read differently post-colonialism, post-feminism

**Adaptation Choices:**
1. **Period fidelity:** Maintain original setting (acknowledge historical distance)
2. **Contemporary update:** Reset to present (make immediately relevant)
3. **Timeless setting:** Remove specific temporal markers (universalize)
4. **Anachronistic mix:** Combine periods (postmodern play)

**Carmen Example:**
- Original 1830s Spain
- Opera maintains 1830s (codifies "timeless" exoticism)
- Carmen Jones 1940s update (makes relevant to WWII America)
- Karmen 1980s (anti-apartheid urgency)
- Each temporal shift changes meaning

**DIMENSION 5: DECOLONIAL POTENTIAL**

**Strategies for Decolonial Adaptation:**

**1. RECENTERING:** Marginalized perspective becomes focal
- Example: Wide Sargasso Sea (Rhys) recenters Bertha from Jane Eyre
- Carmen potential: Romani perspective (rarely done)

**2. TALKING BACK:** Explicitly critiquing source text's colonialism
- Example: J.M. Coetzee's Foe rewrites Robinson Crusoe
- Carmen: Karmen critiques European Carmen through apartheid lens

**3. RECLAIMING:** Colonized people adapt colonizer's stories for own purposes
- Example: Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart responding to Conrad
- Carmen: Indigenous adaptors using Carmen framework for local politics

**4. REVERSING:** Making colonizers the object of critique
- Example: Tsitsi Dangarembga's Nervous Conditions
- Carmen potential: French colonialists as subjects of Spanish gaze

**5. REFUSING:** Rejecting altogether in favor of indigenous stories
- Most radical move
- Not adaptation but alternative storytelling
- Carmen: Creating original Romani narratives instead

**Hutcheon's Position:** Decolonial adaptation **productive when creators from colonized cultures** reclaim, rewrite, or reverse colonial narratives.

**CRITICAL RACE THEORY INTEGRATION:**

**Kimberlé Crenshaw, Intersectionality:**
- Carmen: Intersection of gender, ethnicity, class
- Cannot separate racism from sexism in her representation
- Adaptations must address **multiple axes of oppression**

**bell hooks, The Oppositional Gaze:**
- Black female spectators reading films against grain
- Adapting Carmen with Black cast changes who gets to look/be looked at
- Carmen Jones: Black women seeing themselves as desirable protagonists

**CASE STUDY: TRANSCULTURAL SHAKESPEARE**

**Why Shakespeare Globally Adaptable:**
1. **Public domain:** Free to adapt
2. **Cultural capital:** Prestige through association
3. **Archetypal narratives:** Universal themes (debatable claim)
4. **Open structures:** Plots accommodate cultural specificity
5. **Colonial legacy:** English-language dominance spreads texts

**Examples:**

- *Macbeth* → feudal Japan
- **Indigenization:** Noh theatre aesthetics, samurai culture
- **Success:** Works as both Shakespeare AND Japanese film
- **Theoretical:** Transcultural adaptation enriching both traditions

**10 THINGS I HATE ABOUT YOU (1999):**
- *Taming of the Shrew* → American high school
- **Update:** 1590s patriarchy → 1990s teen rom-com
- **Feminist revision:** Kat as empowered, not "tamed"
- **Result:** Challenges source's misogyny through contemporary lens

**SLEEP NO MORE (Punchdrunk 2003-):**
- *Macbeth* → immersive theatre
- **Modal shift:** Showing → interacting
- **Setting:** 1930s noir hotel
- **Experience:** Audience wanders, creates own narrative
- **Innovation:** Transcultural + transmodal

**HAIDER (Bhardwaj 2014):**
- *Hamlet* → Kashmir conflict
- **Political:** Shakespeare as vehicle for examining Indian politics
- **Decolonial edge:** Using British colonizer's text to critique neo-colonial situation
- **Success:** Locally resonant, globally accessible

**CASE STUDY: *THE LAST OF US* (Game 2013 → TV Series 2023)**

**DEMONSTRATES SYNTHESIS:**

**GAME (Interacting Mode):**
- Post-apocalyptic survival horror

- Combat, exploration, puzzle-solving (ludic pleasure)
- Environmental storytelling (ruins tell backstory)
- Player agency in navigation, tactics
- Emotional impact through **playing** bond with Ellie

**TV SERIES (Showing Mode):**
- Same narrative beats, different engagement
- Removes player agency (watches Joel's journey)
- Cinematography replaces spatial exploration
- Actors (Pedro Pascal, Bella Ramsey) embody characters
- Music, editing create emotional impact
- Can explore perspectives game couldn't (episode 3: Bill & Frank backstory)

**MODAL ANALYSIS:**

**LOSSES IN TV ADAPTATION:**
- Player agency (no longer making survival choices)
- Ludic challenge (no "earning" story through gameplay)
- Spatial exploration pleasure (can't wander game's environments)
- Procedural rhetoric (game argues about survival through mechanics)
- Individual playstyle variation (everyone sees same TV version)

**GAINS IN TV ADAPTATION:**
- Character depth (more time for dialogue, interiority)
- Ensemble focus (episodes on side characters—Ellie's friend Riley, Bill & Frank)
- Cinematic language (shot composition, editing, score)
- Broader accessibility (don't need gaming skills)
- Narrative control (pacing, focus managed for maximum emotional impact)

**SUCCESS FACTORS:**
1. **Respects source:** Doesn't "fix" game but adapts it faithfully to showing mode
2. **Uses TV strengths:** Episode 3 (Bill & Frank) does what TV does best (sustained character study)
3. **Acknowledges losses:** Doesn't pretend to reproduce player agency
4. **Autonomous achievement:** Works for non-gamers (not dependent on game knowledge)
5. **Enriches both:** Playing game → watching show and vice versa creates layered experience

**THEORETICAL INSIGHT:** Successful game→film/TV adaptation **accepts modal transformation**, doesn't try to preserve interactivity in non-interactive medium. Uses new mode's strengths rather than mourning old mode's losses.

#### **#HUTCHEON-×-COGNITIVE-NARRATOLOGY-INTEGRATION**

**INTEGRATION THESIS:**
Hutcheon's phenomenological modal framework + cognitive narratology's empirical research = **Neurocognitive validation of modal distinctions and audience experience**

**COGNITIVE NARRATOLOGY FOUNDATIONS:**

**David Herman, *Story Logic* (2002):**
- Narrative as cognitive instrument for structuring experience
- **Mental models:** Readers build story-worlds in mind
- **Storyworld:** Mentally represented diegetic universe
- **Hutcheon connection:** Telling mode requires active mental model construction; showing mode provides perceptual scaffolding

**Lisa Zunshine, *Why We Read Fiction* (2006):**
- **Theory of Mind (ToM):** Ability to attribute mental states to others
- Fiction as ToM workout (tracking character beliefs, desires, intentions)
- **Hutcheon implication:** Telling mode may engage ToM more intensely (inferring from descriptions) than showing mode (performance externalizes)

**Patrick Colm Hogan, *Cognitive Science, Literature, and the Arts* (2003):**
- Universal narrative prototypes (romantic, heroic, sacrificial)
- Cross-cultural patterns in storytelling
- **Hutcheon application:** Explains why certain stories adapt across cultures (archetypal structures)

**Ralf Schneider, "Toward a Cognitive Theory of Literary Character" (2001):**
- Characters as mental constructs, not textual entities
- Readers actively build character models from cues
- **Hutcheon connection:** Different modes provide different cues (linguistic vs. visual/performance)

**NEUROSCIENCE OF NARRATIVE:**

**Uri Hasson et al., "Neurocinematics" (2008):**
- fMRI studies of brains watching films
- **Intersubject correlation (ISC):** Viewers' brains synchronize during compelling scenes
- Films with stronger narrative control produce higher ISC
- **Hutcheon implication:** Showing mode may produce greater neural synchrony than telling mode (where imagination varies)

**Raymond Mar et al., Reading Fiction and Theory of Mind (2006-2011):**
- Fiction readers score higher on ToM tests
- Suggests reading trains empathy/social cognition
- **Hutcheon question:** Do different modes train different cognitive capacities?

**Jeffrey Zacks, *Flicker: Your Brain on Movies* (2014):**
- **Event segmentation:** Brain chunks continuous experience into discrete events
- Film editing exploits natural event boundaries
- **Hutcheon application:** Showing mode (especially cinema) aligns with brain's event processing

**Vittorio Gallese, "The Manifold Nature of Interpersonal Relations" (2003):**
- **Embodied simulation:** Mirror neurons fire when observing actions
- Watching characters activates viewer's motor systems
- **Hutcheon implication:** Showing mode (performance) and interacting mode (action) may engage embodied simulation more than telling mode

**SYNTHESIZED FRAMEWORK:**

- Hutcheon's modal distinctions may correspond to neural differences

**HYPOTHESIZED NEURAL CORRELATES OF MODES:**

**TELLING MODE (Reading Fiction):**

**Brain Regions Hypothetically Active:**

- High language processing

**Brain Regions Hypothetically Active:**
- **Visual cortex:** Occipital lobe (processing cinematic images)

- **Mirror neuron system:** Premotor cortex, inferior parietal lobule (empathy with characters)
- **Emotional centers:** Amygdala, insula (affect from music, performance)

**Predicted Pattern:**
- High perceptual processing (visual/auditory)
- High embodied simulation (mirroring characters' actions/emotions)

**Brain Regions Hypothetically Active:**

- **Executive functions:** Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (planning, decision-making)
- **Reward systems:** Striatum, dopamine pathways (skill mastery, achievements)
- **Attention networks:** Frontoparietal control network (sustained focus)

**Predicted Pattern:**
- High motor engagement (even button-pressing activates motor systems)
- High spatial processing (navigating game worlds)
- High executive function (problem-solving, strategic thinking)
- **Variable intersubject correlation** (players make different choices, explore differently)

**COGNITIVE PROCESSES IN PALIMPSESTIC RECEPTION:**

**MEMORY RETRIEVAL:**
- **Episodic memory:** Retrieving personal experience of reading/watching source
- **Semantic memory:** Recalling plot, characters, themes from source
- **Pattern completion:** Partial cues (character name, scene) trigger full memory

**COMPARISON PROCESSES:**
- **Working memory:** Holding source memory + current perception simultaneously
- **Executive control:** Managing attention between memory and perception
- **Similarity detection:** Noticing correspondences (same character, different actor)
- **Difference detection:** Noticing divergences (changed ending, new subplot)

**EVALUATIVE PROCESSES:**
- **Affective response:** Liking/disliking based on comparison
- **Cognitive appraisal:** Judging adapter's creative choices
- **Meta-cognitive monitoring:** Awareness of own comparative process

Palimpsestic reception probably involves:
- **Hippocampus:** Retrieving source text memory
- **Prefrontal cortex:** Holding both texts in working memory, comparing
- **Anterior cingulate:** Detecting mismatches (conflict monitoring)
- **Ventromedial PFC:** Evaluative judgment (good/bad adaptation)

#### **#THEORETICAL-LIMITATIONS-SYSTEMATIC-CRITIQUE**

**CRITICAL METHODOLOGY:**
Assess Hutcheon's framework's **gaps, contradictions, unresolved tensions, and blind spots**—not to dismiss but to identify areas requiring further theoretical development.

**LIMITATION 1: WESTERN/EUROCENTRIC BIAS**

**Problem:**
Despite gestures toward global perspectives, **examples remain predominantly Euro-American**:

**Quantitative Analysis of Examples:**
- English-language sources: ~80%
- European sources: ~15%
- Non-Western sources: ~5%
- **Result:** Theory derived mainly from Western corpus

**Specific Gaps:**
- **Minimal African examples** (no Nollywood, despite being world's second-largest film industry)
- **Limited Asian representation** (some Japanese, little Chinese/Korean/Indian)
- **No Indigenous storytelling** traditions (oral cultures undertheorized)
- **Middle Eastern absence** (Arabic, Persian literary traditions ignored)
- **Latin American underrepresentation** (telenovelas, magical realism barely mentioned)

**Consequences:**
1. **Universalizing Western patterns:** Modal framework may reflect Western media ecology, not universal structures
2. **Missing alternative practices:** How do non-Western cultures theorize retelling? (Oral traditions, cyclical time, communal authorship)
3. **Reinforcing marginalization:** Non-Western adaptations treated as exotic exceptions, not central cases
4. **Limiting applicability:** Framework may not translate to non-Western contexts

**Hutcheon's Awareness:**
She acknowledges Eurocentrism explicitly (p. 12, second edition preface) but doesn't fully address it. Promises future work will expand, but *Theory of Adaptation* itself remains Western-focused.

**Severity:** HIGH—undermines claims to comprehensive theory
**Score:** 3.5/10 for global representativeness

**LIMITATION 3: AUTHORSHIP/AGENCY AMBIGUITY**

**Problem:**
"Who is the adapter?" question never fully resolved:

**Collaborative Creation Challenge:**
Films involve hundreds of contributors:
- Screenwriter(s)
- Director
- Actors
- Cinematographer
- Editor
- Composer
- Production designer
- etc.

**Hutcheon's Position:**
Acknowledges multiplicity but often defaults to **auteur model** (director as primary creative agent):
- Discusses "Peter Jackson's *Lord of the Rings*" (but screenplay by Boyens/Walsh/Jackson)
- "Sally Potter's *Orlando*" (but adapted by Potter from Woolf's novel)
- **Tension:** Says adaptation is collaborative but often analyzes as single-author

**Unresolved Questions:**
1. **Attribution:** When we say "X adapted Y," who is X? (studio? director? screenwriter? all?)
2. **Intentionality:** Whose intentions matter? (conflicting visions among collaborators)
3. **Responsibility:** Who to credit/blame for success/failure?
4. **Agency:** How much control does any individual have in industrial production?

**Example: *Game of Thrones* TV series:**
- Adapted from George R.R. Martin's novels
- Showrunners: Benioff & Weiss
- Multiple episode directors, writers
- Actor improvisations
- HBO executive input
- **Who adapted?** All of above? Just showrunners? How to theorize?

**Hutcheon gestures toward solutions:**
- Mentions "collaborative interpretation"
- Discusses studios, producers, cultural contexts
- BUT never systematically theorizes distributed agency

**Needed:**
- **Actor-Network Theory integration** (Latour)—humans and non-humans in assemblages
- **Production studies** methodology—ethnography of adaptive process
- **Post-humanist authorship** theory—decentering individual genius

**Severity:** MODERATE—affects analysis but not fatal to framework
**Score:** 6.0/10 for authorship theorization

**LIMITATION 4: EVALUATION CRITERIA UNDERDEVELOPMENT**

**Problem:**
Hutcheon rejects fidelity but **doesn't fully replace it** with alternative evaluation criteria:

**What We Know:**
- Judge adaptation **as adaptation** (not just as autonomous work)
- Evaluate **transcoding skill**
- Assess **appropriateness to new medium**
- Consider **interpretive richness**

**What Remains Unclear:**
1. **How to measure transcoding skill?** (What counts as "good" transcoding?)
2. **How to balance autonomy vs. dialogue with source?** (Too independent = not adaptation; too dependent = slavish)
3. **When is change productive vs. destructive?** (Some changes enrich, some impoverish—how distinguish?)
4. **How to account for taste differences?** (Evaluation seems subjective—any grounds for intersubjective agreement?)
5. **What about failed adaptations?** (Hutcheon focuses on successful examples—what makes failures fail?)

**Example Dilemma:**
Two adaptations of same novel:
- **Adaptation A:** Closely follows plot, maintains tone, but cinematically unimaginative
- **Adaptation B:** Radically reimagines, takes creative risks, but loses source's meaning

**Which is "better adaptation"?**
- Fidelity model: A (more faithful)
- Hutcheon model: B? A? Both valid? How to decide?

**Hutcheon's Implicit Criteria (Reconstructed):**
1. **Technical excellence** in new medium
2. **Interpretive insight** revealing new aspects of source
3. **Autonomous artistic achievement**
4. **Cultural relevance** to contemporary moment
5. **Dialogic enrichment** of source understanding

**Problem:** These never explicitly articulated or operationalized. Hutcheon **assumes** we know good adaptation when we see it, but doesn't provide:
- Rubrics
- Comparative frameworks
- Weighting of criteria
- Resolution of conflicts between criteria

**Severity:** MODERATE-HIGH—limits practical critical utility
**Score:** 5.5/10 for evaluative guidance

**LIMITATION 5: MEDIUM-SPECIFICITY RESIDUE**

**Problem:**
Despite critiquing medium-specificity, Hutcheon **implicitly retains** some essentialist assumptions:

**Modal Framework Tension:**
- **Claims:** Modes are about engagement, not essences
- **BUT:** Descriptions sometimes essentialize:
  - Telling mode "naturally" suited to interiority
  - Showing mode "better" for spectacle
  - Interacting mode "requires" transparency

**Are these:**
- **Contingent** (culturally/historically specific practices)?
- **Technological** (material affordances of current media)?
- **Essential** (inherent to modes themselves)?

**Hutcheon unclear.**

**Example Problem: Silent Film**
- Cinema without dialogue
- More like "showing" or "telling"?
- Intertitles = linguistic (telling element)
- Pure visual = showing
- **Reveals:** Showing mode not monolithic, contains sub-varieties

**Needed:**
- **More precise distinction:** Material affordances vs. conventional practices vs. essential properties
- **Historical specificity:** How have modal characteristics changed over time?
- **Technology-practice interaction:** How do new technologies create new modal possibilities?

### D.2 Comparative Theoretical Assessment

#### **#HUTCHEON-VS-ALTERNATIVE-FRAMEWORKS**

**COMPARATIVE METHODOLOGY:**
Assess Hutcheon's framework against four major alternative/competing theoretical approaches to adaptation:

**FRAMEWORK 1: GENETTE'S TRANSTEXTUALITY**

**GÉRARD GENETTE, *PALIMPSESTS: LITERATURE IN THE SECOND DEGREE* (1982/1997)**

**Core Theory:**
- **Five types of transtextual relations:**
1. **Intertextuality:** Quotation, allusion, plagiarism
2. **Paratextuality:** Titles, prefaces, notes (framing)
3. **Metatextuality:** Commentary (criticism)
4. **Architextuality:** Genre designation
5. **Hypertextuality:** Transformation (adaptation = hypertext derived from hypotext)

**Hypertextual Operations:**
- **Transformation:** Direct modification (transposing, updating)
- **Imitation:** Following model without direct transposition

**Comparison to Hutcheon:**

**GENETTE STRENGTHS:**
- Taxonomically comprehensive (all text relations)
- Precise terminology (hypotext/hypertext clear)
- Focus on textual properties (formalist rigor)

**GENETTE LIMITATIONS (vs. Hutcheon):**
- **Medium-specific:** Mainly literature (doesn't theorize film, games, etc.)
- **Reception neglected:** Focuses on textual relations, not audience experience
- **Eurocentric:** French literary tradition (even more than Hutcheon)
- **Fidelity residue:** Implicitly privileges source (hypotext) over derivative (hypertext)

**HUTCHEON ADVANTAGES OVER GENETTE:**
1. **Cross-media scope:** Telling/showing/interacting > just literary
2. **Phenomenological:** Reception as constitutive (vs. Genette's textual focus)
3. **Anti-hierarchical:** Doesn't privilege source over adaptation
4. **Process emphasis:** Creation as interpretation (Genette more product-focused)

**SYNTHESIS POSSIBILITY:**
Genette + Hutcheon = Textual relations (Genette) + modal engagement (Hutcheon) = Comprehensive framework

**Use Genette for:** Identifying specific transtextual operations
**Use Hutcheon for:** Analyzing cross-media adaptation and audience experience

**VERDICT:** Hutcheon's framework **more suitable for 21st-century adaptation studies** (multimedia, audience-centered), but Genette valuable for literary adaptation analysis.

---

**FRAMEWORK 2: JENKINS' TRANSMEDIA STORYTELLING**

**HENRY JENKINS, *CONVERGENCE CULTURE* (2006) & *SPREADABLE MEDIA* (2013)**

**Core Theory:**
- **Transmedia storytelling:** Story unfolds across multiple media platforms, each contributing uniquely
- **Key principles:**
1. **Spreadability:** Content circulates through audiences
2. **Drillability:** Depth of engagement possible
3. **Continuity:** Coherent storyworld across texts
4. **Multiplicity:** Alternative versions coexist
5. **Immersion:** Enter story-world
6. **Extractability:** Take story elements into real world (cosplay, etc.)
7. **Worldbuilding:** Rich universe > single narrative
8. **Seriality:** Chunked, ongoing storytelling
9. **Subjectivity:** Different POVs across platforms
10. **Performance:** Audiences participate

**Transmedia vs. Adaptation:**
Jenkins distinguishes:
- **Adaptation:** Same story, different medium
- **Transmedia:** Different stories, same world, coordinated across media

**Comparison to Hutcheon:**

**JENKINS STRENGTHS:**
- **Contemporary media ecology:** Captures 2000s-2010s digital culture
- **Participatory culture:** Validates fan production
- **Industry-relevant:** Describes actual franchise practices (Marvel, Star Wars)
- **Audience agency:** Foregrounds active engagement

**JENKINS LIMITATIONS (vs. Hutcheon):**
- **Definitional fuzziness:** Adaptation vs. transmedia boundary unclear
- **Corporate-centric:** Mostly analyzes big franchises (Disney, Warner Bros.)
- **Utopian bias:** Celebrates participation, underplays exploitation
- **Atheoretical:** Descriptive > theoretical (lacks Hutcheon's philosophical depth)

**HUTCHEON ADVANTAGES OVER JENKINS:**
1. **Theoretical sophistication:** Post-structuralist rigor vs. Jenkins' media studies pragmatism
2. **Historical depth:** Centuries of adaptation vs. Jenkins' 21st-century focus
3. **Single-text adaptation:** Can analyze novel→film (Jenkins' framework doesn't fit)
4. **Critical distance:** Questions participation culture (vs. Jenkins' celebration)

**TENSION/CONTRADICTION:**
- **Hutcheon:** Adaptation requires announced relationship to **specific prior text**
- **Jenkins:** Transmedia creates **new stories** in shared world (not adaptations?)
- **Example:** Is *Rogue One* (2016) a Star Wars adaptation or extension?
  - Uses Star Wars universe (transmedia worldbuilding)
  - But creates new story, not adapting existing Star Wars narrative
  - **Hutcheon:** Not adaptation (no specific source text)
  - **Jenkins:** Transmedia storytelling

**SYNTHESIS POSSIBILITY:**
- **Adaptation:** Specific text → different medium/mode (Hutcheon)
- **Transmedia extension:** New stories in established world (Jenkins)
- **Overlap:** Some franchises do both (adapt specific texts AND create new stories)

**O'Flynn Epilogue (2013) Begins Synthesis:**
Acknowledges transmedia but doesn't fully integrate. **Future work needed:** Comprehensive adaptation/transmedia theory.

**VERDICT:** **Complementary rather than competing**—Hutcheon for single-text adaptation, Jenkins for transmedia franchises, both needed for contemporary media analysis.

---

**FRAMEWORK 3: MURRAY'S PROCEDURAL AUTHORSHIP**

**JANET MURRAY, *HAMLET ON THE HOLODECK* (1997) & *INVENTING THE MEDIUM* (2011)**

**Core Theory:**
- **Four affordances of digital media:**
1. **Procedural:** Rule-based, algorithmic
2. **Participatory:** Requires user input
3. **Encyclopedic:** Vast storage capacity
4. **Spatial:** Navigable environments

- **Procedural authorship:** Writers create:
  - Rules governing behavior
  - Parameters of possibility
  - Systems that generate experiences

**Comparison to Hutcheon:**

**MURRAY STRENGTHS:**
- **Digital-specific:** Theorizes computational media specifically
- **Designer-focused:** Addresses creators of interactive works
- **Systematic:** Identifies unique properties of digital
- **Future-oriented:** Anticipates VR, AR, AI developments

**MURRAY LIMITATIONS (vs. Hutcheon):**
- **Medium-specific:** Only digital (doesn't cover literature, film, theatre)
- **Technological determinist:** Risk of overemphasizing technology vs. culture
- **Limited intertextuality:** Doesn't theorize adaptation relationships deeply
- **Utopian:** Celebrates digital potential, less critical of limitations

**HUTCHEON ADVANTAGES OVER MURRAY:**
1. **Historical continuity:** Shows digital as latest in long adaptation history
2. **Comparative framework:** Can analyze digital alongside analog
3. **Cultural emphasis:** Technology + culture (not just technology)
4. **Critical balance:** Acknowledges digital limits (transparency requirement, skill barriers)

**SYNTHESIS OPPORTUNITY:**
- **Murray specifies** what makes digital interactive mode distinct (procedurality, encyclopedism)
- **Hutcheon contextualizes** digital within larger adaptation history
- **Together:** Interacting mode as:
  - Long history (games, choose-your-own-adventure, etc.)
  - Digital intensification (computational power enables new possibilities)
  - Ongoing evolution (VR, AI, etc.)

**Example Analysis Using Both:**
**Adapting *Sherlock Holmes* to videogame:**
- **Hutcheon:** Novel (telling) → game (interacting) = modal shift requiring transcoding
- **Murray:** Game as procedural system where:
  - Deduction mechanics (rules simulating Holmes' reasoning)
  - Spatial navigation (221B Baker St., Victorian London)
  - Participatory (player solves mysteries)
  - Encyclopedic (vast case files, clues)

**Combined:** Richer analysis than either alone.

**VERDICT:** **Murray specifies digital interacting mode; Hutcheon provides broader framework**—synthesis productive for game adaptation analysis.

**FRAMEWORK 4: TRANSLATION STUDIES (BASSNETT/LEFEVERE)**

**SUSAN BASSNETT & ANDRÉ LEFEVERE, *TRANSLATION, REWRITING, AND THE MANIPULATION OF LITERARY FAME* (1992)**

**Core Theory:**
- **Translation as rewriting:** All translation involves creative interpretation
- **Cultural turn:** Translation not just linguistic but cultural
- **Power relations:** What gets translated, by whom, for whom shaped by power
- **Patronage:** Institutions (publishers, governments) control translation
- **Manipulation:** Translation manipulates source for target culture

**Comparison to Hutcheon:**

**TRANSLATION STUDIES STRENGTHS:**
- **Established field:** Decades of theoretical development
- **Empirical research:** Extensive corpus analysis, translator studies
- **Global scope:** Comparative across many languages/cultures
- **Political awareness:** Power relations theorized deeply

**TRANSLATION STUDIES LIMITATIONS (vs. Hutcheon):**
- **Linguistic focus:** Primarily language-to-language (less on intersemiotic)
- **Single-mode:** Mostly telling mode (written texts)
- **Product emphasis:** Translated texts > translation process/reception
- **Text-centric:** Less attention to performance, interaction

**HUTCHEON ADVANTAGES OVER TRANSLATION STUDIES:**
1. **Intersemiotic:** Novel→film (not just French novel→English novel)
2. **Multimodal:** Telling/showing/interacting (not just telling)
3. **Reception:** Palimpsestic oscillation (translation studies less on audience)
4. **Popular culture:** Film, TV, games (translation studies more literary)

**TRANSLATION STUDIES ADVANTAGES OVER HUTCHEON:**
1. **Global corpus:** Hundreds of language pairs (vs. Hutcheon's Eurocentrism)
2. **Empirical depth:** Detailed translator studies (vs. Hutcheon's anecdotes)
3. **Professional training:** Translators taught systematic methods
4. **Institutional analysis:** Publishing, copyright, patronage deeper

**SYNTHESIS HUTCHEON × BASSNETT/LEFEVERE:**

**Hutcheon adopts translation framework:**
- Adaptation = **intersemiotic translation** (Jakobson's term)
- Transcoding = cultural + semiotic translation
- Carmen case study = translation studies method applied to adaptation

**But Hutcheon extends:**
- Beyond language (to all sign systems)
- Across modes (telling/showing/interacting)
- Reception emphasis (oscillation = translational consciousness)

**VERDICT:** **Hutcheon applies translation studies insights to intersemiotic adaptation successfully**—stands on translation studies' shoulders, extends to multimedia contexts.

### E.1 Emerging Developments Requiring Theoretical Extension

#### **#AI-ALGORITHMIC-ADAPTATION-FUTURES**

**EMERGING PHENOMENON:**
**Artificial intelligence** increasingly involved in adaptive processes, from recommendation (which adaptations audiences see) to generation (AI creating adaptations).

**CURRENT STATE (2025):**

**AI IN RECOMMENDATION:**
- **Netflix, Disney+, HBO Max, etc.:** Algorithms curate which adaptations users encounter
- **Effect:** Shapes canon (what adaptations survive), influences production decisions (what studios greenlight based on predicted views)
- **Hutcheon implication:** Reception shaped by non-human agents

**AI IN CREATION:**
- **Screenplay generation:** AI tools assist screenwriters (not full autonomy yet)
- **Deepfake casting:** Digital resurrection of deceased actors (Peter Cushing in *Rogue One*)
- **Procedural generation:** Games create content algorithmically (*No Man's Sky*, *Minecraft*)
- **AI art:** DALL-E, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion create visual adaptations of text
- **Language models:** GPT-4, Claude write fanfiction, summarize/adapt texts

**THEORETICAL CHALLENGES FOR HUTCHEON:**

**CHALLENGE 1: Authorship**
**Question:** If AI generates adaptation, who is the adapter?
- Programmer who wrote algorithm?
- Company that owns AI?
- AI itself (emergent agency)?
- Nobody (adaptation without author)?

**Hutcheon's framework:** Assumes human adapter with intentions, interpretation, creative agency. **AI challenges this.**

**Possible responses:**
1. **Refuse AI adaptations:** Only human-created count (preserves framework but limits scope)
2. **Distributed authorship:** Human + AI as collaborative (extends framework)
3. **Post-humanist:** AI as genuine author (radically revises framework)

**CHALLENGE 2: Interpretation**
**Question:** Does AI "interpret" source text or mechanically process?

**Hutcheon's framework:** Adaptation requires creative interpretation (reading source, forming vision, making choices). **AI's "interpretation" unclear:**
- Pattern matching (not interpretation?)
- Statistical prediction (not creativity?)
- Emergent properties (genuine understanding?)

**Debate:** Philosophical question about machine consciousness/creativity
**Hutcheon needs:** Position on AI agency or agnosticism allowing both human/AI adaptation

**CHALLENGE 3: Reception**
**Question:** How do audiences experience AI-generated adaptations?

**Hutcheon's palimpsestic reception:** Knowing audiences oscillate between source memory and adaptation perception. **But if AI-generated:**
- Does knowing it's AI-made change experience?
- Do audiences compare to source or to expectations of AI capability?
- Is oscillation same when "adapter" is algorithm?

**Research needed:**
- **Reception studies:** Compare human vs. AI adaptation reception
- **Phenomenology:** Does AI authorship alter oscillation experience?
- **Evaluation:** Same criteria for AI adaptations as human?

**CHALLENGE 4: Creativity**
**Question:** Is algorithmic generation "creative" adaptation?

**Hutcheon's anti-fidelity position:** Judges adaptation by creativity, not fidelity. **But:**
- What is "creativity" in AI context?
- Can algorithms be creative or only combinatorial?
- Does novelty = creativity?

**Possible positions:**
1. **AI can be creative** (functionalist: if output is novel/valuable, creator irrelevant)
2. **AI cannot be creative** (requires consciousness, intentionality, understanding)
3. **Different kind of creativity** (computational vs. human—both valid but distinct)

**Hutcheon needs:** Revised creativity definition or separate category for AI adaptation

**SPECULATIVE FUTURES:**

**SCENARIO 1: AI as Adapter's Tool (Near-term, likely)**
- AI assists human adapters (idea generation, draft creation, tedious tasks)
- Humans retain creative control, AI augments capability
- **Hutcheon framework:** Mostly applicable—human remains primary agent, AI is tool like camera or word processor

**SCENARIO 2: Human-AI Collaboration (Mid-term, probable)**
- AI contributes substantively (not just tool)
- Co-authorship model: human + AI creative partnership
- **Hutcheon framework:** Needs extension—distributed agency theory, hybrid authorship

**SCENARIO 3: Autonomous AI Adaptation (Long-term, uncertain)**
- AI independently creates adaptations without human input
- Reads source, interprets, generates adaptation in new medium
- **Hutcheon framework:** Requires radical revision—post-humanist adaptation theory

**RESEARCH AGENDA:**

**EMPIRICAL STUDIES NEEDED:**
1. **Audience reception:** Compare known-human vs. known-AI vs. unknown-authorship adaptation response
2. **Turing test for adaptation:** Can audiences distinguish human from AI adaptations?
3. **Quality assessment:** Do AI adaptations achieve transcoding success?
4. **Process studies:** How do human-AI collaborations work in practice?

**THEORETICAL DEVELOPMENT:**
1. **Post-humanist adaptation theory:** AI as adapter
2. **Algorithmic creativity theory:** What constitutes computational creativity?
3. **Extended authorship:** Distributed agency across human/non-human actors
4. **Modal affordances:** Do AI adaptations create new modes or fit existing?

**CASE STUDY (Hypothetical but Plausible):**

**AI Adapts *Pride & Prejudice* to Film (2026)**

**Process:**
1. GPT-7 (hypothetical) reads Austen's novel
2. Analyzes century of P&P adaptations (pattern learning)
3. Generates screenplay in novel cinematic style
4. DALL-E-5 creates storyboards

#### **#GLOBAL-SOUTH-ADAPTATION-EPISTEMOLOGIES**

**CRITICAL RECOGNITION:**
Hutcheon's framework, despite postcolonial awareness, remains **epistemologically Western**—derived from Western texts, media, and theoretical traditions.

**THE CHALLENGE:**
**Non-Western cultures have different:**
- Storytelling traditions (oral, cyclical, communal vs. written, linear, individual)
- Conceptions of authorship (collective vs. individual)
- Temporal ontologies (circular vs. progressive)
- Media ecologies (different technologies, industries, aesthetics)
- Intertextual practices (allusion, variation, reinvention traditions)

**Do these require different adaptation theories?**

**CASE STUDY 1: INDIAN CINEMA (BOLLYWOOD/REGIONAL)**

**DISTINCTIVE FEATURES:**

**1. REMAKE CULTURE:**
- Bollywood routinely remakes Hollywood films AND regional Indian films (Tamil, Telugu → Hindi)
- No stigma (unlike Western "unoriginality" anxiety)
- Often **unannounced** (no credit to Hollywood source)
- **Example:** *Ghajini* (2008) remakes *Memento* (2000) but Indianizes completely

**2. MASALA AESTHETICS:**
- Genre hybridity: Romance + action + comedy + musical in single film
- **Intermission culture:** Films designed for breaks (different pacing)
- **Song picturization:** Narrative interruption for musical spectacle (not diegetic)
- **Star system:** Actor persona > character (Amitabh Bachchan, Shah Rukh Khan)

**3. CULTURAL SPECIFICITY:**
- Family-centric narratives (vs. Western individualism)
- Religious/mythological references (Hindu epics permeate)
- Language mixing (Hindi + English + regional)
- Diaspora address (NRI—Non-Resident Indian—audiences significant)

**4. INTERTEXTUAL DENSITY:**
- Constant allusion to prior films (knowing audience assumed)
- **Tribute culture:** References as homage, not plagiarism
- **Example:** Every film quotes *Sholay* (1975)—cultural literacy required

**HUTCHEON APPLICATION:**
- **Modal framework applies:** Bollywood films are showing mode (perceptual immersion)
- **BUT:** Showing mode conventions differ (intermission, songs, masala)
- **Transcoding concept applies:** Novel → film (many literary adaptations)
- **BUT:** Fidelity never expected—Indianization assumed necessary
- **Reception theory applies:** Knowing audiences compare (to Hollywood source, to other Bollywood versions)
- **BUT:** Oscillation may operate differently (allusion networks, tribute culture)

**REQUIRES:**
- **Indian adaptation theory** developed by Indian scholars (Tejaswini Ganti, Rosie Thomas, etc.)
- Hutcheon as **starting point**, not final word
- Recognition of **Bollywood's own theoretical tradition** (not derivative of Hollywood)

---

**CASE STUDY 2: AFRICAN ORAL STORYTELLING TRADITIONS**

**DISTINCTIVE FEATURES:**

**1. ORAL CULTURE:**
- Stories transmitted orally, not written
- **Variability:** Each telling differs (no "original" text)
- **Performance:** Griot/storyteller adapts to audience, moment
- **Collective memory:** Community holds story, not individual author

**2. CYCLICAL TEMPORALITY:**
- Stories return, repeat, spiral (vs. Western linear narrative)
- **Mythic time:** Past and present collapse
- **Seasonal:** Stories tied to agricultural cycles, rituals

**3. COMMUNAL AUTHORSHIP:**
- No single author—story belongs to community
- **Improvisation:** Storyteller varies, audiences contribute
- **Ownership:** Not individual property but cultural inheritance

**4. FUNCTIONAL NARRATIVE:**
- Stories teach, heal, resolve conflict, entertain
- **Embedded in life:** Not separate "art" sphere
- **Proverbs, songs, riddles:** Integrated into storytelling

**HUTCHEON CHALLENGES:**

**1. What is "source text" in oral tradition?**
- No fixed, authoritative version
- Each telling is adaptation of... what? (prior tellings? Collective memory? Archetype?)
- **Hutcheon's "announced relationship to specific prior text"** doesn't fit

**2. What is "adaptation" when variability is norm?**
- Every telling varies—all adaptations?
- When does variation become distinct adaptation vs. iteration?
- **Western adaptation/original binary collapses**

**3. How does reception work without fixed source?**
- Knowing audiences can't compare to specific prior text (no fixed original)
- Oscillation between... memory of multiple prior tellings? Ideal type?
- **Palimpsestic reception model inadequate**

**4. How does modal framework apply?**
- Oral storytelling: Telling mode? Showing mode? (Performance elements)
- Interacting mode? (Audience participation, call-and-response)
- **Hybrid that predates modern media**

**REQUIRES:**
- **Orality-specific adaptation theory**—drawing on Walter Ong, Ruth Finnegan, oral literature scholars
- Recognition that **Western print-culture assumptions** don't universalize
