## WORKFLOW 1: CHARACTER DEVELOPMENT (Concept → Screen-Ready)

### OBJECTIVE
Transform a character concept into a fully dimensional, dramatically functional character ready for screen execution, integrating insights from Truby's moral architecture, McKee's dimensional complexity, Seger's seven-layer system, Johnstone's status dynamics, and genre-specific requirements.

### WHEN TO USE
- Beginning any new project (feature, TV, short)
- Character feels flat or generic in existing draft
- Ensemble cast lacks differentiation
- Character motivations unclear or unconvincing
- Protagonist is passive or reactive

### METHODOLOGY INTEGRATION

**Foundation Layer: Truby's Character Web + Moral Need**
- *Primary Source:* Anatomy of a Story (Truby), Seven Key Steps of Story Structure
- *Function:* Establishes character's moral/psychological need and relational position
- *Why This First:* Provides causal engine for entire character—without need, there's no arc
- *Stage 1 Reference:* [→ Truby Analysis: Character Web, Weakness & Need]

**Enhancement Layer: McKee's Dimension of Character + Seger's Seven Layers**
- *Sources:* Story (McKee), Character dimension; Advanced Screenwriting (Seger), Seven-layer system
- *Function:* Adds internal contradictions and comprehensive psychological texture
- *Why Second:* Foundation establishes what drives character; enhancement reveals HOW they're complex
- *Stage 1 References:* [→ McKee Analysis: Characterization vs. True Character] [→ Seger Analysis: Seven-Layer Character Model]

**Refinement Layer: Johnstone's Status + Genre-Specific Techniques**
- *Sources:* Impro (Johnstone), Status transactions; Genre-specific texts (Blake, Vorhaus, Kaplan)
- *Function:* Adds behavioral specificity and performable qualities
- *Why Third:* Makes character playable by actors, adds scene-level dynamics
- *Stage 1 References:* [→ Johnstone Analysis: Status Dynamics] [→ Comedy texts for comic character requirements]

### STEP-BY-STEP PROCESS

#### PHASE 1: CORE CONCEPTION (Foundation Layer - Days 1-3)

**Step 1.1: Identify Moral/Psychological NEED (Truby)**

*Action:* Complete the Need Questionnaire

**Questions:**
1. What psychological or moral deficiency prevents this character from being complete?
2. What fundamental misunderstanding about life causes them suffering?
3. If they could have one internal transformation, what would it be?
4. What do they need to learn about themselves or the world?
5. What wounds or fears block their growth?

**Common Need Categories:**
- **Psychological:** Self-acceptance, vulnerability, trust, courage, maturity
- **Moral:** Empathy, justice, responsibility, forgiveness, integrity
- **Social:** Connection, collaboration, authentic communication, healthy boundaries

*Output:* One-sentence Need Statement
- *Example:* "Sarah needs to learn that vulnerability creates genuine connection, not weakness"
- *Example:* "Marcus needs to accept that he can't control everything, only his response"

**Validation Checkpoint:** 
✓ Need is INTERNAL (not external circumstance)
✓ Need is unconscious to character initially (they don't know they need this)
✓ Need creates potential for transformation (clear "before" and "after" states)

**Step 1.2: Define DESIRE (External Goal - Truby)**

*Action:* Identify what character consciously WANTS (vs. unconsciously NEEDS)

**The Critical Distinction:**
- **NEED** = Internal, unconscious, what they truly require for fulfillment
- **DESIRE** = External, conscious, what they think will make them happy

**Best Character Structure:** Desire and Need are in OPPOSITION initially
- Character pursues wrong external goal believing it will fulfill them
- Journey reveals that achieving desire means addressing need first
- OR: Desire transforms to align with need through the arc

*Example Mappings:*
| Character | DESIRE (External) | NEED (Internal) | Conflict |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|----------|
| Michael Corleone | Avoid family business | Face moral responsibility | Avoidance vs. Acceptance |
| Annie Hall | Maintain relationships unchanged | Accept impermanence | Control vs. Release |
| Lester Burnham (Am. Beauty) | Recapture youth/freedom | Authentic self-acceptance | Regression vs. Integration |

*Output:* One-sentence Desire Statement
- *Example:* "Sarah wants to become partner at her law firm without appearing weak"
- *Example:* "Marcus wants to prevent his daughter from making any mistakes"

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Desire is EXTERNAL (achievable through action)
✓ Desire is CONSCIOUS (character can articulate it)
✓ Desire creates pursuit (drives plot forward)
✓ Desire conflicts with Need (creates internal journey)

**Step 1.3: Establish WEAKNESS/FLAW (Truby + Comedy Requirements)**

*Action:* Identify the behavioral/psychological flaw preventing character from achieving need

**Truby's Flaw Categories:**
1. **Moral Flaw:** Ethical failing (selfishness, dishonesty, cowardice, cruelty)
2. **Psychological Flaw:** Internal dysfunction (fear, obsession, denial, rigidity)
3. **Social Flaw:** Relational dysfunction (isolation, manipulation, dependence, arrogance)

**For Comedy (Blake/Vorhaus Requirements):**
- **Exaggerate flaw** beyond realism for comic effect
- Flaw must generate **humor, not pathos**
- Make flaw **visible/behavioral** (not just internal angst)
- Ensure flaw remains **sympathetic** (audience roots for character despite flaw)

*Output:* Flaw Definition + Behavioral Manifestations

Template:
```
FLAW: [Character's weakness in one word]
MANIFESTATION: [3-5 specific observable behaviors showing this flaw]
SYMPATHY HOOK: [Why audience forgives/understands this flaw]
```

*Example (Drama):*
```
FLAW: Emotional avoidance
MANIFESTATION: 
- Makes jokes when conversation gets serious
- Changes subject when emotions arise
- Works 80-hour weeks to avoid family
- Intellectualizes feelings rather than experiencing them
SYMPATHY HOOK: Witnessed traumatic event as child, never processed grief
```

*Example (Comedy):*
```
FLAW: Control obsession  
MANIFESTATION:
- Alphabetizes refrigerator contents
- Creates spreadsheets for social events
- Corrects everyone's grammar mid-conversation
- Color-codes closet by season and garment type
- Has emergency backup plans for backup plans
SYMPATHY HOOK: Childhood chaos makes order feel like safety
```

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Flaw is OBSERVABLE (audience can see it in behavior)
✓ Flaw PREVENTS character from achieving need
✓ Flaw creates CONFLICT (with self, others, situation)
✓ Flaw is TRANSFORMABLE (character can grow beyond it)

**Step 1.4: Map Character Web Position (Truby - Ensemble Only)**

*Action:* If writing ensemble, position this character within moral network

**Character Function Types:**
1. **Hero/Protagonist:** Carries primary need and transformation
2. **Opponent:** Mirrors hero but pursues opposing moral value
3. **Ally:** Represents alternative path to same goal
4. **Fake-Ally Opponent:** Appears supportive, actually opposes
5. **Subplot Character:** Carries parallel or contrasting arc
6. **Mentor:** Embodies what hero could become (positive projection)
7. **Shadow/Warning:** Embodies what hero could become (negative projection)

*For Each Supporting Character, Define:*
- **Relationship to Protagonist's Need:** Support, oppose, mirror, contrast?
- **Unique Moral Value:** What ethical position does this character represent?
- **Method of Pursuit:** How does this character pursue goals (vs. how protagonist does)?
- **Revelation Influence:** How does this character's arc inform protagonist's growth?

*Output:* Character Web Matrix (if ensemble)

```
| Character | Function | Moral Value | Relationship to Protag Need | Key Opposition |
|-----------|----------|-------------|---------------------------|----------------|
| Sarah | Hero | Control/Order | N/A - IS the protagonist | Self vs. chaos |
| Marcus | Mentor | Acceptance/Flow | Models what Sarah needs | Represents growth target |
| Diana | Opponent | Spontaneity | Challenges Sarah's rigidity | Order vs. chaos |
```

**Validation Checkpoint (Ensemble):**
✓ Each character represents DISTINCT moral value
✓ Minimum 3 characters oppose protagonist's flaw
✓ Protagonist connects meaningfully to every major character
✓ No "dead nodes" (characters not influencing main arc)

#### PHASE 2: DIMENSIONAL DEPTH (Enhancement Layer - Days 4-7)

**Step 2.1: Add Internal Contradictions (McKee - Dimension of Character)**

*Action:* Ensure character contains OPPOSING qualities simultaneously

**McKee's Principle:** "True character is revealed through choices under pressure—especially when those choices reveal internal contradictions"

**The Dimensionality Test:**
A dimensional character can authentically be described using OPPOSING adjectives:
- Confident YET insecure
- Loving YET cruel
- Intelligent YET self-destructive
- Brave YET cowardly
- Generous YET selfish

**Process:**
1. List 5 primary character traits (from Steps 1.1-1.3)
2. For EACH trait, identify its OPPOSITE
3. Find specific situations where the OPPOSITE is also true
4. Ensure contradictions are EARNED (explained by psychology/backstory)

*Example Application (Walter White - Breaking Bad):*
```
TRAIT: Brilliant (chemistry genius)
OPPOSITE: Foolish (repeatedly makes terrible decisions)
SITUATIONS: 
- Brilliant in lab, foolish in crime execution
- Genius at chemistry, idiot at covering tracks
EARNED BY: Pride overwhelms intelligence when ego threatened

TRAIT: Loving father
OPPOSITE: Destructive to family
SITUATIONS:
- Claims everything is "for family," actions destroy family
- Wants to provide, actually traumatizes those he loves
EARNED BY: Justification masks true motive (ego/power)
```

*Output:* Contradiction Matrix

```
| Core Trait | Opposing Trait | Where Opposition Manifests | Psychological Root |
|------------|----------------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| [Quality] | [Opposite] | [Specific situations] | [Why both coexist] |
```

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Character has minimum 3 meaningful contradictions
✓ Contradictions feel EARNED (not arbitrary)
✓ Opposites emerge under PRESSURE/STRESS
✓ Contradictions create dramatic interest (not confusion)

**Step 2.2: Apply Seger's Seven-Layer System**

*Action:* Build comprehensive character architecture across seven dimensions

**Layer 1: WHO (Core Identity)**
- Demographics, role, title, profession
- First impression, surface identity
- *Example:* "Sarah Chen, 38-year-old corporate attorney"

**Layer 2: WHAT (Observable Behavior)**  
- Actions, habits, routines, patterns
- What audience SEES character doing
- *Example:* "Arrives office 6 AM, reviews contracts until midnight, eats lunch at desk"

**Layer 3: GOALS (Conscious Desires)**
- What character actively pursues (overlap with Truby's DESIRE)
- External, articulable objectives
- *Example:* "Make partner before 40, win Thompson case, buy dream condo"

**Layer 4: NEEDS (Unconscious Requirements)**
- What character truly requires (overlap with Truby's NEED)
- Internal, unarticulated fulfillments
- *Example:* "Acceptance that self-worth isn't performance-based"

**Layer 5: HOW (Methods & Style)**
- Character's approach to problems
- Strategic vs. impulsive, direct vs. manipulative, aggressive vs. passive
- *Example:* "Hyper-prepared, controlled, intellectualizes emotions, indirect with personal conflict"

**Layer 6: WHY (Motivation & Backstory)**
- Life experiences shaping character
- Formative events, traumas, joys
- *Example:* "Immigrant parents sacrificed everything; learned only achievement earns belonging"

**Layer 7: VALUES (Ethical Core)**
- Principles guiding decisions
- What character believes about life, people, meaning
- *Example:* "Worth must be earned through productivity; weakness is failure; asking for help is defeat"

*Output:* Seven-Layer Character Profile (complete all layers)

**Seger's Integration Principle:** Layers should COHERE but also CREATE TENSION
- **Coherence Example:** Layer 7 (values) EXPLAINS Layer 5 (methods) - if character values control, they'll have controlling methods
- **Tension Example:** Layer 3 (goals) CONFLICTS with Layer 4 (needs) - creating character journey

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ All seven layers completed with specificity
✓ Layers create logical coherence (character feels unified)
✓ Layers also create tension (character has internal conflict)
✓ Backstory (Layer 6) serves character, doesn't overwhelm story

**Step 2.3: Design Character Arc (Transformation Pathway)**

*Action:* Map specific emotional/moral journey from weakness to growth

**Arc Structure (Truby's Seven Key Steps applied to character psychology):**

| Step | Character State | Dramatic Function | Output |
|------|----------------|-------------------|---------|
| 1. Weakness/Need | Flawed, unconscious of need | Establish "before" state | Opening characterization |
| 2. Desire | Pursues wrong external goal | Forward momentum | Plot engine activated |
| 3. Opponent | Encounters meaningful resistance | Tests current approach | Reveals flaw's limitations |
| 4. Plan | Doubles down on flawed strategy | Escalation | Flaw intensifies |
| 5. Battle/Crisis | Forced confrontation with self | Breaking point | Can't avoid truth anymore |
| 6. Self-Revelation | Recognizes need, sees truth | Turning point | Character KNOWS what must change |
| 7. New Equilibrium | Transformed behavior | "After" state | Demonstrates growth |

*Process:*
1. Define STARTING STATE (Step 1 - character with flaw intact)
2. Define ENDING STATE (Step 7 - character transformed)
3. Identify REVELATION MOMENT (Step 6 - precise scene where character SEES truth)
4. Map INTERMEDIATE STEPS (2-5) showing resistance, doubling down, crisis
5. Ensure transformation is EARNED (sufficient pressure/suffering to justify change)

*Example Arc (Romantic Comedy):*
```
1. WEAKNESS: Believes independence means strength, relationships mean weakness
2. DESIRE: Avoid commitment, maintain total control of life
3. OPPONENT: Falls for someone who requires vulnerability
4. PLAN: Try to maintain relationship without real intimacy
5. CRISIS: Partner leaves; realizes isolation is loneliness, not strength
6. REVELATION: "I'm not strong because I need no one—I'm scared"
7. NEW EQUILIBRIUM: Allows genuine intimacy, asks for support, admits feelings
```

*Output:* Character Arc Map (7-step progression showing internal state at each turning point)

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Clear contrast between Step 1 and Step 7 (meaningful change)
✓ Step 6 (Revelation) is SPECIFIC SCENE (not vague "somewhere in Act 3")
✓ Steps 2-5 show RESISTANCE to change (not easy acceptance)
✓ Transformation feels EARNED by suffering/pressure character experiences

#### PHASE 3: PERFORMABLE SPECIFICITY (Refinement Layer - Days 8-10)

**Step 3.1: Add Status Dynamics (Johnstone)**

*Action:* Define character's status behaviors and patterns

**Johnstone's Status Concept:** Every human interaction involves status transaction—playing high or low status through micro-behaviors (posture, eye contact, vocal patterns, spatial dynamics)

**For Character Development:**
1. **Define Default Status:** Does character habitually play high or low status?
2. **Identify Status Flexibility:** Can they shift status strategically?
3. **Find Status Contradictions:** Where does assumed status differ from played status?
4. **Map Status Dynamics with Other Characters:** With whom do they go high/low?

*Status Behavioral Indicators:*

**HIGH STATUS:**
- Sustained eye contact, looks away last
- Relaxed posture, takes space
- Slow, deliberate movements
- Interrupts others, finishes their sentences
- Asks questions, controls conversation direction
- Lower vocal pitch, measured pace

**LOW STATUS:**
- Breaks eye contact first, averts gaze
- Contracted posture, minimizes space
- Quick, nervous movements
- Allows interruption, trails off mid-sentence
- Answers questions, follows conversation
- Higher vocal pitch, rushed pace

*Character Status Profile Template:*
```
DEFAULT STATUS: [High/Medium/Low - character's habitual mode]
STATUS WITH DIFFERENT CHARACTERS:
- With boss: [High/Medium/Low + specific behaviors]
- With subordinates: [High/Medium/Low + specific behaviors]  
- With romantic interest: [High/Medium/Low + specific behaviors]
- With family: [High/Medium/Low + specific behaviors]

STATUS FLEXIBILITY: [Can they shift strategically? Or rigid?]
STATUS/POWER CONTRADICTION: [Do they PLAY status different from actual power?]
```

*Example:*
```
CHARACTER: Sarah Chen (corporate attorney)
DEFAULT STATUS: High (confident, controlled)
WITH DIFFERENT CHARACTERS:
- With senior partners: Medium-high (confident but deferential)
- With opposing counsel: High (aggressive, dominant)
- With sister: Low (defensive, contracted)
- With romantic interest: Oscillating (wants high, fears vulnerability means low)

STATUS FLEXIBILITY: Limited - struggles to consciously lower status (views it as weakness)
CONTRADICTION: Plays high status professionally, feels low status personally (imposter syndrome)
```

**Why This Matters for Writing:**
- Status gives actors **playable actions** ("play high status," "shift to low")
- Status creates **scene dynamics** (status transactions are conflict)
- Status reveals **character psychology** (confident person playing low status = interesting contradiction)

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Character has clear default status mode
✓ Status shifts logically based on relationships and pressure
✓ Status behaviors are SPECIFIC (not vague "confident")
✓ Status contradictions add dimension

**Step 3.2: Genre-Specific Requirements**

*Action:* Apply additional character requirements based on genre

**FOR COMEDY CHARACTERS (Blake/Vorhaus):**

**Requirement 1: Comic Perspective/Attitude (Vorhaus)**
- Character must have UNIQUE WAY of seeing world that generates humor
- *Example:* Optimist in dark world, pessimist in cheerful world, literalist in metaphorical world
- *Output:* One-sentence attitude statement: "Sarah sees all problems as solvable through proper organization"

**Requirement 2: Comic Vulnerability**
- Character weakness must be VISIBLE and SYMPATHETIC
- Audience laughs WITH character (not cruelly AT them)
- *Output:* Specific moment showing vulnerable humanity beneath comic flaw

**Requirement 3: Pattern/Escalation Potential**
- Flaw must enable RECURRING GAG or GAME
- Comedic situation can ESCALATE through character's repeated behavior
- *Example:* Control freak faces increasingly chaotic situations, responds with increasingly absurd control attempts

**Requirement 4: Transformation Visible in Behavior**
- Growth shown through ACTION (not explanation)
- Final act demonstrates NEW BEHAVIOR contrasting with opening
- *Output:* Two contrasting scenes - character in Act 1 vs. Act 3 handling same type of situation differently

**FOR DRAMATIC CHARACTERS (McKee/Truby):**

**Requirement 1: Moral Complexity**
- Avoid pure heroes/villains
- Even antagonist has JUSTIFICATION for actions (from their perspective)
- *Output:* Antagonist's self-justification statement (how they view themselves as right)

**Requirement 2: Active Decision-Making**
- Character makes DIFFICULT CHOICES under pressure
- Choices REVEAL true character (not circumstances forcing outcomes)
- *Output:* Minimum 3 major decisions where character could go either way

**Requirement 3: Internal Logic**
- Every action must make sense given character's psychology
- No "plot-convenient" behavior (character acts out of character to serve plot)
- *Output:* Behavior motivation map (why character does each major action)

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Genre requirements fully met
✓ Character has performable specificity (actors can play this)
✓ All layers cohere into unified character portrait
✓ Character feels UNIQUE (not generic or clichéd)

### QUALITY CHECKPOINTS

**After Foundation Layer (Step 1.4):**
□ Character has clearly defined Need (internal, unconscious)
□ Character has clearly defined Desire (external, conscious)
□ Need and Desire are in productive tension
□ Character has observable, playable Flaw
□ If ensemble: character web has no dead nodes

**After Enhancement Layer (Step 2.3):**
□ Character has minimum 3 meaningful internal contradictions
□ All seven layers completed with specificity
□ Character arc maps clear transformation journey
□ Revelation moment is specific and earned
□ Character feels DIMENSIONAL (not flat)

**After Refinement Layer (Step 3.2):**
□ Status dynamics defined and playable
□ Genre requirements fully satisfied
□ Character behaviors are SPECIFIC (not vague)
□ Character is UNIQUE (distinctive voice/perspective)
□ Everything coheres into unified portrait

**Final Integration Checkpoint:**
□ You can clearly articulate: Who is this character? What do they want? What do they need? What's preventing them from getting it? How do they change?
□ Character would make different choices than any other character (distinctiveness test)
□ Character can carry audience interest for 2 hours / 10 episodes (engagement test)
□ You know how character would respond to any situation (completeness test)

### COMMON PITFALLS

**Pitfall 1: Need Too Vague**
*Symptom:* "Character needs to learn to love" or "Character needs to find themselves"
*Fix:* Get SPECIFIC. What precise psychological/moral shift must occur? "Character needs to release control and trust others' competence"

**Pitfall 2: Protagonist Passive**
*Symptom:* Things happen TO character; character reacts but doesn't drive action
*Fix:* Ensure Desire is ACTIVE pursuit. Character makes choices that move plot forward.

**Pitfall 3: Contradiction = Inconsistency**
*Symptom:* Character behaves randomly or "whatever plot needs"
*Fix:* Contradictions must be EARNED through psychology. Both opposing qualities coexist logically.

**Pitfall 4: Backstory Overwhelms Present**
*Symptom:* Excessive exposition about character's past
*Fix:* Use "iceberg principle"—know 90% of backstory, show 10%. Present behavior reveals past without explaining it.

**Pitfall 5: All-Good or All-Bad Character**
*Symptom:* Hero has no flaws, or villain has no humanity
*Fix:* Every character believes they're right. Hero has weakness; antagonist has justification.

**Pitfall 6: Character Serves Plot**
*Symptom:* Character acts "out of character" because plot requires it
*Fix:* Reverse engineer. Plot should emerge from character choices, not force character into plot needs.

### CASE STUDY: Breaking Bad's Walter White

**Demonstrating Complete Character Development Workflow**

**PHASE 1: FOUNDATION**
- **Need:** Face mortality with dignity, accept help, prioritize family connection over ego
- **Desire:** Provide for family financially, prove his brilliance was wasted
- **Weakness/Flaw:** Pride, ego, need for recognition masks as selfless sacrifice
- **Character Web Position:** Protagonist in moral decline narrative; Jesse = moral opposite (wants redemption)

**PHASE 2: DIMENSIONALITY**
- **Contradictions:** Loving father/family destroyer, brilliant/self-destructive, victim/predator, selfless/egomaniacal
- **Seven Layers:**
  - WHO: High school chemistry teacher, cancer patient
  - WHAT: Cooks meth, lies to family, builds drug empire
  - GOALS: Provide for family, build empire, get recognition
  - NEEDS: Acknowledge mortality, release ego, genuine connection
  - HOW: Methodical, calculated, increasingly ruthless
  - WHY: Brilliant mind wasted in mediocre life, emasculated by circumstances
  - VALUES: Initially family > self, transforms to recognition > everything
- **Arc:** Humble teacher → ruthless kingpin (moral descent rather than ascent)

**PHASE 3: PERFORMABILITY**
- **Status:** Starts low (emasculated, overlooked), plays increasingly high status as Heisenberg, contradiction between identities
- **Genre Needs (Drama):** Moral complexity (we understand why he does this), active choices (every major turn driven by his decision), consistent internal logic (each choice flows from previous)

**Result:** One of television's most dimensional, compelling, complex characters

## WORKFLOW 2: STORY STRUCTURE (Premise → Outline)

### OBJECTIVE
Transform a premise or story idea into a comprehensive structural outline that ensures dramatic causality, escalating conflict, and satisfying resolution—integrating Field's three-act paradigm, Truby's 22-step organic structure, Snyder's beat sheet precision, and McKee's value progression principles.

### WHEN TO USE
- Beginning any new script (before writing scenes)
- Existing script has "saggy middle" or unclear structure
- Unsure how premise translates to plot events
- Story feels episodic (A then B then C) rather than causal
- Uncertain where act breaks should fall

### METHODOLOGY INTEGRATION

**Foundation Layer: Field's Three-Act Paradigm**
- *Primary Source:* Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting (Syd Field)
- *Function:* Establishes macro-structural framework and pacing checkpoints
- *Why First:* Provides clear beginning/middle/end architecture with page-specific guideposts
- *Stage 1 Reference:* [→ Field Analysis: Three-Act Structure, Plot Points, Paradigm]

**Enhancement Layer: Truby's 22 Building Blocks OR Snyder's 15 Beats**
- *Sources:* Anatomy of a Story (Truby) OR Save the Cat! (Snyder)
- *Function:* Adds granular story progression and ensures complete dramatic development
- *Why Second:* Field gives acts, Truby/Snyder give beats within acts
- *Choice Criteria:* Truby for character-driven/literary, Snyder for high-concept/commercial
- *Stage 1 References:* [→ Truby Analysis: 22 Building Blocks] [→ Snyder Analysis: 15-Beat Structure]

**Refinement Layer: McKee's Value Progression + Scene Design**
- *Source:* Story: Substance, Structure, Style (Robert McKee)
- *Function:* Ensures each scene advances story through value shifts
- *Why Third:* Macro structure (Field) + beat progression (Truby/Snyder) + scene logic (McKee) = complete architecture
- *Stage 1 Reference:* [→ McKee Analysis: Value Shift in Scenes, Progressive Complications]

### STEP-BY-STEP PROCESS

#### PHASE 1: PREMISE VALIDATION & CLARIFICATION (Foundation - Days 1-2)

**Step 1.1: Compress Premise to Single Sentence**

*Action:* Reduce story idea to one compelling sentence capturing core dramatic situation

**Premise Formula (Field/Snyder):**
```
[PROTAGONIST] must [GOAL] 
before/or else [STAKES] 
despite [OBSTACLE/OPPOSITION]
```

*Examples:*
- "A disabled forensic investigator must catch a serial killer before he strikes again despite being physically limited and psychologically unstable" (The Bone Collector)
- "A single mother must protect her son from supernatural threats despite having no resources and everyone thinking she's crazy" (The Babadook)  
- "A social outcast must win the school science fair before his scholarship is revoked despite his brilliant but arrogant competitor sabotaging him" (original premise)

*Output:* Single-sentence premise (under 30 words)

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Premise includes protagonist, goal, stakes, and obstacle
✓ Premise is SPECIFIC (not "a man seeks redemption" but specific man, specific redemption, specific obstacles)
✓ Premise suggests CONFLICT (inherent opposition built in)
✓ Premise is DRAMATIC (suggests meaningful stakes/consequences)

**Step 1.2: Identify Core Dramatic Question**

*Action:* Convert premise into YES/NO question story must answer

**McKee's Principle:** Every story is driven by a question in audience's mind: "Will the protagonist achieve their goal?"

*Process:*
1. Take protagonist's GOAL from premise
2. Frame as question: "Will [protagonist] [achieve goal]?"
3. Ensure question has BINARY answer (yes/no, not "maybe" or "sort of")

*Examples:*
- The Fugitive: "Will Dr. Richard Kimble prove his innocence and catch the real killer?"
- Tootsie: "Will Michael Dorsey get the acting job while pretending to be a woman, and will he be exposed?"
- Whiplash: "Will Andrew become a great jazz drummer despite Fletcher's abuse?"

*Output:* Core Dramatic Question (one sentence)

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Question has clear YES/NO answer
✓ Question can sustain audience interest for 2 hours
✓ Question relates to protagonist's EXTERNAL goal (not just internal journey)
✓ Answering question = resolving story

**Step 1.3: Map Three-Act Structure (Field)**

*Action:* Apply Field's paradigm to establish macro architecture

**The Field Paradigm (Feature Film - 110 pages):**

**ACT 1: SETUP (Pages 1-25)**
- *Function:* Establish world, protagonist, ordinary life, dramatic context
- *Duration:* Approximately 25 pages (22-27 acceptable range)
- *Ends with:* PLOT POINT 1 (major event spinning story in new direction)

**ACT 2: CONFRONTATION (Pages 25-85)**  
- *Function:* Escalating complications, protagonist pursues goal, faces obstacles
- *Duration:* Approximately 60 pages (55-65 acceptable range)
- *Contains:* MIDPOINT (around page 55) - major revelation or reversal
- *Ends with:* PLOT POINT 2 (lowest point, all seems lost)

**ACT 3: RESOLUTION (Pages 85-110)**
- *Function:* Final confrontation, climax, resolution of dramatic question
- *Duration:* Approximately 25 pages (20-30 acceptable range)  
- *Contains:* CLIMAX (protagonist answers dramatic question) and DENOUEMENT (tie up loose ends)

**For Television (Hour Drama - 60 pages):**
- Act 1: Pages 1-15 (ends with first commercial break)
- Act 2: Pages 15-30 (ends with second commercial break)
- Act 3: Pages 30-45 (ends with third commercial break)
- Act 4: Pages 45-60 (climax and resolution)

*Process:*
1. Determine target length (feature = 110, TV hour = 60, TV half-hour = 30)
2. Calculate act break pages using Field's ratios
3. Identify what PLOT POINT events would effectively end Acts 1 and 2
4. Sketch preliminary act contents

*Output:* Three-Act Framework Template
```
ACT 1 (Pages 1-[X])
- Ordinary world: [brief description]
- Inciting incident: [event that disrupts normal]
- Rising conflict: [building toward plot point]
- PLOT POINT 1 (Page [X]): [major event propelling into Act 2]

ACT 2 (Pages [X]-[Y])  
- New situation: [protagonist in new circumstance]
- Escalating obstacles: [series of complications]
- MIDPOINT (Page [Z]): [major revelation/reversal]
- Downward spiral: [things get worse]

ACT 3 (Pages [Y]-[END])
- Final push: [protagonist's last attempt]
- CLIMAX (Page [W]): [dramatic question answered]

```

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Act 1 approximately 25% of total length

#### PHASE 2: BEAT-LEVEL DEVELOPMENT (Enhancement Layer - Days 3-7)

**DECISION POINT: Choose Truby's 22 Steps OR Snyder's 15 Beats**

**When to Choose TRUBY (Character-Driven, Literary, Dramatic):**
- Story emphasizes internal character transformation
- Moral/philosophical complexity central to narrative
- Literary or auteur ambitions
- Ensemble cast with interconnected character arcs
- Examples: prestige drama, character studies, literary adaptations

**When to Choose SNYDER (High-Concept, Commercial, Genre-Driven):**
- Clear high-concept premise
- Primarily external/plot-driven narrative
- Commercial or studio production
- Strong genre elements (thriller, comedy, action)
- Examples: studio comedies, concept-driven films, accessible genre work

*Note: Can apply BOTH sequentially for maximum rigor—Truby for character logic, then Snyder for market/pacing polish*

---

**OPTION A: Truby's 22 Building Blocks (Character-Driven Structure)**

**Truby's Principle:** Structure emerges organically from character's moral need and opposition network

**The 22 Steps (Grouped by Function):**

**FOUNDATION (Steps 1-3): Character & World**
1. **Weakness & Need:** Character's moral/psychological deficiency
2. **Desire:** External goal character consciously pursues
3. **Ghost:** Wound/event from past shaping present behavior

**DRIVE (Steps 4-10): Pursuit Begins**
4. **Inciting Incident:** Event disrupting equilibrium, compelling action
5. **Desire Statement:** Character articulates goal
6. **Ally:** Support character joining protagonist's pursuit
7. **Opponent:** Force actively working against protagonist
8. **Fake-Ally Opponent:** Character seeming ally, actually opposition
9. **First Revelation:** New information changing understanding
10. **Plan:** Strategy protagonist will attempt

**CONFLICT (Steps 11-16): Escalating Opposition**
11. **Opponent's Plan:** Antagonist's counter-strategy revealed
12. **Drive:** Protagonist executes plan
13. **Attack by Ally:** Ally challenges protagonist's approach
14. **Apparent Defeat:** Plan fails, seems all is lost
15. **Second Revelation:** New information forcing strategy shift
16. **Audience Revelation:** Audience learns something protagonist doesn't yet know

**CLIMAX (Steps 17-22): Resolution**
17. **Third Revelation:** Critical information revealed to protagonist
18. **Gate, Gauntlet, Visit to Death:** Physical/symbolic threshold crossing
19. **Battle:** Final confrontation
20. **Self-Revelation:** Protagonist recognizes their need/truth
21. **Moral Decision:** Choice demonstrating transformation
22. **New Equilibrium:** Transformed world after moral choice

**Application Process:**
1. For each of 22 steps, create 2-3 sentence description of how this manifests in your story
2. Identify EXACT PAGE/SCENE where each step occurs
3. Ensure CAUSALITY: each step flows logically from previous
4. Map to Field's Act Structure (Steps 1-7 in Act 1, 8-18 in Act 2, 19-22 in Act 3)

*Output:* 22-Step Story Progression Map

```
STEP 1 - WEAKNESS & NEED (Page X, Scene Y)
Description: [What weakness character has, what they need to learn]

STEP 2 - DESIRE (Page X, Scene Y)  
Description: [What character wants to achieve]

[Continue for all 22 steps...]
```

**Validation Checkpoint (Truby):**
✓ All 22 steps present and accounted for
✓ Each step CAUSES next step (no arbitrary jumps)
✓ Self-Revelation (Step 20) is SPECIFIC and EARNED
✓ Moral Decision (Step 21) demonstrates character growth from Step 1
✓ Character arc and plot arc fully integrated

**OPTION B: Snyder's 15 Beats (Commercial/Genre Structure)**

**Snyder's Principle:** Successful films follow predictable pattern audiences unconsciously expect

**The 15 Beats (with Page Guidelines for 110-page script):**

1. **Opening Image (Page 1):** Snapshot of protagonist's "before" state
2. **Theme Stated (Page 5):** Someone articulates story's moral lesson (protagonist doesn't grasp yet)
3. **Setup (Pages 1-10):** Establish world, protagonist's ordinary life, "six things that need fixing"
4. **Catalyst (Page 12):** Inciting incident—event disrupting normal life
5. **Debate (Pages 12-25):** Protagonist resists call to action, weighs costs
6. **Break into Two (Page 25):** Decision to pursue goal, enter "upside-down world"
7. **B Story (Page 30):** Introduce subplot (often romantic/mentor relationship teaching theme)
8. **Fun and Games (Pages 30-55):** "Promise of premise"—deliver concept's potential, usually positive
9. **Midpoint (Page 55):** False victory OR false defeat—stakes raised, time clock activated
10. **Bad Guys Close In (Pages 55-75):** Opposition intensifies, internal dissension if team
11. **All Is Lost (Page 75):** Lowest point, opposite of Midpoint, "whiff of death"
12. **Dark Night of Soul (Pages 75-85):** Protagonist wallows in defeat, contemplates giving up
13. **Break into Three (Page 85):** Protagonist has insight (often from B Story), knows what to do
14. **Finale (Pages 85-110):** Protagonist executes new plan, confronts opposition, achieves goal AND addresses internal need
15. **Final Image (Page 110):** Snapshot showing transformation from Opening Image

**Application Process:**
1. For each beat, determine SPECIFIC SCENE executing that function
2. Place beats at approximately correct page numbers (flexibility ±5 pages okay)
3. Ensure "Fun and Games" delivers on premise's promise
4. Confirm "All Is Lost" truly feels like lowest point
5. Verify Final Image CONTRASTS with Opening Image (shows change)

*Output:* 15-Beat Screenplay Outline

```
BEAT 1: OPENING IMAGE (Page 1)
Scene: [Description]
Image: [Visual snapshot of "before" state]

BEAT 2: THEME STATED (Page 5)
Scene: [Description]  
Theme: [What lesson/truth is stated that protagonist will learn]

[Continue for all 15 beats...]
```

**Validation Checkpoint (Snyder):**
✓ All 15 beats present at approximately correct pages
✓ "Fun and Games" actually delivers on premise promise
✓ Midpoint shifts direction (false victory → downward, or false defeat → upward)
✓ "All Is Lost" is genuinely lowest/darkest moment
✓ Final Image clearly shows transformation from Opening Image
✓ B Story teaches lesson protagonist needs for Act 3

#### PHASE 3: SCENE-LEVEL VALUE PROGRESSION (Refinement Layer - Days 8-10)

**Step 3.1: Apply McKee's Value Shift Principle**

*Action:* Ensure every scene turns on value charge

**McKee's Core Principle:** "A scene is a story event that turns on value. If the value charge stays the same, you don't have a scene—you have exposition or description."

**Value Charge Defined:**
- The emotional/philosophical state of affairs
- Examples: freedom/slavery, hope/despair, trust/suspicion, connection/isolation, success/failure

**The Scene Turning Requirement:**
Every scene must shift value charge from positive to negative OR negative to positive
- Begins: Character feels hopeful (+)
- Ends: Character feels devastated (-)

*OR*

- Begins: Character is isolated (-)
- Ends: Character finds connection (+)

**Process:**
1. Take each scene from Truby's 22 Steps or Snyder's 15 Beats
2. Identify VALUE at stake in this scene
3. Mark scene's OPENING VALUE CHARGE (+ or -)
4. Mark scene's CLOSING VALUE CHARGE (opposite of opening)
5. If charge doesn't flip → diagnose problem (usually scene is exposition, not dramatic)

*Example Scene Value Progression:*
```
SCENE: Detective interrogates suspect
OPENING VALUE: Detective has CONTROL (+), suspect is DEFENSIVE (-)
MIDPOINT: Power shifts as suspect reveals alibi
CLOSING VALUE: Detective has lost CONTROL (-), suspect is CONFIDENT (+)
VALUE SHIFT: + control to - control
```

*Output:* Scene-by-Scene Value Progression Chart

```
| Scene # | Scene Description | Opening Value | Closing Value | Value Shift |
|---------|------------------|---------------|---------------|-------------|
| 1 | [Description] | [+ or -] [value] | [+ or -] [value] | [+ to - or - to +] |
```

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Every scene has identifiable value at stake
✓ Every scene's value charge FLIPS (no static scenes)
✓ Value shifts create ESCALATION (complications progressive, not random)
✓ Overall value progression leads to climactic reversal

**Step 3.2: Map Progressive Complications (McKee)**

*Action:* Ensure complications escalate geometrically, not arithmetically

**McKee's Principle:** "Complications should intensify exponentially—each obstacle worse than the last"

**Progressive Complication Structure:**
```
Obstacle 1: Minor setback (solvable with standard effort)
Obstacle 2: Significant challenge (requires new strategy)
Obstacle 3: Major crisis (threatens goal achievement)
Obstacle 4: Seemingly insurmountable barrier (appears to make goal impossible)
```

**Anti-Pattern (Arithmetic Complication):**
```
Obstacle 1: Flat tire
Obstacle 2: Another flat tire  
Obstacle 3: Third flat tire
[Same type of problem, no escalation]
```

**Correct Pattern (Geometric Complication):**
```
Obstacle 1: Flat tire (fixable)
Obstacle 2: Entire car breaks down (need new transportation)
Obstacle 3: Pursued by antagonist (survival threat)
Obstacle 4: Betrayed by ally (isolation + survival threat)
[Each complication COMPOUNDS previous, raises stakes]
```

*Process:*
1. List all major obstacles/complications from your Truby/Snyder outline
2. Assign each a SEVERITY RATING (1-10)
3. Arrange chronologically and check progression
4. Severity should INCREASE (with occasional relief for pacing)
5. If severity decreases or plateaus → reorder or intensify complications

*Output:* Complication Escalation Map

```
ACT 1:
Complication 1 (Page X): [Description] - Severity: 3/10
Complication 2 (Page Y): [Description] - Severity: 5/10

ACT 2A (Pre-Midpoint):
Complication 3 (Page Z): [Description] - Severity: 6/10
Complication 4 (Page W): [Description] - Severity: 7/10

ACT 2B (Post-Midpoint):
Complication 5 (Page P): [Description] - Severity: 8/10
Complication 6 (Page Q): [Description] - Severity: 9/10

ACT 3:
Final Complication (Page R): [Description] - Severity: 10/10
```

**Validation Checkpoint:**
✓ Severity ratings INCREASE overall (allow occasional brief relief)
✓ No "arithmetic" complications (same type repeated)
✓ Each complication COMPOUNDS previous (not isolated)
✓ Final complication feels genuinely climactic

### COMMON PITFALLS

**Pitfall 1: Episodic Structure (A then B then C)**
*Symptom:* Events happen in sequence but don't CAUSE each other
*Fix:* Apply Truby's causality—each beat must CAUSE next beat. Ask "Why does this happen NOW because of what just happened?"

**Pitfall 2: Saggy Middle (Act 2 Problem)**
*Symptom:* Story meanders around pages 40-70, lacks drive
*Fix:* Ensure strong Midpoint that shifts direction + escalating complications with rising severity + clear approach to Plot Point 2

**Pitfall 3: Unearned Climax**
*Symptom:* Protagonist suddenly capable of winning without sufficient build-up
*Fix:* Map protagonist's growing capability through Act 2—skills/knowledge acquired that enable Act 3 success

**Pitfall 4: Static Scenes**
*Symptom:* Scenes that are just characters talking, nothing changing
*Fix:* Apply McKee value shift—every scene must reverse charge or it's not a scene (cut it or add turn)

**Pitfall 5: Arbitrary Page Counts**
*Symptom:* Following Field/Snyder page numbers slavishly even when story logic demands different pacing
*Fix:* Use page counts as GUIDELINES, not prison. Story logic trumps formula, but know when/why you're deviating
